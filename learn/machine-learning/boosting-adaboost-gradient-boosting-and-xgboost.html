1. Why should a second learner correct the first?

A single Decision Tree is easy to inspect, but its first partition may miss information that only becomes visible after the initial predictions. Boosting builds an additive prediction by training learners sequentially. Instead of independently resampling data as Bagging does, each new learner responds to an inadequacy of the current ensemble. This is a strategy for reducing a chosen Loss, not a promise of lower Test Error.

We will study three related but distinct constructions. AdaBoost changes the importance assigned to incorrectly classified Training observations; Gradient Boosting fits a new learner to the direction that reduces a differentiable Loss; and XGBoost uses a regularized, second-order approximation to select and weight tree additions. They share an additive structure, but their update rules are not interchangeable.

The guiding problem is an insurer deciding which claims warrant enhanced investigation and estimating the eventual severity of a claim. The numerical examples are deliberately synthetic: they teach calculations, not how much an insurer would actually save. A classification error and a severity prediction error have different units and operational consequences. We will use both tasks, explain why, and never silently switch their Targets.

2. Learning outcomes, prerequisites, and the complete roadmap

By the end, you should be able to calculate two AdaBoost rounds, including every sample weight; derive the negative-gradient rule and construct two regression boosting stages; derive an XGBoost leaf weight and Split Gain; compare Bagging with Boosting without conflating their goals; and audit Validation, Test, regularization and early stopping decisions.

You will need the mean of a small dataset, squared error, logarithms, exponentials, elementary differentiation and a Decision Stump (a tree with one split). A Weak Learner is a simple learner that can contribute useful predictive information; it is not required to be uniformly worse than a complex learner. A Stage or Round is one additional fitted learner, and the Learning Rate scales that learner's contribution.

The pedagogical sequence is intentional: first classification with six observations, then regression with the same number of observations, then second-order optimization, and finally a larger reproducible experiment. A few different Targets appear because AdaBoost classification and Gradient Boosting regression optimize different objectives; each small dataset is shown in full.

3. Two insurance questions—and the information available at prediction time

An insurance analyst can ask Claim Risk: is a case likely to require manual investigation? Here the Target is a binary label \(y\in{-1,+1}\), with \(+1\) meaning higher risk. Alternatively, Claim Severity: what will the settled claim cost, measured in thousands of dollars? The Target \(y\geq0\) is numerical. These are different predictions, and an accurate risk label is not a dollar amount.

For the small examples, \(x\) is a synthetic risk or exposure score ordered from 1 to 6. In a real project, the score must itself be constructible from information known when the claim is opened. Using final settlement cost, a post-investigation fraud finding, or future claim adjustments as an Input would be Data Leakage, no matter how accurate Validation appeared.

The simple examples are designed to make arithmetic transparent, not to imply that exposure alone determines claim risk or cost. In the later 180-case experiment, additional Features capture age of the claim, previous incidents, weather exposure and reporting channel. The future experiment is also synthetic, with its generation procedure disclosed in Python.

4. Bagging and Boosting: same ensemble idea, different training logic

In Bagging, independently constructed resampled datasets produce learners \(T_1,\ldots,T_M\), which are combined by averaging or voting. Their training can be parallel. Averaging helps most when individual errors are not too correlated. In Boosting, learner \(h_m\) depends on what the preceding model \(F_{m-1}\) predicts; its training is sequential.

Question Bagging / Random Forest Boosting
How is the next learner trained? A new Bootstrap sample and possibly random Feature subset Against weights, residuals or gradients determined by prior learners
What is the combination? Usually unweighted vote / average Weighted additive score or sequential correction
Main mechanism Stabilize high-variance learners Iteratively reduce an objective
Principal sensitivity Tree correlation, sample variation Number of stages, Learning Rate, depth, regularization
Does it guarantee better Test performance? No No

A crucial qualification: neither "Bagging reduces variance" nor "Boosting reduces bias" is a universal theorem for every implementation. Depending on the learner, Loss, noise and tuning, Boosting can also reduce variance or overfit. We will compare what actually happens on held-out data, rather than treating slogans as evaluation evidence.

5. The shared mathematical structure: build a model in stages

For a regression model, an additive ensemble has the form \(F_M(x)=F_0(x)+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\nu h_m(x)\), where \(F_0\) is the initial prediction, \(h_m\) is the new learner fitted at stage \(m\), and \(\nu\) (nu) is the Learning Rate. The summation is not a vote: each new function contributes a numerical correction to the existing prediction.

\[F_m(x)=F_{m-1}(x)+\nu h_m(x).\]

For AdaBoost classification, a closely related additive score is \(S_M(x)=\sum_m\alpha_m h_m(x)\), where stump predictions are \(-1\) or \(+1\), and \(\alpha_m\) measures their influence. The final label is \(\operatorname{sign}(S_M(x))\); a sign alone does not provide a calibrated probability.

Check your understanding. If a model predicts 5, a new tree outputs \(-2\), and \(\nu=0.2\), the updated prediction is \(5+0.2(-2)=4.6\), not 3. This small operation is the basic step repeated by Gradient Boosting.

Consider six hypothetical claim investigations. Higher labels \(+1\) indicate claims requiring enhanced investigation; \(-1\) indicates routine review. Start with equal weights, \(w_i^{(1)}=1/6\). A stump can ask whether \(x\leq t\) and assign opposite labels to the two sides.

To select a stump, compare the weighted errors of candidate thresholds and orientations. For a split at 2.5 with left \(-1\), right \(+1\), only E is wrong; therefore the weighted error is \(1/6\). The next-lowest error among the other simple threshold candidates is \(1/3\). Thus this is the best first stump among those candidates.

The model has not learned probabilities; it has learned a binary rule using the current weights. The apparently inconvenient error on E is exactly what allows us to demonstrate how the second learner reacts.

7. AdaBoost round 1: weighted error and learner influence

For binary AdaBoost with labels and predictions in \({-1,+1}\), define \(\varepsilon_m=\sum_iw_i^{(m)}\mathbf1[y_i\ne h_m(x_i)]\), with weights normalized to sum to one. The stump influence is

\[\alpha_m=\frac12\ln\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}\right).\]

Substitute the observed error:

\[\alpha_1=\frac12\ln\left(\frac{5/6}{1/6}\right)=\frac12\ln 5=0.804719.\]

Why a logarithm? The weight update is exponential, and this value minimizes the one-dimensional exponential-loss normalization for the selected stump. A learner with error just below 0.5 receives a small positive weight; one with error 0.5 receives zero weight. A learner with error zero requires special handling because the formula diverges; practical implementations stop or regularize rather than calculate \(\ln(1/0)\).

The initial score of this one-stump ensemble is \(S_1(x)=0.804719h_1(x)\). Its predicted signs match the stump's labels; only E is wrong. That alone is 5/6 Training Accuracy, but accuracy is not the quantity AdaBoost uses to update the sample weights.

8. AdaBoost round 1: every observation receives a new weight

AdaBoost changes the weights by

\[\widetilde w_i^{(2)}=w_i^{(1)}\exp[-\alpha_1 y_i h_1(x_i)],\qquad w_i^{(2)}=\frac{\widetilde w_i^{(2)}}{\sum_j\widetilde w_j^{(2)}}.\]

If the prediction is correct, \(y_ih_1(x_i)=+1\), and its unnormalized weight is multiplied by \(e^{-0.804719}=1/\sqrt5\). If wrong, that product is \(-1\), and the weight is multiplied by \(e^{+0.804719}=\sqrt5\). The ratio of wrong to correct multipliers is \(5\).

The normalizing constant is

\[Z_1=\frac56\frac1{\sqrt5}+\frac16\sqrt5=0.745356.\]

A correct claim obtains \([(1/6)(1/\sqrt5)]/Z_1=0.1\); claim E obtains \([(1/6)\sqrt5]/Z_1=0.5\). Thus the next round's six weights are 0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.5, 0.1. They sum to 1 and allocate half of the mass to the claim the first stump missed. This is the mechanism of AdaBoost's emphasis; no additional claims were created.

9. AdaBoost round 2: a different stump and its complete update

Using these new weights, test the threshold \(x\leq5.5\) with left prediction \(-1\), right prediction \(+1\). It misclassifies C and D, both with weight 0.1, while correctly classifying E with weight 0.5. Its weighted error is \(\varepsilon_2=0.1+0.1=0.2\). This is the smallest weighted error among the threshold stumps on these six scores.

\[\alpha_2=\frac12\ln\left(\frac{0.8}{0.2}\right)=\frac12\ln4=0.693147.\]

The second update multiplies correct weights by \(e^{-\alpha_2}=0.5\) and incorrect weights by \(e^{\alpha_2}=2\). Before normalizing, the six weights become \((0.05,0.05,0.20,0.20,0.25,0.05)\), whose sum is 0.8. Divide by 0.8 to obtain (0.0625, 0.0625, 0.25, 0.25, 0.3125, 0.0625).

10. Combining AdaBoost learners: the final score is not a majority vote

After two rounds, the score is \(S_2(x)=0.804719h_1(x)+0.693147h_2(x)\). For claim E, the first stump predicts \(+1\) while the second predicts \(-1\): \(S_2(E)=0.804719-0.693147=+0.111572\), so E remains incorrectly classified as \(+1\). A student might expect the model to correct E immediately; the calculation reveals why that expectation is unjustified.

For C, the signs reverse: \(S_2(C)=0.804719-0.693147=+0.111572\), now correctly predicting \(+1\). For A, both stumps predict \(-1\), so \(S_2(A)=-1.497866\). The magnitude \(|S_2(x)|\) is a margin-like confidence score, not a calibrated probability. Two rounds still yield 5 of 6 correct classifications.

This reveals three different objects: sample weights guide the next learner; learner weights \(\alpha_m\) control their influence in the final score; class labels are produced by the sign of that score. Mixing them up creates mistakes even when the logarithm is computed correctly.

11. Why AdaBoost's exponential objective matters—and its limits

AdaBoost can be understood as minimizing the exponential loss \(\sum_i\exp[-y_iS_M(x_i)]\). A claim with a large positive margin \(y_iS_M(x_i)\) contributes little; a confidently wrong prediction has a negative margin and contributes exponentially more. This makes the algorithm strongly responsive to repeated mistakes.

For E after two rounds, \(y_E=-1\) and \(S_2(E)=+0.111572\), so its loss contribution is \(e^{+0.111572}\approx1.118\). For A, the margin is \((-1)(-1.497866)=1.497866\) and its contribution is \(e^{-1.497866}\approx0.224\). Although both contribute to the training objective, the incorrect case contributes much more.

This responsiveness is helpful when hard examples contain signal, but problematic when the labels are mistaken, unusually noisy, or the training distribution is unrepresentative. AdaBoost with simple stumps is often interpretable stage by stage, but the final weighted ensemble may be less interpretable than any individual tree. Boosting's sequential updates do not establish causality.

13. Gradient Boosting starts with the best constant model

Define the squared-error loss for six observations as \(L(F)=\frac12\sum_i(y_i-F(x_i))^2\). Its factor \(1/2\) simplifies the derivative and does not change the minimizing model. For a constant prediction \(F(x)=c\), differentiate:

\[\frac{dL}{dc}=\sum_i(c-y_i)=6c-35.\]

Setting this derivative to zero gives \(c=35/6=5.833333\); the second derivative is 6, positive, so it is a minimum. The resulting Stage 0 prediction is \(F_0(x)=5.833333\) for every claim.

The six Residuals, defined as \(r_i=y_i-F_0(x_i)\), are approximately \((-3.833333,-2.833333,-0.833333,0.166667,2.166667,5.166667)\). Their sum is zero. The sum of squared errors is \(54.833333\) in ($1,000)², so the MSE is \(54.833333/6=9.138889\) ($1,000)². We have a fully specified initial model, not unexplained starting parameters.

15. Learning Rate: make the first update, then recalculate every error

Let \(\nu=0.5\). The first fitted residual stump is \(h_1(x)=-1.833333\) for \(x\leq4.5\), and \(+3.666667\) otherwise. Therefore

\[F_1(x)=F_0(x)+0.5h_1(x).\]

The updated predictions are 4.916667 for A–D and 7.666667 for E–F, both in thousands of dollars. The residuals become \((-2.916667,-1.916667,+0.083333,+1.083333,+0.333333,+3.333333)\). Squaring and summing them gives \(24.583333\), so the updated MSE is \(24.583333/6=4.097222\). We have reduced Training MSE from 9.138889 to 4.097222, without changing any observed claim amount.

If \(\nu=1\), the first stage would predict the leaf means 4 and 9.5 and yield SSE 14.5 immediately. The smaller rate only makes a partial step; it can require more rounds but often facilitates controlled learning. A smaller rate is not automatically better on Test. Choosing it requires Validation under an explicitly defined search budget.

At the second round, we do not reuse the original residuals. The new Training Targets for the residual stump are \(y_i-F_1(x_i)\): \((-2.916667,-1.916667,+0.083333,+1.083333,+0.333333,+3.333333)\).

Checking all five possible partitions of these residuals gives SSE values 14.3750, 7.0625, 9.5417, 14.5000 and 11.2500 at thresholds 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 4.5 and 5.5 respectively. 17. The general Gradient Boosting rule: why negative gradients?

Squared error is only one possible Loss. Let \(\ell(y,F)\) be a differentiable per-observation Loss evaluated at the current numerical prediction \(F\). The partial derivative with respect to \(F(x_i)\) tells us whether increasing that prediction would increase or decrease Loss. Moving in the negative-gradient direction locally reduces Loss for a sufficiently small step.

\[r_{im}=-\left.\frac{\partial\ell(y_i,F)}{\partial F}\right|_{F=F_{m-1}(x_i)}.\]

For \(\ell(y,F)=\tfrac12(y-F)^2\), differentiate with respect to \(F\): \(\partial\ell/\partial F=F-y\), so the negative gradient is \(y-F\), precisely the residual used above. This is why fitting residuals works for squared error; it is not a universal definition of Gradient Boosting.

The next learner approximates these negative gradients as a function of the available Features. Some implementations then optimize a separate leaf value or take a line-search step to account for the chosen Loss. Thus “fit a tree to residuals” is a special case; the general principle is functional gradient descent, where the object being updated is a predictive function, not merely a vector of coefficients.

18. Binary Log Loss: pseudo-residuals are not ordinary residuals

For classification, let \(F(x)\) be a real-valued log-odds score and \(p=\sigma(F)=1/(1+e^{-F})\). With \(y\in{0,1}\), Binary Log Loss is \(\ell=-[y\ln p+(1-y)\ln(1-p)]\). By the Chain Rule, \(\partial\ell/\partial p=(p-y)/[p(1-p)]\) and \(dp/dF=p(1-p)\), so

\[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial F}=p-y,\qquad r^{\mathrm{pseudo}}=y-p.\]

For a positive case \(y=1\) currently predicted \(p=0.30\), its negative gradient is \(1-0.30=0.70\): the score should increase. For a negative case \(y=0\) predicted \(p=0.80\), it is \(-0.80\): the score should decrease. These are probability-scale differences used to update a log-odds score, not dollar errors and not automatically the leaf outputs of every implementation.

Using \(F\) instead of the probability directly also avoids accidentally adding tree predictions to a probability and leaving the interval [0,1]. The inverse link Sigmoid converts the final score back to a probability. That probability still requires independent Calibration checks before operational interpretation.

19. XGBoost: from the exact objective to a second-order approximation

XGBoost is a particular optimized family of Gradient-Boosted Trees. Its contribution is not merely “more trees”: it uses a regularized objective, first and second derivatives, split scoring, sparsity-aware procedures and efficient implementation strategies. To understand its mathematics, consider adding a tree \(f_t(x)\) to the current scores \(\widehat y_i^{(t-1)}\).

\[\mathcal L^{(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^n\ell\!\left(y_i,\widehat y_i^{(t-1)}+f_t(x_i)\right)+\Omega(f_t).\]

A second-order Taylor approximation around the existing score replaces each Loss change with \(g_if_t(x_i)+\tfrac12h_if_t(x_i)^2\), where \(g_i=\partial\ell_i/\partial\widehat y_i\) and \(h_i=\partial^2\ell_i/\partial\widehat y_i^2\). Constants independent of the new tree can be removed when comparing candidate trees.

The common tree regularizer is \(\Omega(f)=\gamma T+\tfrac12\lambda\sum_{j=1}^T w_j^2\), with \(T\) leaves, leaf scores \(w_j\), per-leaf cost \(\gamma\), and L2 penalty \(\lambda\). These are structural and magnitude penalties, not an assurance of generalization. We will now solve one leaf and one split numerically.

20. Derive XGBoost leaf weights and Split Gain—then calculate them

Group observations falling in leaf \(j\): define \(G_j=\sum_{i\in I_j}g_i\), \(H_j=\sum_{i\in I_j}h_i\). Its approximate regularized contribution is \(G_jw_j+\frac12(H_j+\lambda)w_j^2+\gamma\). Differentiating with respect to \(w_j\) and setting to zero gives

\[G_j+(H_j+\lambda)w_j=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad w_j^*=-\frac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda}.\]

For our severity dataset at the constant baseline \(5.833333\), use \(\ell=\frac12(y-F)^2\), so \(g_i=F-y_i\), \(h_i=1\). At a proposed split \(x\leq4.5\), the left four gradients sum to \(G_L=7.333333\), with \(H_L=4\); the right two sum to \(G_R=-7.333333\), \(H_R=2\). Let \(\lambda=1\), \(\gamma=0.2\). Then:

\[w_L^*=-\frac{7.333333}{5}=-1.466667.\]
\[w_R^*=+\frac{7.333333}{3}=2.444444.\]

The parent has \(G_P=0\) and \(H_P=6\). The Split Gain after including the extra leaf penalty is

Define the intermediate quantities \(A_j=G_j^2/(H_j+\lambda)\). Then the same Split Gain formula becomes

\[\mathrm{Gain}=\tfrac12(A_L+A_R-A_P)-\gamma.\]

Here \(A_L\), \(A_R\) and \(A_P\) correspond to the left, right and parent nodes. We introduced their abbreviation to make the formula readable on a small screen; it is algebraically identical to the full expression.

Substitute:

\[A_L=10.755556,\quad A_R=17.925926,\quad A_P=0.\]
\[\mathrm{Gain}=\tfrac12(28.681482)-0.2.\]
\[=14.340741-0.2=14.140741.\]

A positive Gain favors this split relative to leaving that node unsplit under this local quadratic objective. It is not Validation improvement or a dollar saving. The calculation also reveals why the XGBoost leaf values differ from the unregularized Gradient Boosting residual means: \(\lambda=1\) shrinks them toward zero.

When the Loss is Log Loss, the Hessian is not 1

The preceding claim-severity example has \(h_i=1\) because it uses half squared error. For binary classification, the model instead stores a log-odds score \(F\) with probability \(p=\sigma(F)\). As derived in Section 18, \(g_i=p_i-y_i\). Differentiating once more with \(dp_i/dF_i=p_i(1-p_i)\) gives \(h_i=p_i(1-p_i)\). This is curvature, not a second kind of residual.

Take three additional synthetic observations in the same proposed leaf: \(y=(1,1,0)\), \(p=(0.20,0.30,0.60)\). We obtain \(g=(-0.80,-0.70,+0.60)\), so \(G=-0.90\), and \(h=(0.16,0.21,0.24)\), so \(H=0.61\). With \(\lambda=1\), the optimized leaf score is \(w^*=-(-0.90)/(0.61+1)=0.559006\) log-odds units. At Learning Rate 0.10, the score moves by only 0.055901. For the first case the probability moves from 0.20 to \(\sigma(\ln(0.20/0.80)+0.055901)=0.209094\), not to 0.255901. These calculations distinguish gradient, curvature, leaf score, stage update and final probability.

21. How Gamma, Lambda, Learning Rate and tree size differ

Four hyperparameters act at different points. Lambda (\(\lambda\)) shrinks leaf scores through \(H_j+\lambda\); increasing it from 1 to 3 would change the left score from \(-7.333333/5=-1.466667\) to \(-7.333333/7=-1.047619\). Gamma (\(\gamma\)) subtracts from Gain, discouraging splits whose improvement is too small. Learning Rate (\(\nu\)) shrinks the entire fitted tree's contribution after its values are estimated. Max Depth limits the complexity of each tree's successive decisions.

These are related but not interchangeable. If \(\gamma\) exceeds the unpenalized gain 14.340741 in our example, that split would not be selected on a strictly positive-gain rule, while changing \(\nu\) does not alter the formula used to rank candidate splits. XGBoost also supports row and column subsampling, which inject randomness but does not make it equivalent to a Random Forest: the trees still update sequentially.

A subtle point: the simple derivation assumes differentiable Loss with a useful Hessian and leafwise constant outputs. Actual XGBoost offers multiple objectives and implementation-specific details such as histogram splitting and missing-value directions; its behavior cannot be inferred from this single six-observation calculation alone.

22. Compare the three Boosting methods without collapsing their differences

Dimension AdaBoost (binary classification) Gradient Boosting XGBoost
Signal for the next learner Updated case weights emphasizing mistakes Negative gradients of chosen Loss First + second derivatives and regularized Gain
Standard output Weighted classification score Additive prediction/score Regularized tree ensemble prediction/score
Typical trees Often shallow stumps Shallow Regression Trees Regularized, optimized trees
Important controls Number of rounds, Learning Rate, base learner Rounds, Learning Rate, depth, Loss Rounds, rate, depth, lambda, gamma, subsampling
Main risk Sensitivity to persistent wrong labels Overfitting through excessive fitting of residual patterns Similar plus complex interactions among settings

All three learn from Training, but they need not prefer the same model or make comparable predictions. For example, interpreting AdaBoost's raw score as a claim probability is inappropriate without calibration. Similarly, for Gradient Boosting using Log Loss, a positive tree update to a log-odds score is not itself an increase of that magnitude in probability.

Bridge to Guide 20: Bagging trains trees on separately resampled data, then aggregates. Boosting creates a path through model space; the final model depends on the order and size of earlier corrections. That difference motivates Learning Rate, early stopping and careful Validation.

23. From hand calculations to a fair 180-claim development experiment

For a more realistic workflow, we generate 180 synthetic insurance claims. The four Features are claim age (days), prior incident count, weather exposure category and reporting channel. The simulated Target is settled claim cost in thousands of dollars. The generation equation includes additive effects, one interaction, and random noise. This is disclosed in the code; no confidential claims are used.

A fixed random partition produces 108 Training, 36 Validation and 36 Test cases. We fit a mean Baseline, a Random Forest comparator and four Gradient Boosting candidates using Training only. The ranking criterion is Validation MAE, in thousands of dollars. The chosen model is then evaluated once on Test; Test results must not be used to revise the candidate list after the fact.

24. Reproducible Python: the dataset, fitting, selection and frozen Test

The following example is self-contained apart from NumPy and scikit-learn. Copy it into one Jupyter cell. All Feature values are available before the synthetic settlement amount is generated; the code does not use the Target as an Input. Setting the generator seed and model random states makes the exercise repeatable on the stated library implementations.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.dummy import DummyRegressor
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor, GradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error
rng=np.random.default_rng(2026)
n=180
age=rng.integers(1,16,n)
prior=rng.integers(0,5,n)
weather=rng.integers(0,3,n)
channel=rng.integers(0,2,n)
X=np.column_stack([age,prior,weather,channel])
y=np.maximum(0.5,1.8+0.27*age+0.9*prior+1.5*weather +0.12*age*weather +0.9*(channel==1)*(prior>=2)+rng.normal(0,1.4,n))
ids=np.arange(n)
tr,tmp=train_test_split(ids,test_size=.4,random_state=42)
va,te=train_test_split(tmp,test_size=.5,random_state=42)
models={'Mean baseline':DummyRegressor(strategy='mean'),'Random Forest':RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=120,max_depth=4,min_samples_leaf=3,random_state=42), 'GB 0.05, 40':GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=40,learning_rate=.05,max_depth=2,random_state=42),'GB 0.10, 40':GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=40,learning_rate=.1,max_depth=2,random_state=42),'GB 0.10, 100':GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=100,learning_rate=.1,max_depth=2,random_state=42),'GB 0.30, 100':GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=100,learning_rate=.3,max_depth=2,random_state=42)}
for name,m in models.items():
 m.fit(X[tr],y[tr]); pr=m.predict(X[va]); print(name,'valid MAE',round(mean_absolute_error(y[va],pr),6),'valid RMSE',round(np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y[va],pr)),6),'train MAE',round(mean_absolute_error(y[tr],m.predict(X[tr])),6))
win=min(models,key=lambda k:mean_absolute_error(y[va],models[k].predict(X[va])))

# Stage-wise development-only diagnostic for the 100-tree selected candidate.
# The already reported Test score belongs to the frozen 100-tree model, not Stage 93.
chosen=models[win]
training_mse=[mean_squared_error(y[tr],p) for p in chosen.staged_predict(X[tr])]
validation_mse=[mean_squared_error(y[va],p) for p in chosen.staged_predict(X[va])]
validation_mae=[mean_absolute_error(y[va],p) for p in chosen.staged_predict(X[va])]
best_stage=int(np.argmin(validation_mae)+1)
print('DEVELOPMENT CURVE: Validation MAE best stage',best_stage,'best',round(validation_mae[best_stage-1],6),'stage 100',round(validation_mae[-1],6))
print('DEVELOPMENT CURVE: Validation MSE stage 93',round(validation_mse[92],6),'stage 100',round(validation_mse[-1],6))
print('DEVELOPMENT CURVE: Training MSE stage 93',round(training_mse[92],6),'stage 100',round(training_mse[-1],6))
print('WIN',win,'TEST MAE',mean_absolute_error(y[te],models[win].predict(X[te])),'TEST RMSE',np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y[te],models[win].predict(X[te]))),'n',len(tr),len(va),len(te))

The code compares candidates by Validation MAE, not Training Loss. It also reports RMSE to expose sensitivity to larger errors. A true production workflow would additionally investigate grouping by customer, temporal validity, data drift, outliers, claim handling changes and uncertainty intervals; a single random split is sufficient for this controlled teaching example, not necessarily for insurance deployment.

25. Early stopping, overfitting and what a Loss curve really shows

Boosting reduces Training Loss repeatedly and may continue fitting increasingly narrow patterns. Validation performance, however, can stop improving and eventually deteriorate. Early Stopping means selecting a stage using development data—typically a Validation set or Cross-Validation—not peeking at Test after each additional tree.

A reproducible way to test early stopping with scikit-learn is to train a sufficiently large GradientBoostingRegressor on Training and evaluate staged_predict(X_validation) across successive stages. Select the stage with minimum Validation MAE, then refit the chosen configuration on the allowed development data if the evaluation protocol specifies that step. The final Test must remain outside every iteration of that choice.

Learning Rate and number of stages are coupled. A small \(\nu\) often needs more learners; a large \(\nu\) may reach a low Training Loss quickly but also overshoot or fit noise. Neither monotonic Training Loss nor an apparent early-stage improvement proves future generalization. A credible comparison documents split strategy, candidate search, scoring function and the single final holdout result.

26. Feature Importance, missing data, fairness and deployment limitations

Boosted-tree Feature Importance typically measures patterns of split usage or improvements within the fitted model. It is not causal evidence. A Feature may appear important because it stands in for correlated predictors or because its measurement contains future information. For higher-stakes insurance applications, compare with held-out Permutation Importance and explicitly examine subgroup performance, uncertainty and potential proxy discrimination.

Missing Values require an algorithm-appropriate strategy: classic sklearn GradientBoostingRegressor generally needs preprocessing or another estimator that supports missing data; XGBoost can learn missing-value branch directions. That convenience does not make missingness harmless—whether a value is absent may itself change across departments or time. The preprocessing and selection protocol must fit any learned transformation only on Training or the corresponding Cross-Validation fold.

In deployment, predicted severity is not a settlement decision by itself. Review the intended use, thresholds for human review, the cost of incorrect actions, monitoring plans, and behavior under shifted claim prevalence or policy rules. Prediction is not causal explanation; changing an important Feature need not change the realized outcome as the model suggests.

27. Understanding checks and common mistakes

The following misconceptions are especially costly because they can survive a technically correct implementation:

  1. “Boosting means Bootstrap.” No. Bagging is organized around resampled independent learners; Boosting is organized around sequential updates. Some boosting implementations do subsample rows, but the learners remain dependent on prior stages.
  2. “AdaBoost weights are class probabilities.” No. Observation weights allocate training attention, \(\alpha_m\) controls stump influence, and the final signed score is not automatically calibrated.
  3. “The next Gradient Boosting tree predicts the original Target.” Not in our derivation. It approximates the current negative gradient, which equals a residual only for the chosen squared-error form.
  4. “XGBoost's Hessian is optional decoration.” The second derivative changes optimal leaf weights and Split Gain; it is part of the second-order approximation, not a synonym for the Gradient.
  5. “Gamma, Lambda and Learning Rate are the same penalty.” They act on splitting, leaf magnitude and stage contribution, respectively; the effects must be interpreted separately.
  6. “Training Error falling means Test Error falling.” False. Generalization requires held-out evaluation and appropriate validation design.
  7. “The best score observed on Test should determine the winner.” That reuses Test for selection and invalidates its role as an independent evaluation.
  8. “Random split always works for insurance.” Repeated customers, temporal drift and policy changes can require Group-Based or Time-Based splits.
  9. “Feature Importance proves that increasing the Feature causes the Target to increase.” Predictive association is not a causal intervention effect.
  10. “A model with fewer errors always yields lower operating cost.” Costs depend on which errors occur, the intervention policy and available capacity.
  11. “All three algorithms return the same kind of score.” AdaBoost margins, regression predictions and classification log-odds occupy different scales.
  12. “A synthetic experiment validates performance for a real insurer.” It validates the teaching calculations and code, not real-world effectiveness.

Self-check: At the first AdaBoost update, why does E receive weight 0.5? Because its error forces the multiplier \(\sqrt5\), while five correct predictions receive \(1/\sqrt5\), and all six values are normalized. At the first regression update, why does the right leaf predict 7.6667 rather than 9.5? Because \(\nu=0.5\) moves only halfway from the initial constant 5.8333 toward the leaf mean 9.5.

28. Worked exercises with complete solutions

Try solving each exercise before opening its answer. Solutions include calculation and interpretation.

1. Why is the first AdaBoost stump optimal among the six-score threshold stumps?

Its rule predicts −1 for x≤2.5 and +1 otherwise; compare with labels (−1,−1,+1,+1,−1,+1). Only E is wrong, so the error with equal weights is 1/6. A neighboring threshold at 1.5 misclassifies B and E, giving 2/6; at 3.5 it misclassifies C and E, also 2/6. The selected split therefore gives a smaller error than those alternatives. The conclusion is conditional on the candidate rules, not on every imaginable classifier.

2. Calculate the first AdaBoost learner coefficient.

The weighted error is ε=1/6. Substitute into α=(1/2)ln[(1−ε)/ε]=(1/2)ln[(5/6)/(1/6)]=(1/2)ln 5=0.804719. It is positive because ε<0.5; the stump contributes more than a near-random stump would. This coefficient weights a learner, not a claim or a probability.

3. Reproduce the first weight update for the incorrectly classified claim E.

Initially w_E=1/6. Since y_E h_1(E)=−1, its unnormalized weight is (1/6)e^{0.804719}=(1/6)√5≈0.372678. The total unnormalized weight is Z≈0.745356. Divide: 0.372678/0.745356=0.5. The five correct claims each obtain 0.1, and the six values sum to 1.

4. Why is the second stump not trained with equal weights?

AdaBoost makes the second learner respond to the first learner’s error. Claim E now holds weight 0.5 and each other claim 0.1. The stump splitting at 5.5 correctly predicts E and misclassifies C and D, whose combined weight is only 0.2. Under equal weights its two errors would cost 2/6, but the relevant second-round objective is weighted error 0.2, not unweighted accuracy.

5. Compute the normalized weight of C after the second round.

Before the update C has weight 0.1. Stump 2 is wrong on C and α₂=(1/2)ln4=0.693147, so its unnormalized weight is 0.1e^{α₂}=0.1×2=0.2. The six unnormalized weights sum to 0.8; therefore w_C^(3)=0.2/0.8=0.25. The result agrees with the second-round table and shows that the focus has shifted partly toward C and D.

6. Reconstruct the two-stump final prediction for E and explain the surprise.

For E, h₁(E)=+1 and h₂(E)=−1. Thus S₂(E)=0.804719(+1)+0.693147(−1)=0.111572>0. The predicted class is +1, but the actual class is −1. Stump 2 corrected E individually; nevertheless the previous stump still has a larger coefficient. This is why adding one corrective learner does not guarantee immediate correction of every mistake.

7. Compare exponential-loss contributions from E and A after two rounds.

For E the signed margin is y_E S₂(E)=−0.111572, so loss is e^{0.111572}≈1.118. For A the margin is (−1)(−1.497866)=+1.497866 and loss is e^{−1.497866}≈0.224. The incorrect E contributes roughly five times as much loss. The exact ratio is e^{1.609438}=5, illustrating the steep penalty on mistakes.

8. Derive the best constant prediction for the six regression claims.

Set L(c)=(1/2)Σ(y_i−c)². Differentiate: dL/dc=Σ(c−y_i)=6c−35. Setting it to zero yields c=35/6=5.833333 thousand dollars. The second derivative is 6>0, confirming a minimum. A constant is a baseline: it predicts the same amount for every claim, regardless of exposure.

9. Verify that the initial residuals sum to zero.

Observed totals sum to 35. Six identical fitted values of 35/6 also sum to 35. Hence Σ(y_i−F₀)=35−35=0. Numerically the residual vector is about (−3.8333,−2.8333,−0.8333,0.1667,2.1667,5.1667), with tiny rounding differences. Zero residual sum is a property of a fitted mean under this loss; it does not prove low prediction error.

10. Compute the split SSE at 4.5 for the first regression stump.

On the left, the values are 2,3,5,6 with mean 4. Squared deviations are 4,1,1,4, summing to 10. On the right, values 8,11 have mean 9.5; squared deviations are 2.25 and 2.25, summing to 4.5. Combined SSE is 10+4.5=14.5 (thousand dollars)², the smallest among the five candidate splits.

11. Apply the first regression update to claim F using Learning Rate 0.5.

The initial prediction for F is 5.833333 thousand dollars. The right leaf’s residual mean is +3.666667. The scaled correction is 0.5×3.666667=1.833333; hence F₁(F)=5.833333+1.833333=7.666667 thousand dollars. Claim F actually costs 11, so its new residual is 11−7.666667=3.333333. This remaining error helps train stage 2.

12. Compute stage-2 stump leaf means and identify the chosen split.

After stage 1 the residuals are (−2.916667,−1.916667,0.083333,1.083333,0.333333,3.333333). The split at 2.5 yields left mean (−2.916667−1.916667)/2=−2.416667 and right mean (0.083333+1.083333+0.333333+3.333333)/4=1.208333. Its residual SSE is 7.0625, smaller than the SSE of the other four splits; thus it is selected.

13. Calculate the stage-2 model’s MSE and interpret units.

After the second half-step, predictions are (3.708333,3.708333,5.520833,5.520833,8.270833,8.270833). Subtract them from observed (2,3,5,6,8,11), square the six residuals and add: SSE=11.442708. Divide by 6 to get MSE=1.907118 in (thousand dollars)². This is smaller than the initial MSE=9.138889 but is still Training error, not a validated future-risk estimate.

14. Use the Chain Rule to find the binary-log-loss negative gradient.

With p=σ(F), dℓ/dp=(p−y)/[p(1−p)] and dp/dF=p(1−p). Multiplying cancels the common factor, leaving dℓ/dF=p−y. Hence the negative gradient is y−p. For y=1,p=0.3 it is +0.7; for y=0,p=0.8 it is −0.8. These scores drive a log-odds update rather than a dollar-valued residual.

15. Derive the XGBoost left-leaf weight with lambda = 1.

The left leaf contains four gradients summing to G_L=7.333333, with H_L=4. The approximate objective for its score w is Gw + (1/2)(H+λ)w² plus a constant. Differentiate: G+(H+λ)w=0. Thus w*=−G/(H+λ)=−7.333333/(4+1)=−1.466667. Lambda shrinks the magnitude relative to the unregularized leaf value −7.333333/4=−1.833333.

16. Calculate the XGBoost gain and explain what it is not.

Use G_L=7.333333,H_L=4,G_R=−7.333333,H_R=2,G_P=0,H_P=6,λ=1,γ=0.2. Gain=(1/2)[53.777778/5+53.777778/3−0]−0.2=14.140741. It is the improvement in a second-order regularized approximation from splitting one node. It is neither held-out MAE improvement nor a measured saving for an insurance business.

17. Audit the experiment: which model is selected, and what is the role of Test?

Among the pre-specified candidates, GB(learning_rate=0.30,n_estimators=100,max_depth=2) has the lowest Validation MAE, approximately 1.4206 thousand dollars. Freeze that decision before evaluating Test. The Test MAE is approximately 1.2728 thousand dollars, and this difference does not authorize changing the winner. If customers repeat or conditions change with time, revise the split design for a new experiment rather than reusing this Test for tuning.

18. Transfer: a claims department doubles its claim volume and changes its policy. What must be rechecked?

A lower historical Validation MAE does not guarantee deployment performance. Check whether Feature definitions, reporting time, settlement policy, loss distribution, class prevalence and missingness patterns have changed. Re-evaluate on a truly new time period, monitor error by claim type and subgroup, and verify operational capacity and human oversight. Feature Importance indicates learned association, not that intervening on a Feature causes the predicted saving.

19. Transfer: compute the XGBoost Gain at 3.5 and compare it with 4.5.

The same six squared-error gradients sum to zero. For the first three claims, \(G_L=(35/6-2)+(35/6-3)+(35/6-5)=15/2=7.5\) and \(H_L=3\). The remaining three have \(G_R=-7.5\) and \(H_R=3\). With \(\lambda=1\) and \(\gamma=0.2\), \(A_L=A_R=7.5^2/(3+1)=14.0625\), and \(A_P=0\). Therefore \(\mathrm{Gain}_{3.5}=\tfrac12(14.0625+14.0625)-0.2=13.8625\). The best candidate at 4.5 gives 14.140741, larger by \(14.140741-13.8625=0.278241\) (rounded). Interpretation: the 4.5 split offers the greatest improvement under this local objective; the gain is not held-out model accuracy.

20. Transfer: compute a Logistic-Loss XGBoost leaf using a nonconstant Hessian.

Take three new synthetic examples with \(y=(1,1,0)\), \(p=(0.20,0.30,0.60)\). Under Binary Log Loss, the score gradients are \(g=p-y=(-0.80,-0.70,+0.60)\), so \(G=-0.90\). The Hessians are \(h=p(1-p)=(0.16,0.21,0.24)\), so \(H=0.61\). For \(\lambda=1\), \(w^*=-G/(H+\lambda)=0.90/1.61=0.559006\) log-odds units. With \(\nu=0.10\), the score correction is \(+0.055901\), not a direct 0.055901 increase in probability. For the first example, new probability = \(\sigma(\ln(0.2/0.8)+0.055901)\approx0.209094\). This shows why curvature and the link function both matter.

21. Early Stopping transfer: why is Stage 93 preferable on Validation, and what remains frozen?

The published candidate has \(\nu=0.30\) and 100 trees. Its Stage 93 Validation MAE = 1.411774 thousand dollars versus 1.420614 at Stage 100; the improvement is 0.008840 thousand dollars, approximately $8.84 per claim in this synthetic Validation sample. Validation MSE likewise changes from 3.134783 at Stage 100 to 3.121289 at Stage 93 in (thousand dollars)². Training MSE keeps falling (1.174820 to 1.156364), demonstrating that a falling Training objective need not imply an improving Validation metric. The already reported 100-tree Test MAE of 1.272816 must not be presented as the Test result of a 93-tree model. Selecting 93 trees would create a distinct, development-selected candidate that must be frozen before its own single Test evaluation under a pre-specified protocol.

29. Synthesis, glossary and the future lab

Synthesis. AdaBoost changes observation weights; Gradient Boosting adds functions in the negative-gradient direction; XGBoost adds regularized trees using both gradient and curvature information. Their shared additive structure should not hide their different objectives. On the six-claim examples you can reconstruct every key update. On the larger example you can make a fair model choice without Test leakage, interpret error units and explain why favorable synthetic scores are not deployment guarantees.

Glossary. Weak Learner: a restricted model contributing to the ensemble. Stump: a one-split tree. Weighted Error: sum of current observation weights on mistakes. Alpha: AdaBoost learner contribution. Pseudo-Residual: negative Loss gradient at the current predictions. Learning Rate: factor scaling each new contribution. Hessian: second derivative of Loss with respect to prediction score. Split Gain: regularized improvement in the approximate tree objective. Early Stopping: select the number of stages using development data. Generalization: performance on new observations from the intended setting.

The final test remains the same: could a first-time student reproduce the arithmetic and explain why each stage changes the model?

Final check: you can reproduce all five Split Gains, explain the Log Loss Hessian and separate the development-selected Stage 93 from the 100-tree model actually evaluated on Test. A gain on synthetic data is not evidence of real insurer benefit.
