Loss Functions: Measuring and Learning from Prediction Errors

From the meaning of prediction error to the mathematics of learning: work through every calculation, choose a defensible objective and understand when different losses favor different models.

Machine Learning · Guide 8 of 33 · Theory, calculations and applications

1. Why does a learning algorithm need a Loss Function?

An insurance company wants to predict how many days a claim will take to resolve. A model can produce a number, but what tells the algorithm whether that number is good? If a claim actually takes 27 days and the model predicts 31, the prediction is wrong by four days. We still need to decide how strongly this mistake should influence learning—and whether a four-day error is comparable to a twelve-day error.

A Loss Function turns a prediction and its observed outcome into a numerical penalty. A small penalty usually means the prediction was close to its desired target under the chosen definition; a larger penalty means the prediction violated that definition more severely. During supervised learning, an optimizer tries to reduce a training objective constructed from such penalties. The model cannot learn an appropriate objective by merely observing that numbers differ: we define what kind of differences matter.

Distinguish four objects from the start. A prediction \(\widehat y_i\) is the model output for observation \(i\). A residual \(r_i=y_i-\widehat y_i\) retains the direction of the error. A per-observation loss \(\ell(y_i,\widehat y_i)\) maps that outcome and prediction to a penalty. A training objective \(J(\theta)\) combines losses across Training, possibly with Regularization, and is minimized with respect to model parameters \(\theta\). An evaluation metric is what we report to judge a model on appropriate held-out data; it need not be identical to the training objective.

A foundational point. A Loss Function is a modeling choice, not an objective law of nature. MSE, MAE and Cross-Entropy answer different mathematical questions. Their rankings can disagree without any arithmetic mistake. We must justify the choice in terms of target type, noise, optimization and intended use.

The data below are deliberately synthetic. They support exact calculations, not claims about an insurer's real customers. We will use them repeatedly, then introduce a separate Classification example when numerical prediction error is no longer the relevant question.

3. From a signed error to a nonnegative penalty

If we simply sum signed errors, positive and negative mistakes can cancel. Imagine two claims with actual resolution times of 10 and 20 days and predictions of 13 and 17 days. Their prediction-minus-actual errors are \(+3\) and \(-3\); the mean signed error is zero. Neither prediction is correct. Cancellation is useful for measuring systematic bias, but inappropriate as the only measure of total error magnitude.

Two common approaches prevent cancellation: take the absolute value or square the error. For a three-day error in either direction, \(|e|=3\) days and \(e^2=9\) days squared. For a six-day error, the absolute penalty doubles to 6, whereas the squared penalty quadruples relative to a three-day error, from 9 to 36.

Signed error \(e\) \(|e|\) \(e^2\)
−6 6 36
−3 3 9
0 0 0
+3 3 9
+6 6 36

The two functions are symmetric: they penalize underprediction and overprediction equally when magnitudes match. Both are minimized at a zero error, but their shapes differ: absolute loss has a sharp corner at zero, while squared loss is smooth and steepens rapidly away from zero. That difference affects both how we interpret exceptional cases and how a numerical optimizer updates parameters.

Units matter. Absolute error preserves the target's unit (days). Squared error has squared units (days²). The latter is not wrong: its purpose is to give larger errors disproportionately greater influence. RMSE later restores the unit by taking a square root.

4. Mean Absolute Error: calculate every term

Mean Absolute Error (MAE) averages absolute prediction mistakes. With \(n\) observations, the formula is:

\[\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_i-\widehat y_i|\]

Here \(n=6\), \(y_i\) is the observed number of days for claim \(i\), and \(\widehat y_i\) is that model's predicted number of days. The vertical bars mean absolute value, not an instruction to remove an observation. We take each difference's magnitude, add those magnitudes and divide by the number of evaluated claims.

For Model A, the absolute errors are \(1,1,1,1,1,12\):

\[\mathrm{MAE}_A=\frac{1+1+1+1+1+12}{6}=\frac{17}{6}=\boxed{2.8333\text{ days}}\]

For Model B, each absolute error is four days:

\[\mathrm{MAE}_B=\frac{4+4+4+4+4+4}{6}=\frac{24}{6}=\boxed{4.0000\text{ days}}\]

MAE prefers A. On these particular observations, its average absolute miss is about 2.83 days rather than four days. This does not mean that every A prediction is within 2.83 days; claim C6 misses by twelve. An average summarizes the dataset—it is not a guarantee for each person. We should inspect the distribution of errors in addition to the average.

MAE is often attractive when the application values a fairly direct measure of typical absolute deviation and we do not intend to make one extremely large error dominate every other observation. Yet “less sensitive to outliers” does not mean “outlier-proof”: the twelve-day error still contributes twelve units to the numerator. MAE can also be less convenient for gradient-based optimization because \(|e|\) has no ordinary derivative at \(e=0\); subgradient and specialized optimization methods address this.

5. Sum of Squared Errors and Mean Squared Error: the full arithmetic

Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) adds squared residuals. Mean Squared Error (MSE) divides that sum by the number of observations. Squaring keeps every contribution nonnegative while making large deviations particularly influential.

\[\mathrm{SSE}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2,\qquad \mathrm{MSE}=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n}\]

Do not skip the per-row squares. For Model A, each of the first five errors is one day, and the final error is twelve days:

\[\mathrm{SSE}_A=1^2+1^2+1^2+1^2+1^2+12^2=5+144=149\]
\[\mathrm{MSE}_A=\frac{149}{6}=\boxed{24.8333\text{ days}^2}\]

For Model B, there are six errors of four days:

\[\mathrm{SSE}_B=6\times4^2=6\times16=96,\qquad\mathrm{MSE}_B=\frac{96}{6}=\boxed{16\text{ days}^2}\]

MSE prefers B, reversing the MAE ranking. Why? Model A's one twelve-day error contributes \(144/149\approx96.64\%\) of A's entire SSE. It overwhelms five one-day errors. Model B's six mistakes each contribute the same amount, 16.

For a fixed evaluation dataset, minimizing SSE or MSE gives the same model ranking because MSE is SSE divided by the same positive constant \(n\). But the numerical values and gradients differ by that factor, and comparing SSE across datasets of different sizes is misleading unless we account for the number of observations. We will revisit the role of normalization when we study optimization.

6. Root Mean Squared Error and why the square root matters

MSE measures squared days; that unit is less intuitive for a claims manager. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) takes the square root of MSE, returning the scale of the target:

\[\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}=\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}\]

Using the exact MSE values rather than rounding too early:

\[\mathrm{RMSE}_A=\sqrt{149/6}=\boxed{4.9833\text{ days}}\]
\[\mathrm{RMSE}_B=\sqrt{96/6}=\sqrt{16}=\boxed{4\text{ days}}\]

RMSE and MSE always rank models identically on the same dataset, because the square-root function increases with its input. RMSE is not the average absolute deviation: Model A has MAE \(2.8333\) days but RMSE \(4.9833\) days. The separation reflects how heavily squared error weights its exceptional miss.

For any fixed set of errors, \(\mathrm{RMSE}\ge\mathrm{MAE}\). This follows from the relationship between the quadratic and arithmetic means of nonnegative numbers. Equality occurs when all absolute errors are equal, as for our Model B: its MAE and RMSE are both four days.

Metric Model A Model B Preferred on these six claims
MAE (days) 2.8333 4.0000 A
SSE (days²) 149 96 B
MSE (days²) 24.8333 16.0000 B
RMSE (days) 4.9833 4.0000 B

Notice what none of these numbers establishes: which model will work better next month, whether the business ought to promise a settlement date, or whether one model is fair across claim categories. Those questions require honest validation and a relevant decision framework—not merely a smaller in-sample loss.

7. Loss geometry: why one large error can change the decision

The change in model preference is not a numerical curiosity; it follows from the shape of the loss function. For an error magnitude \(a\ge0\), absolute loss equals \(a\), while squared loss equals \(a^2\). As \(a\) grows, the square rises faster than the straight line.

Consider raising a mistake from three days to six days. Its absolute penalty grows from 3 to 6, a factor of two. The squared penalty grows from 9 to 36, a factor of four. Increasing a mistake from 3 to 12 days multiplies absolute loss by four but squared loss by sixteen. This produces a strong incentive under MSE to avoid rare, very large mistakes—even at the expense of several moderate mistakes.

This graph shows per-observation losses, not the error distribution of our claims dataset. The independent horizontal variable is the magnitude of an error in days; the vertical axis is the penalty. The two vertical scales differ, so their precise numerical heights should not be interpreted as interchangeable units. What matters is their growth pattern.

When large forecast mistakes cause severe harm, this behavior can be useful. But squared error also gives exceptional measurements considerable influence, including mistakenly recorded outliers. A responsible analyst checks whether a surprising residual reflects a real, consequential event or a defect in the source data. Choosing MSE never excuses deleting a difficult record, and choosing MAE does not prove that large misses are operationally harmless.

8. Why squared loss targets the mean and absolute loss targets the median

What constant prediction minimizes each loss if no Features are available? Consider five hypothetical claim durations: \(2,3,4,5,26\) days. The first four claims resolve quickly; one takes much longer. Suppose our model predicts the same constant \(c\) for all five.

Under squared loss, the best constant is the arithmetic mean:

\[\overline y=\frac{2+3+4+5+26}{5}=\frac{40}{5}=8\text{ days}\]

Check SSE when \(c=8\): \(36+25+16+9+324=410\). If we instead predict the median \(c=4\), SSE is \(4+1+0+1+484=490\). Squared error prefers the mean of eight despite it being larger than four of the five observed outcomes.

Under absolute loss, the optimal constant is a median, here \(4\) days. At \(c=4\), the absolute-error sum is \(2+1+0+1+22=26\). At \(c=8\), it is \(6+5+4+3+18=36\). Absolute error prefers four.

Constant prediction Sum of absolute errors Sum of squared errors
\(c=4\) (median) 26 490
\(c=8\) (mean) 36 410

Why is the mean optimal for squared loss? For \(S(c)=\sum_i(y_i-c)^2\), differentiate:

\[\frac{dS}{dc}=-2\sum_i(y_i-c)=2nc-2\sum_i y_i\]

Setting the derivative to zero gives \(c=(\sum_i y_i)/n=\overline y\); the second derivative \(2n>0\) confirms a minimum. For absolute loss, as the candidate \(c\) crosses each observed value, the balance of points to the left and right changes. A median leaves no more than half of the observations strictly on either side and minimizes the sum of distances. These are different estimation targets, not just two ways to describe the same average.

9. Huber Loss: a deliberately balanced compromise

Huber Loss behaves like squared loss for small errors and like a linear absolute penalty beyond a chosen cutoff \(\delta>0\). Its goal is to preserve smooth, useful learning near zero while reducing the extreme growth of large-error penalties. For the magnitude \(a=|y-\widehat y|\):

\[L_\delta(a)=\begin{cases}\frac12a^2,&a\le\delta,\\\delta(a-\frac12\delta),&a>\delta.\end{cases}\]

Read the formula before using it. First calculate the absolute error \(a\). If it is at most \(\delta\), square it and divide by two. If it is larger, multiply \(\delta\) by \(a-\delta/2\). The two pieces join continuously at \(a=\delta\), and their slopes also agree there. The value of \(\delta\) determines where the penalty stops growing quadratically.

Set \(\delta=3\) days for our claim data. A one-day error contributes \(1^2/2=0.5\); a twelve-day error contributes \(3(12-1.5)=31.5\). Thus Model A's average Huber loss is:

\[\overline L_{A,3}=\frac{5(0.5)+31.5}{6}=\frac{34}{6}=\boxed{5.6667}\]

Each four-day error from Model B is beyond the cutoff, contributing \(3(4-1.5)=7.5\). Its average is \(6(7.5)/6=\boxed{7.5}\). Huber with \(\delta=3\) prefers A.

But suppose we increase the cutoff to \(\delta=6\). Five small errors still contribute \(0.5\) each, while the twelve-day error contributes \(6(12-3)=54\). Model A averages \((2.5+54)/6=\boxed{9.4167}\). Every four-day error of B is now in the quadratic region and costs \(4^2/2=8\), so B averages \(\boxed{8}\) and B is preferred.

Huber cutoff Mean Huber for A Mean Huber for B Choice
\(\delta=3\) days 5.6667 7.5000 A
\(\delta=6\) days 9.4167 8.0000 B

The cutoff is a Hyperparameter, not something we should select after studying the Test outcomes. It belongs in a valid development-and-validation process. Also, the numerical Huber values are not in ordinary days or necessarily days squared across the entire domain, so communicate the cutoff and definition whenever reporting them.

Derive the Huber slope and check that its pieces meet correctly

The cutoff does more than switch labels: it changes the force with which a residual influences learning. Use the signed prediction error \(e=\widehat y-y\) and write Huber as \(L_\delta(e)=\tfrac12e^2\) when \(|e|\le\delta\) and \(L_\delta(e)=\delta(|e|-\delta/2)\) otherwise. Differentiation gives

\[\frac{dL_\delta}{de}=\begin{cases}e,&|e|\le\delta,\\\delta\,\operatorname{sign}(e),&|e|>\delta.\end{cases}\]

Here \(\operatorname{sign}(e)=+1\) for positive errors and \(-1\) for negative ones. At \(e=+\delta\), the left slope is \(+\delta\), and the right slope is also \(+\delta\). At \(e=-\delta\), both limits are \(-\delta\). The loss and its first derivative agree at the join; its second derivative changes there. At \(e=0\), the derivative is zero. For \(\delta=3\) days, errors of 1 and 12 days produce slope magnitudes 1 and 3, not 1 and 12: the exceptionally large error cannot create an arbitrarily large single-row Huber slope.

Interpretation and limit. A gradient of 3 is not “three days of error”; it is the change in the mathematical loss per unit change of signed prediction error, under the stated definition. The cutoff must be chosen with Training/Validation, considering the scale of the Target; changing the unit from days to hours also requires reconsidering \(\delta\).

10. Quantile Loss: when overprediction and underprediction are not equally costly

The losses considered so far depend only on error magnitude: an estimate six days too high costs the same as an estimate six days too low. But a customer-support team may care more about underpredicting completion time, which could create a promise that cannot be kept. This is a question about the direction of the error as well as its size.

Define \(u=y-\widehat y\). A positive \(u\) means the actual duration exceeded the forecast: underprediction. A negative \(u\) means the forecast was too high: overprediction. Quantile Loss, also called Pinball Loss, uses a parameter \(\tau\) between 0 and 1:

\[\rho_\tau(u)=\begin{cases}\tau u,&u\ge0,\\(\tau-1)u,&u<0.\end{cases}\]

For \(\tau=0.8\), an underprediction by five days costs \(0.8(5)=4\), while an overprediction by five days costs \((-0.2)(-5)=1\). This makes underprediction four times as expensive for equal magnitudes. When \(\tau=0.5\), Pinball Loss becomes one-half of absolute error, giving symmetric penalties.

To see what the objective learns, consider six synthetic historical claim durations: \(10,12,15,19,23,27\) days. Predicting one constant \(q\) to minimize total 0.8-Quantile Loss selects an empirical 80th-percentile level. We can demonstrate that with three candidates:

Forecast \(q\) Underprediction contributions Overprediction contributions Total 0.8-Quantile Loss
19 \(0.8(4+8)=9.6\) \(0.2(9+7+4)=4.0\) 13.6
23 \(0.8(4)=3.2\) \(0.2(13+11+8+4)=7.2\) 10.4
27 \(0\) \(0.2(17+15+12+8+4)=11.2\) 11.2

Here \(q=23\) has the smallest total Pinball Loss among these candidate forecasts, and it is also the minimizer for this six-point set at \(\tau=0.8\). A larger model would estimate a conditional quantile given Features, not a universal 23-day prediction. Quantile modeling is useful when a predictive interval, an upper planning threshold, or asymmetric consequences matter. Actual decision costs still need validation; \(\tau=0.8\) is a teaching choice, not evidence about insurer preferences.

11. Why Classification needs a different idea of prediction error

Suppose an online retailer wants to estimate whether an order will be returned (1) or kept (0). The Target is categorical, so a ten-day absolute error is no longer meaningful. A useful probabilistic classifier might predict \(P(Y=1\mid x)=0.80\) for an order. That statement means estimated return probability of 80%, not that the model predicts a numerical return amount of 0.8 orders.

If the retailer uses a decision threshold of 0.50, probabilities above that threshold become predicted class 1. But a class label alone discards information: forecasts of 0.51 and 0.99 both yield the same label, even though the second expresses far greater confidence. Accuracy checks correct labels at a chosen threshold; it does not directly reward or penalize the quality of the underlying probability.

A proper probabilistic loss evaluates probabilities against observed 0/1 outcomes. Two important examples are Binary Cross-Entropy (Log Loss) and the Brier Score. Both penalize confident mistakes more than weak ones, although they grow differently. Their use requires distinguishing the model's raw score, its predicted probability, and any subsequent decision threshold.

Read this distinction carefully. A Loss Function can train a probability model, while a later business rule converts probabilities into actions. Changing a threshold can change Precision and Recall without changing the model's Log Loss at all, because the probabilities themselves remain unchanged.

12. Binary Cross-Entropy: every logarithm worked out

For a binary observed label \(y\in\{0,1\}\) and predicted probability \(p\) of class 1, Binary Cross-Entropy is:

\[\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(y,p)=-\big[y\ln(p)+(1-y)\ln(1-p)\big]\]

Here \(\ln\) is the natural logarithm. When \(y=1\), the formula simplifies to \(-\ln(p)\): predicting a high probability for the true positive class earns low loss. When \(y=0\), it becomes \(-\ln(1-p)\): correctly assigning a low probability to the positive class earns low loss. Probabilities should lie strictly between zero and one for direct logarithm evaluation; real implementations use numerically stable methods for extreme scores.

Here are four invented ecommerce orders. We use Model P to predict each order's return probability:

Order Returned \(y\) Probability \(p\) Correct-class probability BCE contribution
R1 1 0.80 0.80 \(-\ln0.80=0.2231\)
R2 0 0.40 0.60 \(-\ln0.60=0.5108\)
R3 1 0.30 0.30 \(-\ln0.30=1.2040\)
R4 0 0.10 0.90 \(-\ln0.90=0.1054\)

The mean Cross-Entropy across four observations is:

\[\overline\ell_{P}=\frac{0.2231+0.5108+1.2040+0.1054}{4}\approx\boxed{0.5108}\]

Interpret the difficult row R3. The order actually was returned, but Model P placed only a 30% probability on a return. It assigned just 0.30 probability to the observed class, so that row receives the largest penalty. Cross-Entropy is always nonnegative and approaches zero as the predicted probability of the actual class approaches one; it increases sharply if a model is highly confident in the wrong outcome.

The result 0.5108 is not an Accuracy percentage and has no units of days. Cross-Entropy is a dimensionless score in natural-log units (often called nats). Smaller is better when all scores are computed using the same definition and a comparable evaluation population.

Why Cross-Entropy can train a probability model: the gradient of a score

Cross-Entropy is not just an after-training metric. Consider a logit \(z\), an unrestricted real number that is converted to a class-1 probability by the Sigmoid function \(p=\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\). Here \(e\) in the exponential is Euler's constant; it is not our residual. By the Chain Rule, \(dp/dz=p(1-p)\). Starting from the Binary Cross-Entropy formula above, differentiate each logarithm:

\[\frac{d\ell}{dp}=-\frac{y}{p}+\frac{1-y}{1-p}=\frac{p-y}{p(1-p)}.\]

Multiply the derivatives: \(d\ell/dz=(d\ell/dp)(dp/dz)=[(p-y)/(p(1-p))]p(1-p)=\boxed{p-y}\), for probabilities strictly inside \((0,1)\). If the observed label is 1 and the predicted probability is 0.80, the score gradient is \(0.80-1=-0.20\). A sufficiently small negative-gradient step raises the score, and therefore raises the probability of class 1. If the label is zero, the gradient is positive and the update reduces the score. This is a one-row derivative, not a complete fitted classifier; Logistic Regression is developed in Guide 10.

Numerical stability. Directly evaluating \(\ln 0\) is undefined, and blindly clipping probabilities changes the mathematical objective. Stable libraries typically compute Log Loss directly from logits using transformations that avoid extreme exponential/logarithm calculations; report which definition and implementation is used.

13. The same Accuracy can hide radically different probability quality

Compare Model P with Model Q below. At the same threshold of 0.50, both produce exactly the same class labels for our four orders: \((1,0,0,0)\). Since the observed labels are \((1,0,1,0)\), both classify three of four orders correctly and attain 75% Accuracy.

Order Actual \(y\) P probability Q probability Label at 0.50 (both)
R1 1 0.80 0.99 1
R2 0 0.40 0.05 0
R3 1 0.30 0.01 0
R4 0 0.10 0.02 0

Model Q is exceptionally confident—and exceptionally wrong—on R3, assigning only 1% probability to an event that happened. Its BCE is:

\[\overline\ell_Q=\frac{-\ln0.99-\ln0.95-\ln0.01-\ln0.98}{4}\approx\boxed{1.1717}\]

Thus Model P's mean BCE of 0.5108 is better than Q's 1.1717 despite identical 75% Accuracy. Q's \(-\ln(0.01)\approx4.6052\) penalty on R3 drives much of the difference. This is a core reason probabilistic losses matter: the model is accountable not merely for a decision but for how certain it was.

We must also avoid a converse mistake: a low mean Log Loss on four examples does not establish that a model is calibrated in the population. Calibration asks whether events occur about 70% of the time among cases assigned approximately 70% probability, across sufficiently large and relevant samples. Log Loss is a useful proper scoring rule, but real calibration assessment needs held-out observations and appropriate plots or grouping.

14. Brier Score: another probability loss, with complete arithmetic

The Brier Score for binary outcomes averages squared differences between the predicted probability and the actual 0/1 label:

\[\mathrm{Brier}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_i-y_i)^2\]

Unlike MAE measured in claim days, this error compares a dimensionless probability with a zero-or-one indicator. For our four orders, Model P contributes \((0.8-1)^2=0.04\), \((0.4-0)^2=0.16\), \((0.3-1)^2=0.49\), and \((0.1-0)^2=0.01\):

\[\mathrm{Brier}_P=\frac{0.04+0.16+0.49+0.01}{4}=\boxed{0.1750}\]

For Model Q, the terms are \(0.0001,0.0025,0.9801,0.0004\), giving:

\[\mathrm{Brier}_Q=\frac{0.0001+0.0025+0.9801+0.0004}{4}=\boxed{0.245775}\]

Both metrics prefer P. Brier is bounded between 0 and 1 for binary probabilities, whereas Log Loss can become arbitrarily large if the model assigns a probability near zero to an event that occurs. Neither should be judged without comparing to an appropriate reference forecast: for instance, a constant prevalence-based prediction estimated using Training, not a rate secretly computed from Test.

A subtle distinction: MSE can indeed be used as a probabilistic classification training loss when the target is encoded 0/1—that is precisely the Brier form. It is therefore inaccurate to claim that squared loss can never be used in Classification. The more precise statement is that different losses emphasize different probability errors, yield different optimization properties and require interpretation consistent with the predicted quantity.

15. Multiclass Cross-Entropy and Hinge Loss: what changes?

Not every Classification problem is binary. Suppose a retailer predicts the reason for a product return, with three mutually exclusive classes: \(K_1\) = size, \(K_2\) = damage, \(K_3\) = other. If a model outputs probabilities \((1/3,1/2,1/6)\), they sum to one. If the observed class is \(K_2\), Multiclass Cross-Entropy penalizes the probability assigned to that true class:

\[\ell_{\mathrm{multiclass}}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_k\ln(p_k)=-\ln(p_{\mathrm{true}})\]

For a one-hot label \((0,1,0)\), the computation is \(-\ln(1/2)=\boxed{0.6931}\). For another order with probabilities \((1/6,1/3,1/2)\) whose true class is \(K_3\), the loss is again 0.6931; the mean over the two orders is 0.6931. We do not sum separate binary probabilities independently when the categories are exclusive; a Softmax model typically normalizes class scores to a probability distribution. For Multilabel Classification, separate binary outputs and Binary Cross-Entropy may be more appropriate.

Hinge Loss, often associated with Support Vector Machines, evaluates a signed classification score rather than a probability. Let \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\) and let \(s\) be the model's real-valued decision score:

\[\ell_{\mathrm{hinge}}(y,s)=\max(0,1-ys)\]

For a positive example \(y=+1\) and \(s=0.4\), the margin \(ys=0.4\), so Hinge Loss is \(1-0.4=0.6\). If \(s=1.2\), the margin exceeds one and loss is zero. For a negative example \(y=-1\) with \(s=0.3\), the margin is \(-0.3\), giving loss \(1.3\). A score of 1.2 here is not a probability of 120%. The distinction between a margin score and a calibrated probability is essential.

16. How Loss actually guides parameter learning: a derivative from start to finish

A loss is more than a score printed after fitting; during training, it can provide a direction for changing parameters. Start with an intentionally simple constant model \(\widehat y=\theta\), whose single unknown parameter \(\theta\) is its prediction for every observation. Take observed outcomes \(y=(2,4,6)\). We choose the MSE objective:

\[J(\theta)=\frac{(\theta-2)^2+(\theta-4)^2+(\theta-6)^2}{3}\]

Differentiate term by term using \(d(\theta-a)^2/d\theta=2(\theta-a)\):

\[J'(\theta)=\frac{2(\theta-2)+2(\theta-4)+2(\theta-6)}3=\boxed{2\theta-8}\]

The derivative tells us the local slope of the training objective. Setting it to zero yields \(2\theta-8=0\), so \(\theta^*=4\). The second derivative is \(J''(\theta)=2>0\), confirming that this stationary point is the minimum. We can independently check: \(J(4)=[4+0+4]/3=\boxed{2.6667}\).

Now pretend we do not know the minimum. Start at \(\theta_0=3\) and use Gradient Descent with learning rate \(\eta=0.10\). The update is \(\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta J'(\theta_t)\). At \(\theta_0=3\), the gradient is \(2(3)-8=-2\); thus:

\[\theta_1=3-0.10(-2)=\boxed{3.2}\]

Check that the objective declined:

\[J(3)=\frac{1+1+9}{3}=3.6667,\qquad J(3.2)=\frac{1.44+0.64+7.84}{3}=\boxed{3.3067}\]

The improvement is a consequence of moving in the negative-gradient direction with a suitable step size, not a guarantee that every arbitrary step will help. For larger learning rates, an update can overshoot or even diverge. Here the objective is convex, so the optimum is straightforward; real models can have more complex landscapes.

17. Extend the gradient to a Linear Regression coefficient

The previous example changes one constant. For a genuine Linear Regression model, the prediction depends on Features. Let \(\widehat y_i=b+wx_i\), where \(b\) is the Intercept and \(w\) is the Slope. With MSE:

\[J(b,w)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b+wx_i-y_i)^2\]

To update parameters, apply the Chain Rule: differentiating a squared error with respect to a coefficient multiplies the derivative of the square by the derivative of the model prediction. Consequently:

\[\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_i(b+wx_i-y_i),\qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial w}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_i(b+wx_i-y_i)x_i\]

Why does \(x_i\) appear in the slope gradient? Because changing \(w\) by a small amount changes prediction \(i\) in proportion to \(x_i\), whereas changing \(b\) shifts every prediction equally. This explains why the Feature scale can affect optimization behavior.

For one observation \((x,y)=(2,5)\), and current values \(b=1,w=1\), the prediction is \(1+1(2)=3\), so \(\widehat y-y=-2\). With a one-observation MSE, \(\partial J/\partial b=2(-2)=-4\) and \(\partial J/\partial w=2(-2)(2)=-8\). If \(\eta=0.05\), the simultaneous update yields \(b_{new}=1-0.05(-4)=1.2\) and \(w_{new}=1-0.05(-8)=1.4\). The new prediction at \(x=2\) is \(1.2+1.4(2)=4.0\), closer to five. This is one numerical step, not evidence that a model trained on a single observation will generalize.

18. From per-row loss to Empirical Risk, weighting and Regularization

Real algorithms learn from many observations. The Empirical Risk is the average loss on Training:

\[\widehat R(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(y_i,f_\theta(x_i))\]

Here \(f_\theta\) is the model with parameters \(\theta\). A Batch step uses all Training rows to compute an update, Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) uses one row at a time, and Mini-Batch learning uses a subset. The objective concept is the same; updates and their noise differ. When computing the mini-batch average, its denominator is the number of rows in that batch, not silently the full-dataset size.

Sometimes observations deserve different, justified weights. Define nonnegative \(w_i\), and for a normalized weighted mean use:

\[\widehat R_w=\frac{\sum_i w_i\ell_i}{\sum_i w_i},\qquad \sum_iw_i>0\]

For losses \((1,4,9)\) and weights \((1,1,2)\), the weighted average is \([1(1)+1(4)+2(9)]/(1+1+2)=23/4=\boxed{5.75}\), versus the unweighted \((1+4+9)/3=\boxed{4.6667}\). The higher weight on the third observation makes its error count more heavily. Weights are policy or modeling choices and can create unfairness or overemphasize questionable records if selected thoughtlessly. Other weighting conventions exist; identify yours.

A training objective may also include a complexity penalty:

\[J(\theta)=\widehat R(\theta)+\lambda\,\Omega(\theta)\]

For Ridge-style regularization, \(\Omega\) commonly involves sums of squared coefficients (with intercept treatment specified); \(\lambda\ge0\) controls penalty strength. The purpose is not to make the Training error alone as small as possible, but to balance fit and complexity with the hope of improved generalization. When reporting Validation/Test prediction metrics, do not quietly add the training-only Regularization penalty to the reported MAE or RMSE unless the evaluation definition explicitly requires it.

Complete one Regularization optimization instead of stopping at the formula

To see how a penalty changes a learned coefficient, take one synthetic Training row with Feature \(x=1\) and Target \(y=3\), both dimensionless for this deliberately algebraic illustration. Choose the model \(\widehat y=wx\), without an Intercept, and the explicit Ridge-style objective \(J_\lambda(w)=(w-3)^2+\lambda w^2\). The first term is one-row MSE; the second penalizes large weights. Here \(\lambda\ge0\) is a Hyperparameter set from development data, and both terms are compatible because the quantities are dimensionless.

\[\frac{dJ_\lambda}{dw}=2(w-3)+2\lambda w=2(1+\lambda)w-6.\]

Set the derivative to zero: \(2(1+\lambda)w-6=0\), so \(\boxed{w_\lambda=3/(1+\lambda)}\). Because \(J_\lambda''(w)=2(1+\lambda)>0\), this is the unique minimum. When \(\lambda=0\), \(w=3\): Training MSE is \((3-3)^2=0\) and total objective is 0. When \(\lambda=1\), \(w=1.5\): Training MSE increases to \((1.5-3)^2=2.25\), penalty is \(1(1.5)^2=2.25\), and total objective equals \(4.5\). Under \(\lambda=1\), the old unregularized coefficient \(w=3\) would cost \(0+1(3)^2=9\), so shrinking from 3 to 1.5 genuinely reduces the regularized objective, even while increasing Training prediction error.

This establishes a mechanism, not improved generalization: one invented Training row cannot demonstrate better future accuracy. A real model chooses \(\lambda\) using Validation and reports held-out prediction metrics separately from the training penalty. The choice not to penalize an Intercept is a separate modeling convention (there is no Intercept here).

19. Loss, Evaluation Metric and actual decision cost are different

A training Loss Function should be useful to optimize; an Evaluation Metric should measure performance that matters; and a business cost describes consequences of actions. They can be related, but should not be confused. Our two claim models make that concrete: Model A minimizes observed MAE; Model B minimizes observed MSE. An insurer still needs to decide whether systematic overestimation, exceptionally late promises, calibration of intervals, or effects on different claimant groups are acceptable.

Suppose the company makes a deadline commitment and the operational cost of underpredicting is twice the cost of overpredicting by one day. A direction-sensitive objective may be more appropriate than either symmetric MAE or MSE. But the cost ratio must come from an explicit, defensible business process. We cannot infer it from six fabricated claims. A Quantile objective reflects asymmetry, whereas the actual financial cost could be nonlinear or have thresholds.

Good practice uses Training to fit parameters; Validation or a well-designed Cross-Validation process to select loss definitions, Hyperparameters, and thresholds; and an untouched Test for final evaluation. Do not tune between A and B after reading Test errors and still treat that same Test as independent evidence. Fit preprocessing transformations only on the appropriate Training folds. Small samples are valuable for teaching the arithmetic but insufficient for claims of production accuracy.

What about extreme cases? Inspect the error distribution, MAE and RMSE together; assess whether high-loss rows are measurement problems or legitimate observations; use held-out checks across relevant subgroups and time periods. The most mathematically convenient loss is not automatically the most useful or responsible one.

Step 1 — Define error direction. Use \(u=y-\widehat y\). Positive \(u\) means the actual restoration took longer than forecast (underprediction), and negative \(u\) means we forecast too many hours. Our entire signed-error vectors are \(u_A=(0,0,0,0,+9)\) and \(u_B=(+2,-2,+2,-2,+2)\).

Step 2 — Calculate both symmetric losses from the five rows. Model A has absolute errors \((0,0,0,0,9)\), hence \(\mathrm{MAE}_A=(0+0+0+0+9)/5=\boxed{1.8\ \mathrm{hours}}\); \(\mathrm{MSE}_A=(0+0+0+0+81)/5=\boxed{16.2\ \mathrm{hours}^2}\). Model B has five absolute errors of two hours, hence \(\mathrm{MAE}_B=(2+2+2+2+2)/5=\boxed{2\ \mathrm{hours}}\) and \(\mathrm{MSE}_B=(4+4+4+4+4)/5=\boxed{4\ \mathrm{hours}^2}\). MAE prefers A; MSE prefers B. The ranking reversal is a property of the losses, not a calculation mistake.

Step 3 — Calculate Huber, showing the cutoff. Set \(\delta=1\) hour. All four zero errors of A cost zero; its one nine-hour miss costs \(1(9-1/2)=8.5\), so mean Huber \(=8.5/5=\boxed{1.7}\) under this loss definition. Each two-hour miss of B costs \(1(2-1/2)=1.5\), giving \(\boxed{1.5}\). With this predeclared cutoff, Huber prefers B. Do not compare these Huber values directly with MAE in hours: their loss scales differ.

Step 4 — Use direction-sensitive Quantile Loss. Suppose an hour of underprediction has a hypothetical $80 operational consequence and an hour of overprediction $20, both linear and additive for teaching purposes. Then the corresponding quantile weight is \(\tau=80/(80+20)=0.8\). For A, total Pinball Loss is \(0.8(9)=7.2\), mean \(7.2/5=\boxed{1.44\ \mathrm{hours}}\). For B, the positive errors total \(2+2+2=6\) hours and negative-error magnitudes total \(2+2=4\) hours, so total Pinball is \(0.8(6)+0.2(4)=4.8+0.8=5.6\), mean \(5.6/5=\boxed{1.12\ \mathrm{hours}}\). B is preferred under this asymmetric objective as well.

Step 5 — Interpret decision costs separately. With the assumed $80/$20 per-hour schedule, A costs \(80(9)+20(0)=\boxed{\$720}\) across five incidents, or \(\$144\) per incident; B costs \(80(6)+20(4)=\boxed{\$560}\), or \(\$112\) per incident. The identity \(\mathrm{mean\ cost}=100\times\mathrm{mean\ Pinball}_{0.8}\) holds here only because these illustrative linear costs share the $100 total weight. Electricity restoration often involves nonlinear safety and customer impacts; no real-world policy should be set from these invented numbers.

Decision rule, not data shopping. If a forecast deadline drives severe underprediction costs, define that requirement before examining independent Test outcomes. Select the loss and cutoff with Training/Validation, investigate large errors, and evaluate the frozen process on Test once. These five incidents teach arithmetic; they cannot establish future reliability.

20. Reproduce the calculations in Python, and know what each line verifies

We can reproduce every key result with NumPy, without fitting a predictive model that the teaching example never specified. The first block stores the exact six claims and the two sets of predictions, then calculates average absolute error, mean squared error and RMSE. The second block computes binary Cross-Entropy and Brier Score for the four ecommerce orders.

import numpy as np

y = np.array([12, 18, 20, 27, 35, 48], dtype=float)
pred_a = np.array([13, 19, 21, 28, 36, 60], dtype=float)
pred_b = np.array([16, 22, 24, 31, 39, 52], dtype=float)

for name, pred in [('A', pred_a), ('B', pred_b)]:
    error = pred - y
    mae = np.mean(np.abs(error))
    mse = np.mean(error ** 2)
    rmse = np.sqrt(mse)
    huber3 = np.mean(np.where(np.abs(error) <= 3,
                              0.5 * error ** 2,
                              3 * (np.abs(error) - 1.5)))
    print(name, round(mae, 4), round(mse, 4), round(rmse, 4), round(huber3, 4))

labels = np.array([1, 0, 1, 0], dtype=float)
prob_p = np.array([0.80, 0.40, 0.30, 0.10])
prob_q = np.array([0.99, 0.05, 0.01, 0.02])

for name, p in [('P', prob_p), ('Q', prob_q)]:
    log_loss = -np.mean(labels * np.log(p) + (1 - labels) * np.log(1 - p))
    brier = np.mean((p - labels) ** 2)
    accuracy = np.mean((p >= 0.50) == labels)
    print(name, round(log_loss, 4), round(brier, 6), accuracy)

Expected numerical values are A: MAE \(2.8333\), MSE \(24.8333\), RMSE \(4.9833\), Huber(3) \(5.6667\); B: \(4\), \(16\), \(4\), \(7.5\). For probabilities, P has Log Loss approximately \(0.5108\), Brier \(0.175\), Accuracy \(0.75\); Q has approximately \(1.1717\), \(0.245775\), and \(0.75\).

A scikit-learn lab can later confirm these metrics with mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error, log_loss and brier_score_loss, taking care that library versions may differ in RMSE helpers. The coding goal today is to reproduce the arithmetic and understand its meaning, not to hide essential steps behind prebuilt functions.

Independent reproducibility: the new industry, Regularization and a gradient check

This second, standalone NumPy cell uses the complete five-incident dataset above. It checks MAE, MSE, Huber, Pinball and hypothetical dollar costs, derives the Ridge-style coefficient directly, and uses a central finite difference to verify the Cross-Entropy logit gradient. It needs only NumPy, downloads no data, and uses no randomness or knowledge of variables in the earlier cell.

# Independent transfer case: fully synthetic electricity-restoration hours.
import numpy as np

y_grid = np.array([6., 8., 5., 9., 12.])
forecast_a = np.array([6., 8., 5., 9., 3.])
forecast_b = np.array([4., 10., 3., 11., 10.])

def huber_loss(u, delta=1.):
    a = np.abs(u)
    return np.where(a <= delta, 0.5*a**2, delta*(a-0.5*delta))

def pinball_loss(u, tau=0.8):
    return np.where(u >= 0., tau*u, (tau-1.)*u)

for label, prediction, expected in (
    ('A', forecast_a, [1.8, 16.2, 1.7, 1.44, 720.]),
    ('B', forecast_b, [2., 4., 1.5, 1.12, 560.]),
):
    u = y_grid - prediction  # positive = underprediction
    mae = np.mean(np.abs(u))
    mse = np.mean(u**2)
    huber = np.mean(huber_loss(u, delta=1.))
    pinball = np.mean(pinball_loss(u, tau=0.8))
    cost = 80*np.maximum(u, 0).sum() + 20*np.maximum(-u, 0).sum()
    obtained = np.array([mae, mse, huber, pinball, cost])
    assert np.allclose(obtained, expected, atol=1e-12)
    assert np.isclose(cost/len(u), 100*pinball)
    print(label, 'MAE MSE Huber Pinball Total-cost:', np.round(obtained, 4))

# One-feature Ridge-style algebra, one dimensionless training observation.
for lam, expected_coef in ((0.,3.),(1.,1.5)):
    w = 3./(1.+lam)
    training_mse = (w-3.)**2
    regularizer = lam*w**2
    total = training_mse+regularizer
    assert np.isclose(w,expected_coef)
    assert np.isclose(2*(w-3)+2*lam*w, 0.)
    print('lambda, coefficient, Training-MSE, penalty, total:',
          lam, w, training_mse, regularizer, total)

# Gradient of Binary Cross-Entropy with a logit score.
y_label, p = 1., 0.8
analytic = p-y_label
h = 1e-5
z = np.log(p/(1-p))
def loss_from_logit(t):
    # numerically stable BCE when label=1:
    return np.logaddexp(0., -t)
numerical = (loss_from_logit(z+h)-loss_from_logit(z-h))/(2*h)
assert np.isclose(analytic,numerical,atol=1e-9)
print('BCE logit gradient analytic / finite difference:', analytic, numerical)

The expected restoration vectors are A: (1.8, 16.2, 1.7, 1.44, $720) and B: (2, 4, 1.5, 1.12, $560) in the printed order (MAE hours, MSE hours², mean Huber at \(\delta=1\), mean Pinball at \(\tau=0.8\), total hypothetical dollars). The Ridge illustration prints \(w=3\) when \(\lambda=0\) and \(w=1.5\) when \(\lambda=1\). The analytic Binary Cross-Entropy logit gradient is \(-0.2\), independently approximated by finite differences. These assertions check arithmetic and code consistency; they do not validate model performance on real grid data.

21. Common mistakes: diagnosis, consequence and correction

1. “Loss” and “Accuracy” are interchangeable. Accuracy uses class labels after a threshold; Cross-Entropy uses probabilities. Our ecommerce models both achieve 75% Accuracy while their Log Loss differs strongly. Correction: name the output and mathematical criterion precisely.

2. MAE and MSE select the same winner. They do not. A wins on MAE; B wins on MSE because of the single twelve-day mistake. Correction: calculate both on the same observations and explain why rankings differ.

3. A low average means every prediction is good. Model A has MAE below three days yet misses C6 by twelve days. Correction: inspect individual residuals, distributions and meaningful tail-error measures.

4. Squared errors are expressed in days. MSE uses days² when the target is days; RMSE returns to days. Correction: track units through the formula.

5. All errors have the same sign convention. Textbooks alternate between \(y-\widehat y\) and \(\widehat y-y\); absolute and squared losses ignore the sign, but Quantile Loss does not. Correction: define signed error before discussing underprediction.

6. More complicated loss always improves generalization. Huber or weighted objectives can be suitable, but a new cutoff or weight can overfit Validation. Correction: justify selections using an evaluation protocol and the problem's consequences.

7. Confidence does not matter if a class label is correct. Model Q makes the same decisions as P yet is dangerously confident about R3. Correction: use proper probabilistic scoring when probabilities matter.

8. Hinge score means return probability. An SVM margin of 1.2 is not 120%. Correction: distinguish decision scores, probabilities and thresholded labels.

9. Average all data before computing transformations. A scaling mean or imputation median fitted using Test contaminates evaluation even without labels. Correction: fit inside Training or each Training fold, then transform held-out data unchanged.

10. Use Test to choose the loss. Choosing MAE, MSE or the Huber cutoff after looking at Test outcomes spoils its independence. Correction: select with development data and freeze the choice before Test.

11. A lower mathematical loss proves a business benefit. Predictive performance is not causal evidence of improved claim operations, fairness or customer outcomes. Correction: separately evaluate the real intervention and operational constraints.

12. Delete every high-loss observation. The largest residual can be an error, but it can also be a real case the system must handle. Correction: investigate provenance, correct documented defects and assess robustness without retrospective cherry-picking.

13. BCE can safely take \(\ln0\) as an ordinary finite number. It cannot; \(\ln0\) diverges. Correction: use stable library implementations that work with logits or appropriately handle numerical boundaries.

14. Regularization is itself a Test metric. A complexity penalty helps shape the training objective, but a Test MAE should measure prediction errors according to its declared definition. Correction: report objective components and held-out metrics separately.

15. The smallest Training loss guarantees the best model. Training fit can improve while unseen performance deteriorates because of overfitting or shift. Correction: evaluate generalization with correct splits and relevant baselines.

22. Knowledge checks: calculate, reason and interpret

1. Distinguish the residual from the per-row loss.

For an actual duration of 18 days and a 22-day prediction, prediction-minus-actual error is +4; the residual defined as observed-minus-predicted is −4 days. Absolute loss is 4 days and squared loss is 16 days². State the sign convention before comparing directional penalties.

2. Recalculate Model A MAE without looking at the summary table.

The complete absolute-error vector is (1,1,1,1,1,12), with sum 17. Divide by n=6: MAE=17/6≈2.8333 days. Note that this average does not mean every claim had an error below 2.8333 days.

3. Which model has lower MSE? Explain the reversal from MAE.

A has SSE=5×1²+12²=149, so MSE=149/6≈24.8333. B has SSE=6×4²=96, MSE=96/6=16. B is preferred by MSE because its quadratic penalty avoids A’s single twelve-day error; MAE instead prefers A’s many one-day errors.

4. Derive RMSE values and interpret units.

RMSE_A=√(149/6)≈4.9833 days and RMSE_B=√16=4 days. Squared loss is in days², but RMSE returns to days. It is not the average absolute error, and the two models retain their MSE ranking after taking square roots.

5. What happens to Model A MSE if C6 were predicted at 49 days instead of 60?

The C6 absolute error would become 1 rather than 12. All six squared errors would equal 1, so SSE=6 and MSE=1 day². This is a hypothetical sensitivity calculation, not permission to change a held-out prediction after seeing the true outcome.

6. Compare mean and median as constant predictions for (2,3,4,5,26).

Mean is (2+3+4+5+26)/5=8, while median is 4. At 8, SSE=410 and sum absolute errors=36; at 4, SSE=490 and sum absolute errors=26. Squared loss favors 8, absolute loss favors 4.

7. Compute the Huber(δ=3) loss for an error of 12 days.

Since |e|=12>3, use δ(|e|−δ/2)=3(12−1.5)=31.5. Using 0.5×12²=72 would incorrectly apply the small-error branch after crossing the cutoff.

8. Why does changing δ alter the preferred model?

At δ=3, A has (5×0.5+31.5)/6=5.6667 and B has 7.5, so A wins. At δ=6, A has (2.5+54)/6=9.4167 and B has 8, so B wins. δ changes where Huber transitions from quadratic to linear, and belongs in validated Hyperparameter selection.

9. Compute Pinball Loss at τ=0.8 for an underprediction of five days and an overprediction of five days.

Using u=actual−prediction, underprediction gives u=+5 and loss 0.8×5=4. Overprediction gives u=−5 and loss (0.8−1)(−5)=1. Underprediction is four times as costly for the same absolute error in this specified loss.

10. Calculate Binary Cross-Entropy for a returned order assigned probability 0.30.

The outcome is y=1, so BCE=−ln(0.30)=1.20397≈1.2040. Its 0.30 confidence in the observed positive class is lower than R1’s 0.80, giving a higher penalty.

11. Why do P and Q have the same Accuracy but different Log Loss?

At threshold 0.50 both predict (1,0,0,0), matching three of four labels. Q gives only 0.01 probability to R3, which actually returns; its penalty −ln(0.01)=4.6052 is much larger than P’s −ln(0.30)=1.2040. Their mean losses differ even though labels agree.

12. Verify the Brier Score for Model P.

The squared probability errors are 0.04, 0.16, 0.49 and 0.01. Their sum is 0.70 and the average is 0.70/4=0.175. The score is not measured in days; it measures probabilistic discrepancy from 0/1 outcomes.

13. Find Multiclass Cross-Entropy from a true-class probability of one-half.

For a mutually exclusive three-class problem with One-Hot target, Cross-Entropy equals −ln(p_true)=−ln(0.5)=0.6931. Do not add the probabilities of incorrect classes as separate positive labels.

14. Calculate a Hinge Loss from score s=0.4 for y=+1.

Hinge=max(0,1−ys)=max(0,1−(+1)(0.4))=0.6. The score is a margin-oriented real number, not a probability of 40% unless a separate probabilistic calibration model is provided.

15. Perform one Gradient Descent update for J(θ) with θ₀=3 and η=0.10.

J′(θ)=2θ−8, so J′(3)=−2. The update θ₁=3−0.10(−2)=3.2. J falls from 11/3≈3.6667 to 9.92/3≈3.3067. Explaining the sign of the update is part of the answer: subtracting a negative derivative moves θ upward.

16. Calculate the weighted loss average for losses (1,4,9), weights (1,1,2).

Compute weighted sum 1×1+1×4+2×9=23; the weights total 4. Thus weighted loss=23/4=5.75, compared with unweighted 14/3≈4.6667. Explain the policy consequence: the third record now influences the objective twice as strongly as either of the first two.

17. Identify Data Leakage in model selection.

If a team tries MAE, MSE and Huber cutoffs, repeatedly consulting Test to choose the best, Test has become development information. Use Training and Validation or Cross-Validation for choices, then report a final frozen model on a genuinely untouched Test.

18. Choose a loss for asymmetric deadline promises and defend the choice.

When underestimating resolution days is materially more harmful than overestimating by the same amount, an upper-quantile / Pinball objective may reflect the asymmetry better than symmetric MAE. The exact τ needs a defensible cost model and validation. If classifying whether deadlines are missed instead, probabilistic Classification loss may be appropriate—define the target first.

Transfer exercise set — a distinct problem, complete answers

19. Reconstruct both MAE and MSE rankings from the five grid incidents without reusing the insurance predictions.

Full actual durations are \((6,8,5,9,12)\) hours. A forecasts \((6,8,5,9,3)\) so absolute errors are \((0,0,0,0,9)\), sum 9 and squared sum \(9^2=81\); dividing each by five gives MAE 1.8 hours and MSE 16.2 hours². B forecasts \((4,10,3,11,10)\), giving signed actual-minus-predicted errors \((2,-2,2,-2,2)\); absolute sum \(2+2+2+2+2=10\), squared sum \(4+4+4+4+4=20\), hence MAE 2 hours and MSE 4 hours². Therefore A wins MAE and B wins MSE; neither metric was computed using a different set of incidents.

20. Calculate the Huber Loss ranking with delta = 1 hour and interpret the result.

For A: four zero-error rows contribute \(0\); the nine-hour error is in the linear regime and contributes \(1(9-0.5)=8.5\); mean Huber \(8.5/5=1.7\). For B: each error of magnitude 2 contributes \(1(2-0.5)=1.5\); total \(5(1.5)=7.5\), mean 1.5. Under this predeclared cutoff, B has smaller mean Huber. The Huber score is defined relative to \(\delta\); we cannot claim B is objectively better for all operational goals or choose the cutoff retrospectively using Test.

21. With hypothetical underprediction cost $80/h and overprediction cost $20/h, derive tau and both total costs.

Underprediction corresponds to positive \(u=y-\widehat y\). Normalizing the assumed linear costs gives \(\tau=80/(80+20)=0.8\) and \(1-\tau=0.2\). A underpredicts by 9 total hours: total cost \(9(80)=\$720\). B underpredicts by \(2+2+2=6\) hours and overpredicts by \(2+2=4\): total cost \(6(80)+4(20)=\$560\). Their mean Pinball scores are \(7.2/5=1.44\) and \([0.8(6)+0.2(4)]/5=1.12\). Check units: costs in dollars, Pinball computed from signed hours and dimensionless weights. These are assumed teaching costs, not grid-operator estimates.

22. A regularized one-feature model fits x=1, y=3. Solve w when lambda=1 and explain why Training MSE can rise.

With \(J(w)=(w-3)^2+w^2\), derivative \(J'(w)=2(w-3)+2w=4w-6\). Setting it to zero gives \(w=6/4=1.5\), and \(J''=4>0\) confirms the minimum. Now Training MSE \((1.5-3)^2=2.25\), penalty \((1.5)^2=2.25\), total 4.5. Without the penalty, \(w=3\) fits Training exactly but costs \(0+9=9\) under the new objective. This shows shrinkage but does not prove better Validation/Test predictions. The loss used for optimization is distinct from the held-out metric.

23. For observed class y=1 and predicted class-1 probability p=0.8, reconstruct d(BCE)/dz for a Sigmoid logit.

\(d\ell/dp=-1/p=-1/0.8=-1.25\) for a positive label. Sigmoid gives \(dp/dz=p(1-p)=0.8(0.2)=0.16\). Multiplying by Chain Rule: \(d\ell/dz=(-1.25)(0.16)=-0.2\), equivalent to \(p-y=0.8-1=-0.2\). Thus a small negative-gradient step increases the logit and the probability assigned to the observed class. This is neither Accuracy nor a decision threshold; it is a derivative of a training loss.

Training Objective Aggregate quantity minimized during fitting; can include average loss and Regularization.
Residual Observed outcome minus prediction; signed and measured in target units.
MAE Average absolute error; in the original target units.
SSE Sum of squared errors; scales with dataset size.
MSE Average squared error; emphasizes larger mistakes.
RMSE Square root of MSE; preserves MSE's ordering while returning target units.
Huber Loss Quadratic for small errors and linear for large errors, with a chosen cutoff.
Quantile / Pinball Loss Asymmetric penalty used for conditional quantile estimation.
Binary Cross-Entropy Proper probabilistic scoring loss for binary outcomes, using logarithms.
Brier Score Average squared difference between binary probability and 0/1 outcome.
Multiclass Cross-Entropy Negative log-probability assigned to the true class.
Hinge Loss Margin-based loss on a signed classification score.
Gradient Derivative(s) of an objective with respect to trainable parameters.
Empirical Risk Average loss over the observed Training sample.
Sample Weight Explicit factor controlling an observation's contribution to an aggregate objective.
Regularization Penalty on model complexity or parameters, added to a fitting objective.
Calibration Agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies in a relevant population.
Data Leakage Improper flow of future or held-out information into fitting or model selection.

Bringing the chapter together. The same six claim outcomes led MAE to favor A and MSE to favor B; neither criterion was inherently mistaken. Mean and median emerged as optimal constants under different penalties. Huber introduced a tunable compromise, Quantile Loss introduced asymmetric consequences, and Cross-Entropy evaluated the confidence of Classification probabilities. Finally, derivatives connected the mathematical loss to parameter updates. The purpose of understanding Loss Functions is not to memorize the biggest possible collection of formulas; it is to know what each objective rewards, what it penalizes, what it can optimize, and whether those choices serve the real problem.
