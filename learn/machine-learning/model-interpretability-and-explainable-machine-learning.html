Model Interpretability and Explainable Machine Learning

Understand Feature Importance, Permutation Importance, PDP, ICE, SHAP and LIME from first principles, with reproducible calculations and responsible interpretation.

1. A prediction can be accurate yet unexplained

A grocery store wants to anticipate how many kilograms of perishable food it may discard each day. A prediction is useful for planning, but a manager also wants to know why the estimate is high. We must separate three questions: What does the model predict? Which inputs influence that prediction? Would changing an input actually change waste in reality? The third is causal and generally cannot be answered merely by inspecting a predictive model.

Model interpretability describes the degree to which a person can understand a model's operation or output. Explainable Machine Learning (XAI) uses techniques to describe model behavior, including when the model itself is complicated. A simple linear formula is directly inspectable; a trained forest or Neural Network often requires approximate explanations. The methods in this chapter explain the model, not the physical mechanism producing waste.

Our complete synthetic case includes only two binary Features. That makes every explanation exactly reproducible before we discuss approximations, correlated Features, and production use. There will be no unsupported claims about actual store operations.

Learning outcomes: reconstruct a fitted interaction formula; compute errors on held-out examples; implement and interpret Permutation Importance; derive PDP and ICE; calculate SHAP values exactly using coalition weights; contrast explanations under different background distributions; and diagnose when an explanation is scientifically misleading.

2. Meet the two Features and the complete reference dataset

A binary indicator equals 1 when a condition holds and 0 otherwise. Let \(P=1\) indicate a promotion planned in advance and \(H=1\) indicate that the forecast available before the decision predicts high heat. Both are known before our illustrative same-day prediction, avoiding future-weather leakage. Let \(\hat y=f(P,H)\) represent predicted discarded kilograms per day. All observations, feature definitions, and quantities here are invented for teaching.

We begin with four synthetic Training prototypes, one for each combination. This tiny, balanced reference also becomes our first explanation background. In real work the background must be chosen to represent the intended reference population—not automatically to obtain convenient numbers.

Prototype Promotion \(P\) High heat \(H\) Observed waste (kg/day)
T1 0 0 8
T2 1 0 12
T3 0 1 14
T4 1 1 26

The Training design has four observations and two predictors; their levels are indicators without physical units. The response has kilograms/day. The four prototypes do not provide evidence that we can forecast an unseen store well: we will later create a distinct synthetic holdout set to illustrate evaluation, and explicitly avoid treating its contrived accuracy as industry evidence.

3. Derive every coefficient instead of announcing a model

Use an interaction model \(f(P,H)=\beta_0+\beta_P P+\beta_H H+\beta_{PH}PH\). Here \(\beta_0\) is the reference prediction in kg/day, \(\beta_P\) is the promotion change when \(H=0\), \(\beta_H\) is the heat change when \(P=0\), and \(\beta_{PH}\) is the additional joint effect (kg/day). The term \(PH\) equals 1 only if both indicators equal 1.

We can solve all four coefficients directly from the complete Training table, without hidden fitting steps. For T1, \(P=H=0\), so \(8=\beta_0\) and \(\beta_0=8\). For T2, \(12=8+\beta_P\), hence \(\beta_P=12-8=4\). For T3, \(14=8+\beta_H\), hence \(\beta_H=14-8=6\). Finally, T4 satisfies \(26=8+4+6+\beta_{PH}\), so \(\beta_{PH}=26-18=8\).

\[f(P,H)=8+4P+6H+8PH\]

Verification by substitution: \(f(0,0)=8\); \(f(1,0)=8+4=12\); \(f(0,1)=8+6=14\); \(f(1,1)=8+4+6+8=26\). Every prototype is reconstructed. Four parameters fitted to four distinct prototypes can interpolate them exactly; such interpolation does not establish generalization or justify causal conclusions.

4. An interaction changes what a coefficient means

Without interactions, the promotion coefficient would imply the same promotion difference regardless of heat. With \(8PH\), the difference depends on the other Feature. Compute it instead of relying on a slogan:

\[f(1,H)-f(0,H)=(8+4+6H+8H)-(8+6H)=4+8H.\]

Thus the promotion difference is 4 kg/day when \(H=0\) and 12 kg/day when \(H=1\). Similarly \(f(P,1)-f(P,0)=6+8P\): heat changes the prediction by 6 kg/day without a promotion but 14 kg/day during a promotion.

Notice that \(\beta_P=4\) is not the universal effect of promotion. The interaction invalidates that interpretation. Likewise, adding a binary Feature's “importance” from the coefficient alone without considering the reference context can be misleading. That is why we need more carefully defined explanation methods.

5. What exactly is being explained?

Before selecting an explanation tool, write down its explanandum: the object we want to explain. Three different targets are commonly confused. (1) A single prediction, such as \(f(1,1)=26\): Why 26 rather than a reference? (2) A global behavior, such as average model dependence on heat over the reference population. (3) Predictive usefulness, such as how badly Test Loss deteriorates when we disrupt a Feature.

These are not identical quantities. A local SHAP value explains a difference between a prediction and a chosen baseline. PDP averages the model while setting one Feature. Permutation Importance measures a change in an evaluation metric under a specific perturbation. None automatically reports whether an intervention would change real-world waste.

Method Quantity answered Units here Important limitation
Coefficients Model's algebraic response under specified other inputs kg/day Scaling and interaction matter
Permutation Importance Increase in held-out Loss after shuffling one Feature kg² for MSE Depends on split, shuffle and dependencies
PDP Mean prediction when a Feature is forcibly set kg/day May evaluate unrealistic combinations
ICE Individual predicted response to a Feature grid kg/day Not an observed causal response
SHAP Attribution relative to a specified reference expectation kg/day Background and dependence assumptions matter

We will calculate each quantity from the same formula, then compare the conclusions precisely.

6. A real evaluation needs observations not used to fit

A prediction explanation is most interesting when the model generalizes. Our four prototypes were used to construct \(f\); we must not call their zero error a Test result. Consider a separate synthetic set of eight days, where the observed waste equals the model prediction plus alternating \(+1\) or \(-1\) kg of invented measurement variation. These eight responses were not used to solve the four coefficients above.

E1 0 0 8 9 +1
E2 0 0 8 7 −1
E3 1 0 12 13 +1
E4 1 0 12 11 −1
E5 0 1 14 15 +1
E6 0 1 14 13 −1
E7 1 1 26 27 +1

Our held-out Mean Squared Error (MSE) averages squared prediction errors. Every residual has magnitude 1 kg, so \(\mathrm{MSE}=(1^2+(-1)^2+1^2+(-1)^2+1^2+(-1)^2+1^2+(-1)^2)/8=8/8=1\ \mathrm{kg}^2\). The corresponding RMSE is \(\sqrt 1=1\) kg; MAE is \(8/8=1\) kg. Since we constructed the labels this way, the numbers are a computational teaching check, not estimates of real retail performance.

7. Permutation Importance: the controlled question

Permutation Importance asks: How much does a fixed model's evaluation Loss deteriorate after we disrupt the alignment between one Feature and its outcomes? The model parameters remain unchanged. The held-out Labels remain unchanged. Only one input column is permuted (reordered across rows), preserving its individual marginal frequency.

For a loss \(L\), define \(I_j=L(\mathbf y,f(\widetilde X^{(j)}))-L(\mathbf y,f(X))\), where \(\widetilde X^{(j)}\) is the original input table with Feature \(j\) shuffled. An importance of 80 \(\mathrm{kg}^2\) means the chosen shuffling experiment raised MSE by 80 squared kilograms, not that the Feature caused 80 kg of waste.

In our balanced eight-day holdout, \(P=(0,0,1,1,0,0,1,1)\) and \(H=(0,0,0,0,1,1,1,1)\). The original model predictions are \((8,8,12,12,14,14,26,26)\). For a simple, reproducible demonstration, we deliberately flip each binary column independently of the other: \(P'=1-P\) or \(H'=1-H\). Those are particular valid permutations because both columns contain four zeros and four ones. Other permutations would produce different numbers; practical Permutation Importance normally averages across many random shuffles.

The chosen holdout is separate from the Training prototypes. Still, repeated feature selection based on this same holdout would turn it into Validation, requiring a new untouched Test set for independent claims.

10. What Permutation Importance cannot identify

An importance score may depend on the loss function. For squared errors a few large mistakes count heavily; with MAE the ranking might be different. It also depends on the holdout distribution, how many shuffles we average, and whether Features can substitute for one another. Two strongly correlated Features can each appear unimportant because the model can exploit the other, or a shuffle can produce impossible combinations and exaggerate reliance.

The simple binary reversal made our calculation transparent. In a real analysis we would use multiple shuffled repetitions on Validation or an appropriately reserved audit set, summarize variability, and avoid repeatedly optimizing the model on the final Test. With correlated columns we might compare grouped permutation (shuffle correlated Features together) or conditional permutation, explicitly changing the question.

Negative importance is possible: a shuffle can accidentally improve a finite-sample score, particularly for noisy or redundant Features. That does not mean the Feature physically prevents waste. Importance near zero may indicate non-use, redundancy, insufficient sample size, or an inappropriate permutation. Therefore report the model, dataset, metric, random seed(s), and perturbation scheme before presenting a ranking as substantive.

11. Partial Dependence Plots: average the model over a fixed grid

A Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) measures the average model prediction when we fix one Feature to a selected value and keep the other observed Features as they were. With background inputs \(x^{(i)}\) and a selected Feature \(j\), the empirical definition is

\[\widehat{PD}_j(z)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(z,x_{-j}^{(i)}).\]

Here \(n\) is the number of reference observations, \(z\) is a chosen Feature level (0 or 1), and \(x_{-j}^{(i)}\) represents all other Features from row \(i\). The unit of PDP is predicted kg/day, not kg²; it is an average prediction, not an average squared error or a causal experiment.

Use the same balanced four-prototype reference with \(H=(0,0,1,1)\) and \(P=(0,1,0,1)\). To calculate the PDP for Promotion at \(z=0\), put \(P=0\) on all four reference rows, giving predictions \((8,8,14,14)\). At \(z=1\), the predictions become \((12,12,26,26)\). We retain the other Feature from each row but do not refit the model. This use of an intervention grid is a mathematical operation on the predictor, not an actual retail promotion experiment.

12. Compute both PDPs and interpret their units

The Promotion PDP at zero is \((8+8+14+14)/4=44/4=11\) kg/day. At one it is \((12+12+26+26)/4=76/4=19\) kg/day. Thus \(\widehat{PD}_P(1)-\widehat{PD}_P(0)=19-11=8\) kg/day.

For heat, hold \(H=0\) on every reference row, retaining \(P=(0,1,0,1)\): predictions \((8,12,8,12)\); their mean is \(40/4=10\). At \(H=1\), the predictions \((14,26,14,26)\) average \(80/4=20\). The Heat PDP difference is \(20-10=10\) kg/day.

Feature PDP at 0 (kg/day) PDP at 1 (kg/day) Average model difference
Promotion 11 19 +8 kg/day
High heat 10 20 +10 kg/day

Verification against interaction algebra: \(f(1,H)-f(0,H)=4+8H\); since background mean \(E(H)=0.5\), the average change equals \(4+8(0.5)=8\). For Heat, average change equals \(6+8E(P)=6+8(0.5)=10\). These match our explicit four-row averages.

13. ICE reveals a pattern the PDP average hides

An Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) curve is the row-specific sequence \(f(z,x_{-j}^{(i)})\) as \(z\) changes. The word expectation in the name does not imply we observed different future outcomes for the same day; each curve is a sequence of model outputs under changed inputs.

For Promotion, a prototype with \(H=0\) has ICE values \(f(0,0)=8\) and \(f(1,0)=12\): difference 4. A prototype with \(H=1\) has values \(f(0,1)=14\) and \(f(1,1)=26\): difference 12. The PDP difference 8 sits exactly between these two, because the balanced reference averages them: \((4+4+12+12)/4=8\).

If an analyst reports only “the average Promotion effect is +8,” a reader may incorrectly expect +8 for every store day. ICE directly contradicts that expectation: the model's dependence changes with heat. In nonlinear models, crossing or diverging ICE curves similarly warn that a single PDP curve can hide substantial heterogeneity.

This does not prove that running a promotion on an actually hot day creates 12 additional kilograms of waste; the model may be confounded by inventory and stocking decisions not observed here.

14. SHAP begins with a coalition, not a mysterious score

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) borrows the Shapley value from cooperative game theory. Imagine that Features join a coalition to “contribute information” about a chosen model prediction. We need a rule \(v(S)\) assigning a model value to each set \(S\) of Features that we hold fixed at the explained observation. Without a definition of this value function and the background distribution of other Features, a SHAP number is incomplete.

For our first computation, the four equally weighted Training prototypes are the reference background. The two Features \(P,H\) are independent in that artificial background: each has probability 0.5 of being 1. We use a marginal-replacement, often called interventional/marginal SHAP, value function:

\[v(S)=E_{Z_{-S}}\!\left[f(x_S,Z_{-S})\right].\]

Here \(x\) is the observation to explain, \(S\) the subset already fixed, and \(Z_{-S}\) the remaining Features sampled from the background marginal distribution. This is an intervention on the prediction function's inputs, not necessarily a causal intervention in the real world. For \(S=\varnothing\), no inputs from \(x\) are fixed: \(v(\varnothing)=E[f(Z)]\). For \(S=\{P,H\}\), both are fixed: \(v(\{P,H\})=f(x)\).

Two Features have only four possible coalitions: \(\varnothing\), \(\{P\}\), \(\{H\}\) and \(\{P,H\}\). We can enumerate every one and derive both SHAP attributions from their marginal contributions.

15. Compute all four coalitions for a hot promotion day

Our target case is \(x=(P=1,H=1)\) and \(f(x)=26\) kg/day. Start with the empty coalition, which is the model's average over the four background prototypes:

\[v(\varnothing)=\frac{f(0,0)+f(1,0)+f(0,1)+f(1,1)}4=\frac{8+12+14+26}{4}=\frac{60}4=15.\]

Now fix only Promotion to 1 while averaging both observed heat levels:

\[v(\{P\})=\tfrac12 f(1,0)+\tfrac12 f(1,1)=\tfrac12(12)+\tfrac12(26)=19.\]

Fix only Heat to 1, averaging both promotion levels:

\[v(\{H\})=\tfrac12 f(0,1)+\tfrac12 f(1,1)=\tfrac12(14)+\tfrac12(26)=20.\]

With both Features known there is no unknown input to average:

\[v(\{P,H\})=f(1,1)=26.\]
Known Features \(S\) Calculation Coalition value (kg/day)
None \((8+12+14+26)/4\) 15
Promotion only \((12+26)/2\) 19
Heat only \((14+26)/2\) 20
Both \(f(1,1)\) 26

These coalition values are the data from which SHAP attribution is constructed. They depend on our chosen background; a different distribution of prototype days will change them.

16. Derive two-feature Shapley weights and the exact explanation

Shapley assigns each Feature its average marginal addition across all orders of the Features. With two Features, there are \(2!=2\) orders, \((P,H)\) and \((H,P)\), each with weight \(1/2\). In the first order, Promotion contributes \(v(\{P\})-v(\varnothing)\); in the second, it contributes \(v(\{P,H\})-v(\{H\})\).

\[\phi_P=\tfrac12[(19-15)+(26-20)]=\tfrac12(4+6)=5\;\mathrm{kg/day}.\]

For Heat, average its contributions when added first and when added after Promotion:

\[\phi_H=\tfrac12[(20-15)+(26-19)]=\tfrac12(5+7)=6\;\mathrm{kg/day}.\]

SHAP satisfies local additivity (efficiency) relative to the defined game:

\[\underbrace{v(\varnothing)}_{15}+\underbrace{\phi_P}_{5}+\underbrace{\phi_H}_{6}=26=f(1,1).\]

The attributions sum to \(26-15=11\) kg/day above the baseline. Importantly, they are not the coefficients \(\beta_P=4\) and \(\beta_H=6\): the nonlinear interaction is distributed across the two players by the Shapley averaging rule. SHAP explanations are tied to both \(f\) and a reference distribution; they should not be read as causes of waste.

17. General Shapley formula and why the factorial appears

For \(d\) Features, let \(N=\{1,\dots,d\}\) and let \(S\) be any subset that excludes Feature \(j\). Its Shapley value is

\[\phi_j=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{j\}}\frac{|S|!\,(d-|S|-1)!}{d!}\left[v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\right].\]

Here \(|S|\) counts the Features entering before \(j\), \(d-|S|-1\) counts those after it, and \(d!\) counts all orderings. For a given subset \(S\), exactly \(|S|!\) arrangements put members of \(S\) before \(j\), and \((d-|S|-1)!\) arrangements put everyone else afterward. The ratio is therefore the fraction of permutations with that predecessor set.

With \(d=2\), both possible \(S\) are \(\varnothing\) and the other singleton. Each coefficient is \(0!1!/2!=1/2\) or \(1!0!/2!=1/2\), reproducing our earlier arithmetic. With \(d=3\), coefficients are \(1/3\) for subsets of size 0 or 2 and \(1/6\) for each singleton subset. For many Features, exact enumeration involves up to \(2^d\) coalitions: model-agnostic sampling methods approximate the game, while specialized methods may exploit tree or linear structure.

Check: Efficiency means \(f(x)=v(\varnothing)+\sum_j\phi_j\) when the same, properly defined game is used throughout. But satisfying this sum alone does not prove that the background, feature-dependence assumption, or explanation is appropriate.

18. Check SHAP on all possible observations, not just the favorable one

A good explanation method should work on every relevant input, not only on a visually appealing positive example. Under our balanced uniform background \(v(\varnothing)=15\), the two-feature SHAP calculation gives the following complete table:

Input \((P,H)\) Prediction (kg/day) \(\phi_P\) (kg/day) \(\phi_H\) (kg/day) Reconstructed
(0,0) 8 −3 −4 \(15-3-4=8\)
(1,0) 12 +3 −6 \(15+3-6=12\)
(0,1) 14 −5 +4 \(15-5+4=14\)
(1,1) 26 +5 +6 \(15+5+6=26\)

Derivation check for (1,0): \(v(\{P\})=(12+26)/2=19\), \(v(\{H\})=(8+12)/2=10\), \(v(\varnothing)=15\), and \(v(\{P,H\})=12\). Then \(\phi_P=[(19-15)+(12-10)]/2=(4+2)/2=3\), whereas \(\phi_H=[(10-15)+(12-19)]/2=(-5-7)/2=-6\).

A negative attribution means that, relative to the chosen reference game, this known Feature pulls the prediction downward. It does not say the Feature itself has a negative physical effect in every circumstance. The signs also change with the particular input: Heat has +6 for (1,1) but −6 for (1,0).

19. Global SHAP summary uses magnitudes, not signed cancellation

A common global SHAP summary takes the mean absolute local attribution \(\mathrm{mean}(|\phi_j|)\) across the evaluation rows. Why absolute? A signed mean can cancel even when a Feature drives many individual predictions upward or downward.

Using the four distinct combinations once each, Promotion's absolute values are \((3,3,5,5)\) kg/day: \((3+3+5+5)/4=16/4=4\) kg/day. Heat's magnitudes are \((4,6,4,6)\): \((4+6+4+6)/4=20/4=5\) kg/day. In the eight-day synthetic holdout, the four patterns each occur twice, so the same means remain 4 and 5.

Method Promotion Heat Correct units
Mean absolute SHAP 4 5 kg/day of attributed model output
Permutation Importance, one flip 80 116 kg² increase in MSE
Absolute PDP difference 8 10 kg/day of average model change

All three happen to rank Heat higher in this example. They remain different questions with different units; we must not conclude that SHAP=5 is “less accurate” than Permutation Importance=116. The values cannot be compared by arithmetic magnitude.

20. SHAP changes when the background population changes

Our original reference assigned equal weight to all four prototypes, giving \(v(\varnothing)=15\). Imagine instead a different synthetic reference population with half the days \((0,0)\) and half \((1,1)\), and no mixed days. The model has not changed, and the explained input remains \((1,1)\) with predicted waste 26. Only the background frequencies change.

The baseline is now \(v(\varnothing)=[8+26]/2=17\). For marginal/interventional SHAP, replace the unknown Feature using its background marginal while holding the known Feature fixed: \(v(\{P\})=[f(1,0)+f(1,1)]/2=(12+26)/2=19\) and \(v(\{H\})=[f(0,1)+f(1,1)]/2=(14+26)/2=20\).

The new attributions are \(\phi_P=[(19-17)+(26-20)]/2=(2+6)/2=4\) kg/day and \(\phi_H=[(20-17)+(26-19)]/2=(3+7)/2=5\) kg/day. Check: \(17+4+5=26\).

Compare with our earlier \(15+5+6=26\). Both decompositions are mathematically valid for their respective backgrounds; neither is an absolute property of the input alone. Background selection is a methodological choice that must be documented, particularly when the business population evolves with seasonality or promotions.

21. Conditional vs. interventional SHAP: a worked correlation paradox

In the second background, Promotion and Heat are perfectly correlated: the only possible historical rows are \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\). Marginal/interventional SHAP still evaluates the model at synthetic combinations \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) when necessary. That does not mean those combinations were observed or could be realized safely.

An alternative defines conditional SHAP using \(v_{\mathrm{cond}}(S)=E[f(X)\mid X_S=x_S]\). For the same target \(x=(1,1)\), observing \(P=1\) already tells us \(H=1\) with certainty in this special background. Thus \(v_{\mathrm{cond}}(\{P\})=26\). Likewise \(v_{\mathrm{cond}}(\{H\})=26\), while \(v_{\mathrm{cond}}(\varnothing)=17\) and \(v_{\mathrm{cond}}(\{P,H\})=26\).

Compute the conditional attributions:

\[\phi_P^{\mathrm{cond}}=\tfrac12[(26-17)+(26-26)]=4.5,\]
\[\phi_H^{\mathrm{cond}}=\tfrac12[(26-17)+(26-26)]=4.5.\]

Their sum again restores the prediction: \(17+4.5+4.5=26\). Contrast with marginal/interventional attributions \(4,5\) under the same correlated background: the two methods distribute information differently. Conditional explanations credit a Feature partly for its information about its correlated partner; marginal replacements ask what the model does when inputs are set independently of that relationship.

Neither answer establishes causality. Which is appropriate depends on whether we are trying to represent the model's functional reliance under input replacements or the information a Feature conveys in the observed population. The dependence structure and purpose must be specified explicitly.

22. LIME and local surrogates: a different kind of local explanation

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) samples nearby or perturbed inputs, asks the original model to predict each, then fits a simpler surrogate—often weighted linear regression—whose predictions approximate the complicated model near one target observation. The key object is not a Shapley average but an optimized local approximation.

A generic weighted surrogate fits parameters \(\theta\) by minimizing \(\sum_i w_i[f(z_i)-g_\theta(z_i)]^2+\lambda\,\Omega(\theta)\). Here \(z_i\) are perturbed points, \(w_i\) are proximity weights, \(g_\theta\) is the interpretable local model, \(\lambda\) tunes regularization and \(\Omega\) penalizes complexity. High local fidelity means the surrogate matches the original model near the point; it does not mean the original predictions match reality.

Our interaction model offers a warning. A purely additive surrogate \(g(P,H)=a+bP+cH\) cannot exactly match all four values \((8,12,14,26)\). The first three points force \(a=8\), \(b=4\), \(c=6\), which would predict \(g(1,1)=18\), not 26: 8 kg/day of missed interaction. A surrogate built tightly around \((1,1)\) can use different weights and coefficients to fit that neighborhood, but its coefficients need not generalize to other input combinations.

A practical LIME audit must document perturbation design, proximity kernel, random seed, surrogate features, fidelity in the relevant neighborhood, and stability across runs. Do not interpret LIME or SHAP attributions as measured interventions.

23. Explanation vs. causation: construct the missing counterfactual

Imagine a high-temperature day where promotions tend to be scheduled only when very large stock deliveries arrive. The dataset omits incoming stock volume. Our model may learn that Promotion and Heat are associated with high waste, even if changing the Promotion indicator alone would not cause any increase. A model-based difference \(f(1,H)-f(0,H)\) is a prediction contrast, not an identified causal effect \(E[Y\mid do(P=1)]-E[Y\mid do(P=0)]\).

A causal claim would require additional design and assumptions: a credible comparison group or random assignment, correct temporal order, no uncontrolled confounding under the chosen identification strategy, and a clear definition of the intervention. Even a formally additive and perfectly efficient SHAP explanation cannot provide these guarantees automatically.

The same distinction matters in healthcare, credit, insurance, education and personnel decisions. An explanation can reveal how a model relies on a proxy for a sensitive characteristic, but it cannot by itself certify fairness, justify denying a service, or guarantee that changing an input will improve outcomes.

Student diagnostic: ask “If the store manager deliberately cancelled the promotion tomorrow but deliveries, pricing and demand also changed, would the predicted change equal the real difference?” We do not know from these synthetic observational inputs. This is the scientifically correct boundary of this example.

24. Build a valid explainability workflow without data leakage

A defensible workflow starts before explaining. First define the prediction time, Target and allowed Features. Split data to match deployment—chronologically for evolving retail demand where appropriate, and grouping stores when deployment must generalize across locations. Fit any scaler, encoder, imputer, Feature selector or predictive model on Training only. Use Validation for model selection, threshold choice and explanation-design decisions; keep final Test protected.

Next choose the explanation target and background. A background used in SHAP should be a documented, representative subset of permitted Training data. For PDP, specify which reference population is averaged. For Permutation Importance, use a held-out evaluation set appropriate for the performance question; repeatedly tuning against it makes it development data. For all tools, decide whether the output is raw prediction, probability, log-odds or something else before interpreting attribution units.

A critical trap: a classifier may expose SHAP explanations of a logit (log-odds) rather than probability. Contributions in log-odds cannot be added directly to a probability percentage. Our regression example avoids this by working consistently in kg/day, but the general protocol must inspect each library's output space.

An explanation chosen after inspecting Test failures to optimize its reported Test narrative is a form of post-selection bias. Separate scientific exploration and final reporting; document any changes and reserve another untouched evaluation when needed. Explanations never repair a model trained with leaked future data.

25. Audit explanations: fidelity, stability and plausibility

Fidelity checks whether a stated explanation actually tracks the model: the SHAP sum must reconstruct \(f(x)\) within numerical tolerance, and a LIME surrogate must approximate the original model in the declared neighborhood. Stability checks whether modest, justified changes to samples, random seeds, reference cohorts or fitting choices cause the explanation to change materially. Plausibility asks whether the explanation uses meaningful Features without unrealistic combinations or unsupported causal interpretations.

For our exact SHAP example, the reconstruction error is \(|26-(15+5+6)|=0\) kg/day. For our simple additive surrogate fitted from the first three prototypes, error at (1,1) is \(|26-18|=8\) kg/day; reporting it as a faithful global explanation would be wrong. Changing from a balanced to a perfectly correlated background shifts the SHAP baseline from 15 to 17 kg/day and alters contributions; this is sensitivity to the reference, not a software bug.

A thorough real-world review also examines subgroup stability and Feature dependency, unexpected jumps, proxy variables, concept drift, adversarial manipulations, data-quality failures, and whether human reviewers understand what an explanation does not support. Store the model version, preprocessing pipeline, explanation method, output scale, background version, code dependencies and audit date.

If explanations disagree, first compare their targets and units. PDP, SHAP and Permutation Importance can disagree legitimately. Look for a methodological mismatch, not automatically a defective algorithm.

26. Reproducible lab I: exact SHAP from all coalitions

The first code block implements the two-feature Shapley formula with only NumPy. It does not call a black-box SHAP function: coalition_value constructs each of the four \(v(S)\) quantities, exact_shap averages their two possible marginal additions, and an assertion checks additive reconstruction. A separate mode implements the conditional expectation using only matching reference rows; it is possible here because our small correlated reference contains the target pattern.

Inputs: the two-feature synthetic model and four independent reference prototypes or two correlated prototypes. Output: reference baseline, Promotion contribution and Heat contribution, all in kg/day. Expected examples: Uniform at (1,1) gives (15,5,6); correlated marginal gives (17,4,5); correlated conditional gives (17,4.5,4.5). These are three distinct games, not three estimates of an observed causal effect.

# Python 3 + NumPy: exact two-feature SHAP without external SHAP library
import numpy as np

def model(P, H):
    return 8 + 4 * P + 6 * H + 8 * P * H

background = np.array([[0, 0], [1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]])
correlated = np.array([[0, 0], [1, 1]])

def coalition_value(x, known, reference, conditional=False):
    rows = reference.copy()
    if conditional and known:
        keep = np.all(rows[:, known] == x[known], axis=1)
        rows = rows[keep]
        if len(rows) == 0:
            raise ValueError("No reference rows for this conditional coalition")
    else:
        for j in known:
            rows[:, j] = x[j]
    return np.mean(model(rows[:, 0], rows[:, 1]))

def exact_shap(x, reference, conditional=False):
    v0 = coalition_value(x, [], reference, conditional)
    vP = coalition_value(x, [0], reference, conditional)
    vH = coalition_value(x, [1], reference, conditional)
    vPH = coalition_value(x, [0, 1], reference, conditional)
    contribution_P = ((vP-v0) + (vPH-vH)) / 2
    contribution_H = ((vH-v0) + (vPH-vP)) / 2
    assert np.isclose(v0 + contribution_P + contribution_H, vPH)
    return v0, contribution_P, contribution_H

for P, H in background:
    base, phi_P, phi_H = exact_shap(np.array([P,H]), background)
    print((P,H), "prediction", model(P,H),
          "base", base, "SHAP", phi_P, phi_H)

x = np.array([1, 1])
print("correlated marginal", exact_shap(x, correlated))
print("correlated conditional", exact_shap(x, correlated, True))
assert exact_shap(x, background) == (15.0, 5.0, 6.0)
assert exact_shap(x, correlated) == (17.0, 4.0, 5.0)
assert exact_shap(x, correlated, True) == (17.0, 4.5, 4.5)

Run the block in an empty Jupyter cell after installing NumPy, or in a Python script. It defines its own imports, data, model and checks. If a future model has many Features, exhaustive enumeration becomes costly and specialized explainers are useful; this exact implementation is a pedagogical ground truth for the two-feature case, not a claim that brute-force computation scales.

27. Reproducible lab II: reconcile Permutation, PDP and ICE

This second standalone block reproduces the full eight-row held-out example and the exact PDP/ICE values. The model stays fixed. To isolate Promotion Importance, the code uses original \(H\) with flipped \(P\). It resets the original \(P\) before changing Heat. This intentionally prevents the common error of cumulatively shuffling both columns in a supposedly single-Feature test.

The printed MSEs must be 1, 81, 117 kg². The two Importance differences are 80 and 116 kg². PDP outputs are Promotion at levels 0/1: 11 and 19 kg/day; Heat at 0/1: 10 and 20 kg/day. ICE for Promotion is 8→12 in low heat and 14→26 in high heat. All reported values are derived from the original source dataset and match the diagrams.

# Python 3 + NumPy: held-out permutation, PDP and ICE
import numpy as np

def predict(P, H):
    return 8 + 4 * P + 6 * H + 8 * P * H

P = np.array([0,0,1,1,0,0,1,1])
H = np.array([0,0,0,0,1,1,1,1])
y = np.array([9,7,13,11,15,13,27,25])

baseline_prediction = predict(P, H)
original_mse = np.mean((y - baseline_prediction) ** 2)
# Deterministic column flips are valid permutations in THIS balanced sample.
promo_changed_prediction = predict(1 - P, H)
heat_changed_prediction = predict(P, 1 - H)
promo_mse = np.mean((y - promo_changed_prediction) ** 2)
heat_mse = np.mean((y - heat_changed_prediction) ** 2)
print("Original MSE", original_mse, "kg^2")
print("Promotion MSE", promo_mse, "importance", promo_mse-original_mse)
print("Heat MSE", heat_mse, "importance", heat_mse-original_mse)
assert (original_mse, promo_mse, heat_mse) == (1.0, 81.0, 117.0)

# PDP: average a model over the original reference distribution.
reference = np.array([[0,0],[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]])
for feature in [0, 1]:
    for level in [0, 1]:
        grid = reference.copy()
        grid[:, feature] = level
        values = predict(grid[:,0], grid[:,1])
        print("PDP", feature, level, float(np.mean(values)), "kg/day")
# ICE for promotion at low heat and high heat.
for heat in [0, 1]:
    print("Promotion ICE, heat", heat, predict(0,heat), predict(1,heat))
assert np.mean(predict(np.zeros(4, dtype=int),reference[:,1])) == 11
assert np.mean(predict(np.ones(4, dtype=int),reference[:,1])) == 19
assert np.mean(predict(reference[:,0],np.zeros(4, dtype=int))) == 10
assert np.mean(predict(reference[:,0],np.ones(4, dtype=int))) == 20

28.1 Calculate the tree's impurity-based Feature Importance

The Training set contains five positive and three negative labels. The root Gini impurity is therefore \(G_{root}=1-(5/8)^2-(3/8)^2=1-25/64-9/64=30/64=0.46875\). Define a node's Gini as \(1-p_0^2-p_1^2\) and a split gain as \(G(parent)-\sum_c (n_c/n_{parent})G(c)\). We use all eight Training records and do not tune on the separate Audit examples below.

Candidate root split on U. For \(U=0\), the four labels consist of three zeros and one one, giving \(G(U=0)=1-(3/4)^2-(1/4)^2=6/16=0.375\). For \(U=1\), all four labels are one, so \(G(U=1)=0\). Weighted child impurity is \((4/8)(0.375)+(4/8)(0)=0.1875\), and the root gain is \(0.46875-0.1875=\mathbf{0.28125}\).

Competing root split on D. For \(D=0\) there are three zeros and two ones, giving \(1-(3/5)^2-(2/5)^2=0.48\). All three observations with \(D=1\) are positive, so their impurity is zero. Weighted impurity is \((5/8)(0.48)+(3/8)(0)=0.30\); gain equals \(0.46875-0.30=\mathbf{0.16875}\). Since 0.28125 exceeds 0.16875, this greedy tree first splits on U.

Within the four Training cases with \(U=0\), splitting on D separates three zeros from one one perfectly, removing that node's remaining 0.375 impurity. Its contribution to the tree-wide impurity reduction is \((4/8)(0.375)=0.1875\). Thus the total reductions are \(R_U=0.28125\) and \(R_D=0.1875\); they sum to the original 0.46875. Dividing by this sum produces the commonly reported normalized Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI): \(MDI_U=0.28125/0.46875=\mathbf{0.60}\) and \(MDI_D=0.1875/0.46875=\mathbf{0.40}\). MDI is a measure of how the fitted tree used its Training splits; it is not a causal effect or an out-of-sample performance gain.

The outcomes are invented to match the pedagogical rule; perfect Audit accuracy is not evidence of real generalization. The loss is simple classification error rate (number wrong divided by four), not MSE.

28.3 Compute local SHAP on a specific credit-risk input

Consider \(x=(U=1,D=0)\), for which the tree returns the positive-class score \(f(x)=1\). We explain the tree's raw leaf positive-class fraction, not a clinically or financially calibrated default probability, a change to anyone's credit standing, or a counterfactual effect. As a transparent explanatory background, choose one of each Feature combination from those available in Training, with equal 1/4 weight. This is an explicitly reweighted educational reference, not the empirical eight-row frequency or a justified production background.

With two Features each of the two possible addition orders receives weight \(1/2\). Scientific and deployment limits. The tiny Training set and constructed Audit labels are not a credit-risk validation study. A real model would require outcome timing checks, leakage and missingness audits, representative independent evaluation, suitable calibration, subgroup and proxy audits, legally appropriate governance and human review. Neither the MDI ranking, the equal Audit permutation changes nor the local SHAP signs establish causal effects or justify approving or denying a loan.

28.4 Reproduce every result with a third standalone Python laboratory

The code below fits the same depth-two tree on Training only, calculates MDI, holds it fixed while flipping one Audit column at a time, then computes the four coalition values and exact two-Feature SHAP without importing SHAP. It checks every numeric statement with assertions. The Audit rows and balanced explanation background have explicitly different roles.

# Python 3 + NumPy + scikit-learn. Synthetic training and audit only.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

# Each row is [high utilization U, recent late payment D].
X_train = np.array([[0, 0]] * 3 + [[0, 1]] +
                   [[1, 0]] * 2 + [[1, 1]] * 2, dtype=int)
y_train = np.array([0] * 3 + [1] + [1] * 2 + [1] * 2)
model = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=31)
model.fit(X_train, y_train)

# Fresh, deliberately balanced synthetic examples; not a performance claim.
X_audit = np.array([[0, 0], [0, 1], [1, 0], [1, 1]])
y_audit = np.array([0, 1, 1, 1])

def error_rate(X):
    return np.mean(model.predict(X) != y_audit)

baseline_error = error_rate(X_audit)
permutation_changes = []
for col in range(2):
    changed = X_audit.copy()
    changed[:, col] = 1 - changed[:, col]  # valid zero/one permutation
    permutation_changes.append(float(error_rate(changed) - baseline_error))

# A four-pattern, deliberately balanced explanatory background
# selected solely from feature combinations present in Training.
background = np.array([[0, 0], [0, 1], [1, 0], [1, 1]])
case = np.array([1, 0])

def predicted_score(rows):
    # Tree's positive-class leaf fraction; NOT a calibrated risk probability.
    return model.predict_proba(np.asarray(rows).reshape(-1, 2))[:, 1]

def coalition_value(known):
    rows = background.copy()
    for index in known:
        rows[:, index] = case[index]
    return float(np.mean(predicted_score(rows)))

v0 = coalition_value([])
vU = coalition_value([0])
vD = coalition_value([1])
vUD = coalition_value([0, 1])
phiU = (vU - v0 + vUD - vD) / 2
phiD = (vD - v0 + vUD - vU) / 2

assert np.allclose(model.feature_importances_, [0.6, 0.4])
assert baseline_error == 0.0
assert np.allclose(permutation_changes, [0.5, 0.5])
assert np.allclose([v0, vU, vD, vUD], [0.75, 1.0, 0.5, 1.0])
assert np.allclose([phiU, phiD], [0.375, -0.125])
assert abs(v0 + phiU + phiD - predicted_score([case])[0]) < 1e-12

print('Impurity importance (U,D):', model.feature_importances_)

29. Operational interpretation: communicate responsibly

A model card or interpretability memo should identify: (1) exact prediction Target and timing; (2) Training, Validation and Test separation; (3) reference dataset and treatment of missing or correlated Features; (4) explanation method and output units; (5) numerical consistency checks; (6) uncertainty or variability when estimated; (7) actions that the explanation does not justify; and (8) monitoring triggers such as seasonal drift.

For our toy prediction, a scientifically accurate statement is: “Under the balanced reference and the specified interaction model, the 26 kg/day estimate is 11 kg/day above the 15 kg/day model baseline. Marginal SHAP allocates +5 to Promotion and +6 to Heat.” A scientifically invalid statement is: “Cancelling Promotion is proven to save exactly 5 kg/day.” The latter confuses attribution, predictive contrast, and causal intervention.

An operations team might use a reliable, validated model to flag days for inventory review, but actual adjustments should account for stock, supplier conditions, temperature forecasts, staff capacity and consequences of wrong warnings. Review important subgroup differences and historical biases. Report the uncertainty of prediction separately from the uncertainty of explanation, especially when the explainer itself is approximate.

This protocol gives explanations a clear place in the Machine Learning workflow: supporting scrutiny, troubleshooting and communication, not substituting for predictive evaluation or responsible operational judgment.

30. Sixteen common mistakes, diagnosed and corrected

Understanding explainability requires learning to reject plausible but false interpretations. Each item below identifies a concrete wrong claim and the calculation, distinction or evaluation protocol that corrects it. These mistakes apply beyond food waste; the same problems arise when interpreting credit, health, logistics or energy models.

Sixteen frequent mistakes and their corrections
MistakeDiagnosis and correction
1. Mistaking explanation for causationSHAP +5 kg is a model-relative attribution; it does not prove changing Promotion changes physical waste by 5 kg. A causal experiment or identification design would be needed.
2. Treating high coefficients as universalThe Promotion coefficient 4 applies at Heat=0. At Heat=1 the predictive difference is 4+8=12; the interaction cannot be ignored.
3. Declaring Training fit to be Test accuracyFour coefficients interpolate four prototypes exactly. Only separate evaluation rows can illustrate generalization, and our held-out rows are still invented.
4. Changing both Features in a one-Feature permutationRestore original other columns for each importance computation; otherwise loss reflects joint disruption rather than the Feature named.
5. Interpreting MSE importance in kilogramsMSE and its increase are kg², while SHAP and PDP of a regression output are kg/day. Numeric magnitudes cannot be compared across units.
6. Treating one shuffle as the unique importanceOur binary flip is only one valid permutation. Repeat shuffles and report variability; changing reference data may change the ranking.
7. Using unrepresentative background as universalA balanced 15 kg baseline and correlated 17 kg baseline are different games, each valid only for its specified reference.
8. Hiding correlations in SHAPConditional and marginal SHAP ask different questions. When P=H in the data, conditional SHAP assigns 4.5 to each, not the same as marginal (4,5).
9. Reading negative SHAP as negative causal effectAt (1,0), heat SHAP is −6 because Heat=0 lowers the prediction relative to the chosen background. It does not describe physical harm or benefit.
10. Substituting PDP for ICEPDP Promotion difference is 8, while ICE differences are 4 and 12. The mean hides meaningful model interactions.
11. Evaluating impossible input combinations without warningMarginal replacements can create (1,0) even when observed data contain only (0,0) and (1,1). Flag support and extrapolation limits.
12. Claiming high LIME fidelity without testingAn additive fit on three prototypes misses the (1,1) value by 8 kg/day. Evaluate surrogate fidelity in its declared neighborhood.
13. Confusing log-odds with probabilityFor classifiers inspect SHAP output units: additive values in logits are not percentages of risk. Explain transformation and baseline.
14. Fitting preprocessing on TestLearn encoders, scaling, imputations and models using Training; protect Test until choices are frozen. Test-aware explanations can cause selection bias.
15. Inferring stable rankings from tiny dataEight synthetic holdout rows and one shuffle do not justify confidence intervals, subgroup robustness or industry deployment claims.
16. Using explainability as a safety certificateAn interpretable model can still be inaccurate, biased, manipulated or causally misused. Separately audit outcomes, validity and human oversight.

The most dangerous errors are semantic rather than computational: a SHAP sum may be perfectly correct while the author claims causation, ignores the background, reports the wrong output scale, or uses Test repeatedly for selection. An academic explanation must satisfy both mathematical reconstruction and scientifically valid interpretation.

Attempt each problem independently, then expand the answer. The sequence tests definitions → full arithmetic → interpretability comparisons → background sensitivity → transfer . Problems 17–19 transfer these concepts to the MDI, Permutation and SHAP credit-risk comparison.

1. Derive the intercept from T1.

At P=H=0, the interaction model is f(0,0)=β0=8 kg/day. No other coefficient contributes because all indicator products are zero.

2. Derive all remaining coefficients from T2–T4.

βP=12−8=4, βH=14−8=6, βPH=26−8−4−6=8 kg/day. Substitute the values into all four prototypes.

3. Compute the Promotion contrast with and without Heat.

f(1,H)−f(0,H)=4+8H, hence 4 kg/day at H=0 and 12 kg/day at H=1. This is model dependence, not proven causal impact.

4. Compute the holdout MSE, RMSE and MAE.

All eight errors are ±1 kg. MSE=(8×1²)/8=1 kg²; RMSE=√1=1 kg; MAE=(8×1)/8=1 kg. Contrived numbers are not industrial evaluation.

5. Why is the Promotion permutation error E5 equal to 121?

E5 originally has P=0,H=1,y=15. After reversing P to 1 the fixed model predicts 26; (15−26)²=(−11)²=121 kg².

6. Calculate Promotion Permutation Importance.

The eight permuted squared errors total 648 kg², so MSE=648/8=81 kg²; original MSE=1; Importance=81−1=80 kg².

7. Calculate Heat Permutation Importance.

The eight heat-permuted squared errors total 936 kg², giving MSE=936/8=117 kg²; Importance=117−1=116 kg².

8. Calculate Promotion PDP at zero and one.

At P=0, predictions 8,8,14,14 average 44/4=11 kg/day. At P=1, 12,12,26,26 average 76/4=19. Difference is 8 kg/day.

9. Reconstruct Heat PDP and its interpretation.

At H=0, 8,12,8,12 average 10; at H=1, 14,26,14,26 average 20 kg/day; difference +10 is a model average, not a causal claim.

10. Derive all coalition values for (1,1).

v(∅)=60/4=15; v(P)=(12+26)/2=19; v(H)=(14+26)/2=20; v(P,H)=26, all measured in predicted kg/day.

11. Derive the two SHAP contributions with both possible Feature orders.

Promotion=.5[(19−15)+(26−20)]=5; Heat=.5[(20−15)+(26−19)]=6. Baseline 15+5+6=26 kg/day.

12. Calculate SHAP at (1,0) and check its sign.

v(∅)=15, v(P)=19, v(H=0)=10, f(1,0)=12. Promotion=.5[(19−15)+(12−10)]=3; Heat=.5[(10−15)+(12−19)]=−6; reconstruction 15+3−6=12.

13. Calculate mean absolute SHAP for each Feature over all four patterns.

Promotion mean |φ|=(3+3+5+5)/4=4 kg/day; Heat mean |φ|=(4+6+4+6)/4=5 kg/day. Do not compare these units to MSE importance.

14. Switch to the perfectly correlated reference: calculate marginal SHAP.

Background (0,0),(1,1) gives baseline 17, v(P)=19, v(H)=20, v(P,H)=26. Marginal attributions are Promotion 4 and Heat 5; 17+4+5=26.

15. Calculate conditional SHAP under perfect correlation.

Given P=1, H=1 with certainty, and vice versa. v(P)=v(H)=26, baseline=17; each conditional Shapley contribution is .5[(26−17)+(26−26)]=4.5; sum=26.

16. Transfer: why can an additive LIME surrogate miss the high-heat promotion day?

Three prototype constraints force a=8,b=4,c=6 in g=a+bP+cH; hence g(1,1)=18 while original f(1,1)=26. Missing interaction produces 8 kg/day error; use a suitable local model or document its limited fidelity.

Self-check: can you explain why a 5 kg/day local SHAP contribution, an 8 kg/day PDP contrast and an 80 kg² Permutation Importance can all be simultaneously correct without measuring the same thing? A complete answer identifies their different reference questions, averaging operations, units and limitations.

17. Verify the two Training split gains in the credit-risk tree.

The root has five positives and three negatives: Gini = 2(5/8)(3/8) = 0.46875. For U, the weighted child Gini is (4/8)(0.375) + (4/8)(0) = 0.1875, giving gain 0.28125. For D, the child impurity is (5/8)(0.48) + (3/8)(0) = 0.30, giving gain 0.16875. The tree selects U for the root. The subsequent D split of U=0 contributes (4/8)(0.375) = 0.1875. Normalizing reductions gives 0.60 and 0.40, not causal percentages.

18. Recompute both Audit permutation importances without retraining.

The four original predictions 0,1,1,1 equal the four Audit labels, so baseline error = 0/4 = 0. Flip only U: predicted labels 1,1,0,1, wrong at A1 and A3, so error = 2/4 = 0.50 and importance = 0.50. Restore U and flip only D: predictions 1,0,1,1, wrong at A1 and A2, so error and importance again equal 0.50. This tie differs from the 60/40 Training Gini ranking because the two metrics answer different questions.

19. Calculate local SHAP and identify an invalid lending conclusion.

For U=1,D=0 and an equally weighted four-pattern background, v(empty)=0.75, v(U)=1, v(D)=0.5 and v(U,D)=1. Therefore phi_U=[(1−0.75)+(1−0.5)]/2=+0.375 and phi_D=[(0.5−0.75)+(1−1)]/2=−0.125. Sum: 0.75+0.375−0.125=1. Claiming “utilization causes a 37.5 percentage-point increase in true default probability” is invalid: the quantity is an attribution of an uncalibrated fitted tree score relative to a chosen reference, not an identified causal or calibrated probability effect.

33. Synthesis: what should an independent learner now be able to prove?

We began with a fully visible four-prototype dataset and obtained \(f(P,H)=8+4P+6H+8PH\) by solving every coefficient. We verified it on the prototypes and used a separate synthetic eight-row holdout to calculate an original MSE of 1 kg². One valid controlled permutation of Promotion raised MSE to 81 and of Heat to 117 kg², teaching model reliance measured in squared-error units, not physical causation.

We constructed exact PDP values—Promotion 11→19 and Heat 10→20 kg/day—and used ICE to reveal that Promotion's model contrast is 4 without Heat but 12 with Heat. Then we enumerated all four coalition values for a high-heat promotion day, obtaining reference 15 and exact SHAP contributions +5 and +6. We proved that a different background gives baseline 17 and marginal attributions 4 and 5, while a perfectly correlated conditional game gives 4.5 and 4.5. Each case reconstructs 26 but answers a different explanatory question.

The essential transferable method is: define the prediction and outcome scale → choose a scientifically defensible evaluation set and reference population → derive the mathematical quantity → verify calculations and reconstruction → test sensitivity and fidelity → interpret what the method establishes and what it cannot. That method is useful whether the next model is Linear Regression, XGBoost or a Neural Network. It does not turn an explanation into causal proof or substitute for external validation.
