1. Why reduce dimensions when no label is supplied?

An industrial or environmental monitoring system may record dozens of sensors per station. Some measurements rise together, some measure different phenomena, and some are noisy. Dimensionality Reduction builds fewer coordinates that summarize a chosen notion of information. It is not the same as Feature Selection: selecting Features retains original columns, whereas extracting components combines columns mathematically.

In Guide 24, Clustering assigned groups to observations. Here we ask a different question: can we describe observations with fewer numerical coordinates before visualizing them, compressing them, or possibly training another model? We will solve PCA from the data upward and then contrast its objective with the fundamentally different visual aims of t-SNE and UMAP.

An important warning: high explained variance does not prove that a representation preserves the rare signals relevant to faults, health outcomes or safety. We will calculate a counterexample, not merely assert it.

2. Learning objectives and the mathematics we need

By the end you should be able to center a dataset, calculate a Covariance Matrix, derive two Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors, project and reconstruct observations, compute Explained Variance and Reconstruction Error, and describe when PCA is unsuitable. You should also be able to explain why t-SNE and UMAP plots must not be read as ordinary distance maps or causal explanations.

Prerequisites: arithmetic, averages, squared differences and a two-dimensional vector. A dot product multiplies corresponding coordinates and adds them: \((a,b)\cdot(c,d)=ac+bd\). A unit vector has length one; its squared coordinates sum to one. We will derive unfamiliar operations as they arise.

Our path is: raw table → centering → covariance → eigenproblem → PCA scores → reconstruction → choice of components → training-only fitting → nonlinear visualization → implementation and exercises.

Additional reproducible outcomes: calculate both PC Whitening scales and independently confirm unit sample covariance; compute t-SNE conditional similarities from squared distances; distinguish these intermediate probabilities from a final nonlinear embedding.

4. Centering: subtract the Training mean before PCA

To center a Feature, subtract its mean. This moves the origin to the average observation without changing relative distances. We center because the direction of maximum variability should describe departures from the average, not simply point toward a large numerical origin.

\[\bar A=\frac{13+7+12+8+11+9}{6}=10,\qquad \bar B=\frac{13+7+12+8+9+11}{6}=10\]

Thus S1 becomes \((13-10,13-10)=(3,3)\); S5 becomes \((11-10,9-10)=(1,-1)\). The complete centered array is below. Check that each column sums to zero: \(3-3+2-2+1-1=0\) and \(3-3+2-2-1+1=0\).

\[Z=X-\mathbf{1}\mu^\top=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\-3&-3\\2&2\\-2&-2\\1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix},\quad Z\in\mathbb R^{6\times2}\]

Here \(X\) is a 6×2 data matrix; \(\mu=(10,10)^\top\) is a 2×1 mean vector; \(\mathbf1\) has six ones. The centered matrix \(Z\) also has six rows and two columns.

A quick check before continuing

Why not divide every centered reading by the mean? Because subtraction and division answer different questions. Centering measures a deviation from the average in the original index-point units. Standardization additionally expresses that deviation in units of Training standard deviations. PCA always needs a clear reference for the mean, but whether it should also use standardized Features depends on the meaning and comparability of the original units.

Look at S2: its deviation is (-3,-3). That does not mean the original sensors measured negative physical readings; they measured (7,7), three index points below the Training mean. Keeping this distinction prevents the later graph from being interpreted as impossible negative sensor values.

5. Covariance Matrix: reconstruct every entry from data

Variance measures how much one variable fluctuates; Covariance measures how two variables move together. For six observations, the sample covariance divides by \(n-1=5\). This is the convention used by scikit-learn’s PCA when reporting `explained_variance_`. Positive covariance indicates that larger-than-average A often accompanies larger-than-average B, not that A causes B.

\[S=\frac{1}{n-1}Z^\top Z,\qquad n=6\]

For the A variance, sum squared centered values: \(3^2+(-3)^2+2^2+(-2)^2+1^2+(-1)^2=9+9+4+4+1+1=28\). The B column has the same squared sum. For the cross-product, compute \(3(3)+(-3)(-3)+2(2)+(-2)(-2)+1(-1)+(-1)(1)=9+9+4+4-1-1=24\).

\[Z^\top Z=\begin{bmatrix}28&24\\24&28\end{bmatrix},\qquad S=\frac15\begin{bmatrix}28&24\\24&28\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5.6&4.8\\4.8&5.6\end{bmatrix}\]

Every entry has units of index points squared; the covariance is symmetric because the product \((A_i-\bar A)(B_i-\bar B)\) is the same in either order. A population-form calculation would divide by six, producing a different scale but the same PCA directions and percentage variance for this dataset.

What does the large positive cross-entry tell us?

The off-diagonal entry 4.8 is relatively large compared with either variance 5.6. That happens because the four jointly moving windows contribute positive cross-products totaling 26, whereas the two contrasting windows contribute only -2. In other words, the majority pattern dominates the average. The corresponding Pearson Correlation would be 4.8/√(5.6×5.6)=4.8/5.6≈0.8571. Correlation is dimensionless, unlike Covariance, and it is not a separate assumption: it is simply Covariance normalized by both standard deviations.

6. An eigenvector is a direction that does not rotate

A matrix can rotate, stretch or combine directions. An Eigenvector \(v\ne0\) of a square matrix is special because applying the matrix only stretches or reverses that direction: \(Sv=\lambda v\). The scalar \(\lambda\) is its Eigenvalue. For symmetric Covariance matrices, we can choose orthogonal unit Eigenvectors; these become Principal Component axes.

The largest Eigenvalue identifies the direction with greatest sample variance. This is not a free-standing theorem to memorize: the variance of data projected onto a unit direction \(v\) is \(v^\top Sv\). When \(Sv=\lambda v\), that variance becomes \(v^\top(\lambda v)=\lambda(v^\top v)=\lambda\), because the vector has unit length.

\[\operatorname{Var}_{\text{sample}}(Zv)=v^\top Sv,\qquad \|v\|^2=v^\top v=1\]

Notice that “direction” here means the orientation of a line through the centered origin. A Principal Component does not represent an original physical sensor; it is a weighted combination of both sensors.

7. Solve the characteristic equation without skipping algebra

We need the Eigenvalues of \(S\). Starting from \(Sv=\lambda v\), rearrange to \((S-\lambda I)v=0\), where \(I\) is the 2×2 identity matrix. A nonzero solution exists only if \(\det(S-\lambda I)=0\). For a 2×2 matrix, the determinant is \(ad-bc\).

\[\det\!\begin{bmatrix}5.6-\lambda&4.8\\4.8&5.6-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(5.6-\lambda)^2-4.8^2=0\]

Expand: \(31.36-11.2\lambda+\lambda^2-23.04=0\). Combine constants: \(\lambda^2-11.2\lambda+8.32=0\). Factor: \((\lambda-10.4)(\lambda-0.8)=0\). Therefore the Eigenvalues are 10.4 and 0.8 index points².

\[\boxed{\lambda_1=10.4,\qquad\lambda_2=0.8}\]

Verification: the Eigenvalues add to \(11.2\), equal to the trace \(5.6+5.6\), and multiply to \(8.32\), equal to \(5.6^2-4.8^2\). Both are nonnegative, as Covariance eigenvalues must be.

Why exactly two solutions?

A 2×2 Covariance Matrix has a second-degree characteristic polynomial, so there are two Eigenvalues counting multiplicities. Here we can solve even more directly: (5.6−λ)²=4.8² implies either 5.6−λ=4.8 or 5.6−λ=−4.8. The first equation gives λ=0.8; the second gives λ=10.4. We order the larger one first because PCA calls it PC1. The two values describe the spread along two perpendicular axes, not the number of meaningful clusters in our data.

8. Solve and normalize both eigenvectors

For \(\lambda_1=10.4\), substitute into \((S-\lambda I)v=0\). The first row gives \((-4.8)v_A+4.8v_B=0\), so \(v_A=v_B\). Choose \((1,1)\), whose length is \(\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2\). Divide both coordinates by \(\sqrt2\).

\[v_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad Sv_1=10.4v_1\]

For \(\lambda_2=0.8\), the first row becomes \(4.8v_A+4.8v_B=0\), hence \(v_B=-v_A\). Normalize \((1,-1)\) in the same way. These vectors are perpendicular because their dot product is \((1-1)/2=0\).

\[v_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad Sv_2=0.8v_2\]

A software library might return \(-v_1\) instead of \(v_1\). That flips all first-component scores but represents exactly the same one-dimensional axis and reconstruction; Eigenvector signs have no intrinsic physical meaning.

Why normalization is essential

The vector (100,100) satisfies the same eigen-direction relation as (1,1), but its numerical coordinates are one hundred times larger. If we treated that longer vector as a projection direction without normalizing, the resulting Scores would be arbitrarily scaled and the usual explained-variance interpretation would no longer follow directly. Unit length makes the comparison meaningful. PC1’s equal positive Loadings mean that the component measures a common increase or decrease of both sensors; PC2’s opposing signs measure their disagreement. This interpretation remains descriptive, not causal.

9. Project every observation onto PC1 and PC2

A Principal Component Score is a coordinate along an Eigenvector. For a centered row \(z_i=(a_i,b_i)\), the scores are the two dot products: \(t_{i1}=z_i\cdot v_1=(a_i+b_i)/\sqrt2\) and \(t_{i2}=z_i\cdot v_2=(a_i-b_i)/\sqrt2\).

\[t_{i1}=\frac{a_i+b_i}{\sqrt2},\qquad t_{i2}=\frac{a_i-b_i}{\sqrt2}\]

For S1, \((3,3)\) becomes \((6/\sqrt2,0)=(4.2426,0)\). For S5, \((1,-1)\) becomes \((0,2/\sqrt2)=(0,1.4142)\). Every score follows from the same two operations:

As a matrix operation, let \(V=[v_1\ v_2]\in\mathbb R^{2\times2}\). Then \(T=ZV\in\mathbb R^{6\times2}\). Keeping only the first component means \(T_1=Zv_1\in\mathbb R^{6\times1}\), not deleting one of the original sensor columns.

Interpreting a Score in its original units

A PC1 Score of +4.2426 is not “4.2426 sensors” or “4.2426 percent.” It is a coordinate measured in the same index-point units as the original two calibrated readings, because each direction vector is dimensionless and has length one. Its positive sign means a displacement along the selected PC1 orientation. If software reversed the Eigenvector sign, exactly the same observation would score −4.2426 without changing its relative location along the axis. That is why interpreting a component requires looking at its Loadings and not only the Score.

10. Reconstruct the data: where does discarded information go?

PCA with one component keeps each observation’s location along \(v_1\), but sets its perpendicular PC2 score to zero. To reconstruct the original units, multiply the retained score by \(v_1^\top\) and add the Training mean: \(\hat x_i=\mu+t_{i1}v_1^\top\). This is an approximation unless PC2 was already zero.

\[\hat X=\mathbf1\mu^\top+T_1v_1^\top\]

For S1, \(t_{11}=3\sqrt2\). Multiply \(3\sqrt2(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)=(3,3)\), then add \((10,10)\) to recover \((13,13)\): no error. For S5, PC1 score is zero, so the reconstruction is \((10,10)\), although the original was \((11,9)\). Its residual is \((1,-1)\) and squared reconstruction error is \(1^2+(-1)^2=2\). S6 contributes another 2; all others contribute zero.

\[\operatorname{SSE}_{k=1}=0+0+0+0+2+2=\boxed4\;\text{index points}^2\]

The overall PCA reconstruction may look impressive, yet it completely removes the opposing-sensor signal in S5 and S6. This is why a fault detector should not rely on variance retention alone.

11. Explained Variance Ratio: derive 92.86%, not a slogan

The Eigenvalues are the sample variances of the Principal Component Scores. The total variance is the sum of Eigenvalues, which also equals the trace of the Covariance Matrix: \(10.4+0.8=11.2\) index points². PCA explains a fraction of this total with each retained axis.

\[\operatorname{EVR}_1=\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}=\frac{10.4}{11.2}=\frac{13}{14}=0.928571\approx92.86\%\]

The second component explains \(0.8/11.2=1/14\approx7.14\%\). Verify the same result using sum of squared centered distances: \(\sum_i\|z_i\|^2=28+28=56\); the captured sum of squares along PC1 is \(52\), leaving \(4\). Therefore \(52/56=13/14\). This equality ties the covariance viewpoint to the reconstruction viewpoint.

Explained variance is a property of variation in the measured Features, not a probability of correct predictions and not a percentage of “meaning” or “information relevant to the business.”

12. Why PCA maximizes variance: derive the optimization condition

PCA is not merely an eigen-decomposition recipe. It chooses a unit direction \(v\) to maximize the variance of projected data. The unit-length requirement prevents multiplying a direction by 100 to artificially increase variance. The constrained optimization problem is:

\[\max_{v\in\mathbb R^2}\;v^\top Sv\quad\text{subject to }v^\top v=1\]

Introduce a Lagrange Multiplier \(\lambda\) to enforce the constraint: \(\mathcal L(v,\lambda)=v^\top Sv-\lambda(v^\top v-1)\). For symmetric \(S\), the derivative with respect to \(v\) is \(2Sv-2\lambda v\). Set it to zero at an interior stationary point:

\[\nabla_v\mathcal L=2Sv-2\lambda v=0\ \Longrightarrow\ Sv=\lambda v\]

This is precisely the Eigenvector equation. Substituting back, the achieved variance is \(v^\top Sv=\lambda\); therefore the largest Eigenvalue gives the maximum projected variance. Subsequent components maximize remaining variance subject to being perpendicular to earlier axes. For our 2D example, this produces PC1 along \((1,1)\) and PC2 along \((1,-1)\).

One important scope condition: this derivation assumes centered numerical data and an ordinary Euclidean inner product; transforming the units or introducing weights changes the optimization problem.

Connecting the stationary condition to a maximum

The Lagrange equation identifies candidate directions, but a stationary equation alone does not establish which candidate is maximal. In this symmetric two-dimensional example, the candidates are PC1 (variance 10.4) and PC2 (variance 0.8). Comparing the objective value at both directions selects 10.4 as the maximum and 0.8 as the minimum. This second check completes the optimization argument. The same reasoning extends to higher-dimensional symmetric Covariance matrices: the Rayleigh quotient reaches its maximum at a principal Eigenvector.

13. Why PCA is also the best linear low-rank reconstruction

A second interpretation is geometrically powerful: among all one-dimensional linear subspaces through the centered origin, PCA chooses the line minimizing total squared perpendicular reconstruction distance. Because every centered vector decomposes into a projection plus a perpendicular residual, the Pythagorean identity gives \(\|z_i\|^2=\|\hat z_i\|^2+\|z_i-\hat z_i\|^2\).

\[\sum_i\|z_i-\hat z_i\|^2=\sum_i\|z_i\|^2-\sum_i\|\hat z_i\|^2\]

The first term is fixed by the data (56). Maximizing captured squared length is therefore equivalent to minimizing reconstruction SSE. With no retained component, reconstruction is the mean for everyone and SSE is 56. With one PCA component it is 4; with both components it is zero (to floating-point precision).

The word “linear” matters. PCA is optimal for this particular squared-distance projection objective. It is not guaranteed optimal for preserving nonlinear geometry, labels, rare signals or downstream prediction.

14. PCA, Singular Value Decomposition and dimensions

For larger datasets, software generally does not expand and solve characteristic polynomials by hand. One stable route uses Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), \(Z=U\Sigma V^\top\). Here \(Z\) is 6×2, \(U\) contains left singular directions, \(\Sigma\) holds nonnegative Singular Values, and columns of \(V\) are the PCA directions.

Why is SVD connected to Covariance? Multiply the decomposition: \(Z^\top Z=V\Sigma^\top\Sigma V^\top\). Therefore each covariance Eigenvalue equals a squared Singular Value divided by \(n-1\):

\[\lambda_j=\frac{\sigma_j^2}{n-1},\qquad \sigma_1=\sqrt{5(10.4)}=\sqrt{52}\approx7.2111\]
\[\sigma_2=\sqrt{5(0.8)}=\sqrt4=2\]

This verifies the earlier decomposition without changing its meaning. The 6×2 score matrix has entries \(T=ZV=U\Sigma\). If we retain one column of \(V\), then the reduced score matrix is 6×1. SVD has sign ambiguities, like PCA; these do not affect retained subspaces.

If the original Features are linearly redundant, some Singular Values are zero; PCA cannot extract more nonzero-variance components than the centered matrix rank. This matters when the number of Features exceeds the number of observations.

PCA Whitening: a second transformation, not a different component

So far a PC1 score measures displacement in the original sensor-index units: its sample variance is 10.4 index points², while PC2 has variance 0.8 index points². Whitening divides each retained score by the Training standard deviation of that component so that retained directions have sample variance one. Unlike selecting a different eigenvector, this rescales existing axes. It can be useful for later distance-sensitive algorithms, but it changes distances and amplifies low-variance coordinates.

The Training Eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1=10.4\) and \(\lambda_2=0.8\), both strictly positive. Define for any centered row \(z_i\) the scores \(t_{ij}=z_i\cdot v_j\). The whitened coordinates are:

\[w_{ij}=\frac{t_{ij}}{\sqrt{\lambda_j}},\qquad \sqrt{\lambda_1}\approx3.224903,\quad\sqrt{\lambda_2}\approx0.894427\]

For S1, the scores are \((3\sqrt2,0)\), so \(w_{11}=3\sqrt2/\sqrt{10.4}\approx1.315587\) and \(w_{12}=0\). For S5, scores are \((0,\sqrt2)\), hence \(w_{51}=0\), \(w_{52}=\sqrt2/\sqrt{0.8}=\sqrt{2.5}\approx1.581139\). All six rows, computed by the same divisions, appear below.

Verify the variance claim, do not assume it: in PC coordinates the sample covariance is \(\operatorname{diag}(10.4,0.8)\). Let \(D=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{10.4},\sqrt{0.8})\) and \(W=TD^{-1}\). The whitened sample covariance is:

\[\frac{W^\top W}{n-1}=D^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}10.4&0\\0&0.8\end{bmatrix}D^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.\]

For an independent check from the table: \(\sum_i t_{i1}^2=52\), so \(\sum_i w_{i1}^2/(6-1)=52/(10.4\cdot5)=1\); likewise PC2 contributes \(4/(0.8\cdot5)=1\). Cross-products cancel. Here both components are retained, so the full 2×2 covariance is the identity; if only PC1 is retained, only that one whitened coordinate has unit variance. A zero Eigenvalue cannot be divided out; software must exclude or regularize such a direction.

Frozen transform versus refitting: a new Validation reading \((14,12)\) uses the Training mean and axes. PC1 is \(6/\sqrt2\), so its whitened coordinate is \((6/\sqrt2)/\sqrt{10.4}\approx1.315587\). Never recompute the denominator with Validation. To reconstruct from whitened scores, first multiply by the corresponding Training standard deviations, then apply the inverse PCA rotation and add the Training mean. Whitening does not restore a component discarded by dimensionality reduction.

15. Scaling can rotate PC1: same data, new units

PCA uses numerical variance, so it is sensitive to units. Suppose Sensor B is expressed using a unit ten times larger: \(B^*=10B\), while A stays unchanged. This conversion does not alter the underlying physical situations, but it changes the Covariance Matrix and therefore the Principal Components:

\[S^*=\begin{bmatrix}5.6&48\\48&560\end{bmatrix}\]

The B variance becomes \(100(5.6)=560\), while the cross-covariance becomes \(10(4.8)=48\). The leading component now points mostly along the rescaled B axis. This is not “wrong” mathematically—it solves a different variance-maximization problem—but it may be scientifically arbitrary if the factor of ten reflects only a unit convention.

A common response is `StandardScaler`, which subtracts each Training mean and divides by each Training standard deviation. This makes both variances equal to one, so PCA acts on standardized variation (equivalently, the Correlation Matrix). However, standardizing is a modeling choice, not a rule for every dataset: meaningful physical amplitudes can be lost.

In our two-sensor case, standardized Covariance has a symmetric form and the principal directions stay at 45 degrees. That coincidence is caused by the equal original variances; do not generalize it to arbitrary datasets.

16. How many components? Scree, thresholds and purpose

A Scree Plot displays each Eigenvalue or Explained Variance Ratio in descending order. Some workflows select the smallest \(k\) reaching a target cumulative variance; others evaluate downstream Reconstruction Error or Validation performance. Here a 90% target retains one component because \(92.857\%\ge90\%\). A 95% target needs two: \(92.857\%\lt95\%\).

\[\operatorname{CumulativeEVR}(k)=\frac{\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_k}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_j}\]

No universal 90% or 95% rule follows from PCA theory. The cost of discarding small components depends on whether the goal is compression, visualization, anomaly detection or predicting a Target. In an anomaly application, a low-variance sensor disagreement can be exactly the phenomenon of interest.

A crucial distinction: selecting \(k\) based on a fixed, prespecified variance threshold is unsupervised, but the PCA basis still must be fitted on Training in a held-out workflow. If you compare multiple values of \(k\) using labeled Validation performance, that choice is Hyperparameter Tuning and must stay within the validation procedure.

17. Fit on Training, transform Validation and Test

The `fit()` step of PCA learns at least a Training mean and a set of component directions. `transform()` does not recompute them; it uses those frozen settings for new rows. For our six-record teaching Training set, the stored mean is \((10,10)\) and the first axis is \((1,1)/\sqrt2\). Suppose a new Validation reading is \((14,12)\).

\[z_{\text{new}}=(14,12)-(10,10)=(4,2)\]
\[t_{\text{new},1}=(4,2)\cdot\frac{(1,1)}{\sqrt2}=\frac6{\sqrt2}\approx4.2426\]

The reconstruction using one retained component is \((10,10)+4.2426(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)=(13,13)\). Its residual is \((1,-1)\) and squared reconstruction error equals 2 index points². These results use only the original fitted mean and direction.

What would go wrong if we fitted the mean after adding Validation? The A mean would become \((60+14)/7=10.5714\), and B mean \((60+12)/7=10.2857\), so even the centering reference changes. The PCA axis might change as well. That allows evaluation data to influence preprocessing—Data Leakage for an ordinary held-out test.

A reliable Pipeline calls `fit()` only on the fitting partition of each Cross-Validation fold, while held-out rows receive `transform()` with frozen settings. PCA itself does not use labels, but it does learn from the Feature distribution.

18. PCA is not automatically useful for predicting a Target

The PCA objective maximizes variation of the Inputs \(X\); it never asks which direction predicts an outcome \(Y\). Imagine that a maintenance team later learns the two contrasting windows S5 and S6 were sensor calibration failures. Their distinguishing contrast lies entirely in PC2, which explains only 7.14% of the overall variance. A one-component PCA representation gives both Score zero, potentially hiding the signal.

This does not show that PC2 is always useful or that S5/S6 actually are failures—they were unlabeled synthetic records. It demonstrates a possible mismatch between unsupervised variance and supervised utility. To test utility, we would need credible failure labels and an evaluation that learns PCA and any Scaling inside each Training fold, then measures performance on held-out labels.

PCA can help reduce collinearity, compress input, speed some models or stabilize estimation, but can hurt interpretability, remove low-variance predictive signals, or be unnecessary for models such as many Decision Trees. Always compare with a baseline using the original Features and consistent validation.

19. Distance geometry before and after projection

Reducing from two coordinates to one must discard some geometric information unless all points lie on one line. For instance, S5 is \((11,9)\) and S6 is \((9,11)\). Their original Euclidean distance is \(\sqrt{(11-9)^2+(9-11)^2}=\sqrt8\approx2.8284\) index points.

\[d_{\text{original}}(S5,S6)=\sqrt{4+4}=2\sqrt2\approx2.8284\]

But both have PC1 score zero, so the projected one-dimensional distance is \(|0-0|=0\). This is not a bug: the two points differ only along discarded PC2. Conversely, distances between S1 and S3, both lying exactly on PC1, remain unchanged: \(\sqrt{(13-12)^2+(13-12)^2}=\sqrt2\approx1.4142\), and \(|4.2426-2.8284|\approx1.4142\).

Therefore “PCA preserves distances” is generally false after dropping components. It preserves orthogonal projection geometry and optimizes aggregate squared reconstruction, not every pairwise distance. For clustering after PCA, changing neighborhoods can change assignments, especially of rare or boundary observations.

20. t-SNE: preserve neighbors for exploratory visualization

t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) is commonly used to visualize high-dimensional points in two dimensions. Instead of maximizing linear variance, it first constructs similarities between pairs of original observations, assigning larger probabilities to nearby neighbors. It then searches for map coordinates whose neighborhood probabilities resemble those relationships.

Conceptually, the loss is Kullback–Leibler (KL) Divergence between the original pairwise similarity distribution \(P\) and the map distribution \(Q\):

\[D_{KL}(P\|Q)=\sum_{i\ne j}p_{ij}\log\left(\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}\right)\]

Here \(p_{ij}\) measures similarity in original space and \(q_{ij}\) similarity on the map; both are normalized pairwise probability distributions. Minimizing the expression strongly penalizes placing originally close pairs far apart. t-SNE uses a heavy-tailed Student-t similarity in the map so moderately distant points can separate instead of crowding together.

The perplexity parameter influences effective neighborhood scale; it is not the number of clusters. Different seeds, perplexities or samples can change a plot. Axes have arbitrary orientations and units: large blank spaces between colored clouds do not establish that their original populations are equally far apart.

One tiny calculation to interpret KL Divergence

Before applying t-SNE to many pairwise relationships, consider a simplified two-outcome probability comparison: P=(0.8,0.2), Q=(0.5,0.5). Its KL Divergence is 0.8×ln(0.8/0.5)+0.2×ln(0.2/0.5)=0.8×0.470004+0.2×(−0.916291)≈0.192745 nats. If Q=P, both log-ratios become ln(1)=0, so KL=0. These two numbers are a teaching analogy for how unequal similarity distributions incur loss; real t-SNE uses many pairwise probabilities with additional symmetry and normalization details.

Numerical neighbor probabilities: derive one t-SNE input from S1

The previous description can be verified before fitting any nonlinear map. Take S1=(13,13) as the anchor and use the same six synthetic windows. The squared Euclidean distance to S3=(12,12) is \((13-12)^2+(13-12)^2=2\). To S5=(11,9), it is \((13-11)^2+(13-9)^2=4+16=20\). Compute the other distances exactly the same way. For illustration, fix the local Gaussian bandwidth \(\sigma_1=2\) index points; a full t-SNE fit normally chooses bandwidths from a target perplexity, so our fixed value is an illustrative conditional-similarity calculation, not a fitted embedding or the result of a perplexity search.

For every other observation j, its conditional affinity from S1 is obtained by exponentiating a negative squared distance and normalizing the five weights. The exponential \(e^u\) is positive for finite u and decreases when u becomes more negative. Thus shorter distances receive larger weights, but these are similarity probabilities, not the probability that two sensors share an unknown true cluster.

\[p_{j\mid1}=\frac{\exp(-d_{1j}^{2}/(2\sigma_1^2))}{\sum_{k\neq1}\exp(-d_{1k}^{2}/(2\sigma_1^2))},\quad 2\sigma_1^2=2(2^2)=8.\]

For S3, the unnormalized weight is \(e^{-2/8}=e^{-0.25}\approx0.778801\); for S5 and S6 it is \(e^{-20/8}=e^{-2.5}\approx0.082085\). Add all five weights before division:

\[Z_1=e^{-72/8}+e^{-2/8}+e^{-50/8}+2e^{-20/8}\approx0.9450246443.\]

Contrast with UMAP: from S1, the first nearest neighbor is S3 (squared distance 2). S5 and S6 tie at squared distance 20, so a rule selecting exactly one of them must define its tie handling. UMAP forms a weighted local neighbor graph through a different normalization and optimization than the probabilities above. Therefore do not reuse the table as UMAP edge weights. Sensitivity to distance definitions, neighbor counts and ties deserves testing before interpreting any apparent island or cluster.

21. UMAP: a neighborhood graph, not a universal distance map

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) is another nonlinear dimensionality-reduction method commonly used for visualization. At a high level it builds a weighted neighbor graph representing local relationships in the original space, then optimizes low-dimensional positions to reflect that graph. You do not need the graph-theory details to understand the central decision: UMAP tries to preserve selected neighborhoods, not PCA’s linear maximum-variance directions.

Two consequential settings are `n_neighbors`, controlling the scale of local relationships considered, and `min_dist`, influencing how tightly points can pack together in the embedding. UMAP can use different distance metrics, and an implementation may support transforming future samples using the fitted structure. However, embeddings can change with random seeds or dataset composition and do not automatically preserve all global distances.

If original inputs include mixed units, inappropriate distances or sensitive protected attributes, UMAP can make visually clean but substantively misleading maps. When comparing t-SNE and UMAP on the same data, hold the preprocessing and evaluation objective constant; do not declare one “more accurate” merely because its plot looks more separated.

A 2D embedding is exploratory evidence, not a hypothesis test of natural cluster existence. It is also not a standalone fairness, causality or anomaly diagnosis tool.

A simple graph intuition

Imagine three points along one axis: A=0, B=1 and C=4. The Euclidean distances are d(A,B)=1, d(B,C)=3 and d(A,C)=4. If the local-neighbor rule selects one neighbor per point, A naturally connects to B and C naturally connects to B, while a direct A–C link is less plausible. UMAP generalizes such local links using weighted neighborhood relationships and optimizes their low-dimensional representation. The weights are more sophisticated than raw distances, so we do not invent them here; the example illustrates only why local graph construction matters.

22. Choose the method according to the question

PCA, t-SNE and UMAP answer different questions. This table is a decision aid, not an algorithmic ranking. Each method depends on representation, parameters, sample size and the real scientific objective.

PCAMaximum linear variance; minimum squared projection loss`transform()` with fixed mean and axesCan discard low-variance signals
t-SNENeighborhood-focused exploratory mapClassic scikit-learn version has no ordinary `transform()`Map distances and cluster gaps can mislead
UMAPNeighbor-graph representation in lower dimensionsMany implementations provide `transform()`Sensitive to graph assumptions/settings

For compression with reproducible inverse approximation, start by evaluating PCA. For visualization of local high-dimensional structure, t-SNE or UMAP may be useful after thoughtful Scaling and distance choice. For downstream prediction, compare pipelines using held-out scores and include an original-Features baseline. No method is automatically the best just because it is newer or more visually expressive.

A useful diagnostic sequence is: first explain the data sources and units; second define what information matters; third select an evaluation criterion; fourth fit on the authorized Training partition; fifth examine stability under plausible preprocessing and hyperparameter changes. This protects interpretation from attractive but ungrounded charts.

23. Reproduce the mathematics and the graphs in Python

The following self-contained code reconstructs the six observations, calculates the Covariance Matrix, fits scikit-learn PCA, fixes the arbitrary Eigenvector sign for comparison, and verifies the reconstruction. It requires NumPy and scikit-learn. The short example does not use Test for fitting. Numerical signs of components can differ between implementations while reconstructed values remain identical.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline

X_train = np.array([
    [13., 13.], [7., 7.], [12., 12.],
    [8., 8.], [11., 9.], [9., 11.]
])
X_validation = np.array([[14., 12.]])
mean = X_train.mean(axis=0)
centered = X_train - mean
covariance = centered.T @ centered / (len(X_train)-1)

pca = PCA(n_components=1)
scores = pca.fit_transform(X_train)
reconstructed = pca.inverse_transform(scores)
residual_sse = np.sum((X_train-reconstructed)**2)

print('mean:', mean)
print('covariance:', covariance)
print('EVR:', pca.explained_variance_ratio_)
print('reconstruction SSE:', residual_sse)
print('new score:', pca.transform(X_validation))
print('new reconstructed:', pca.inverse_transform(
    pca.transform(X_validation)))

assert np.allclose(mean, [10, 10])
assert np.allclose(covariance, [[5.6, 4.8], [4.8, 5.6]])
assert np.isclose(pca.explained_variance_ratio_[0], 13/14)
assert np.isclose(residual_sse, 4.)
assert np.allclose(reconstructed[4], [10, 10])
assert np.allclose(pca.inverse_transform(
    pca.transform(X_validation)), [[13, 13]])

# Additional QA: whitening and conditional t-SNE similarities
pca_whitened = PCA(n_components=2, whiten=True)
whitened_train = pca_whitened.fit_transform(X_train)
whitened_sample_cov = np.cov(whitened_train, rowvar=False)
print('whitened sample covariance:', whitened_sample_cov)
print('S1 whitened absolute coordinate:', abs(whitened_train[0, 0]))
print('S5 whitened absolute PC2:', abs(whitened_train[4, 1]))
assert np.allclose(whitened_sample_cov, np.eye(2), atol=1e-12)
assert np.isclose(abs(whitened_train[0, 0]), 3*np.sqrt(2)/np.sqrt(10.4))
assert np.isclose(abs(whitened_train[4, 1]), np.sqrt(2)/np.sqrt(0.8))
# Reuse only fitted Training parameters on Validation
w_validation = pca_whitened.transform(X_validation)
assert np.isclose(abs(w_validation[0, 0]), 6/np.sqrt(2)/np.sqrt(10.4))
# Single-anchor Gaussian similarities before t-SNE symmetrization
squared_distances = np.sum((X_train-X_train[0])**2, axis=1)
sigma_anchor = 2.0
weights_anchor = np.exp(-squared_distances/(2*sigma_anchor**2))
weights_anchor[0] = 0.0
p_conditional = weights_anchor / np.sum(weights_anchor)
print('squared distances from S1:', squared_distances)
print('conditional t-SNE affinities from S1:', p_conditional)
assert np.allclose(squared_distances, [0, 72, 2, 50, 20, 20])
assert np.isclose(weights_anchor.sum(), 0.9450246442595169)
assert np.isclose(p_conditional[2], 0.8241063212501105)
assert np.isclose(p_conditional[4], 0.08686016721629232)
assert np.isclose(p_conditional.sum(), 1.0)
# Sensitivity: sigma 1 gives a sharper conditional distribution
weights_sigma1 = np.exp(-squared_distances/2)
weights_sigma1[0] = 0
p_sigma1 = weights_sigma1 / weights_sigma1.sum()
print('sigma=1, P(S3 | S1):', p_sigma1[2])
assert np.isclose(p_sigma1[2], 0.999753, atol=1e-6)

For a downstream classifier, use `Pipeline([("scaler", StandardScaler()), ("pca", PCA(n_components=...)), ("model", estimator)])` and fit it only on each Training fold. The tiny six-row example deliberately does not claim generalizable classification accuracy. Larger labeled datasets and proper Test procedures are necessary for such claims.

24. A diagnostic gallery of common mistakes

The following errors are worth diagnosing because each may produce plausible-looking results. For every error, explain both its cause and a practical correction. They are not simply vocabulary mistakes.

1. Treating EVR as accuracy. EVR quantifies Feature variance, not correctness of label predictions; evaluate downstream performance separately.

2. Skipping centering. Without centering, the leading uncentered singular direction can reflect the offset of the coordinate system. Center using Training means.

3. Treating PCA as selecting a sensor. Each component combines measurements; report its loadings and units rather than naming it after one original Feature.

4. Comparing incompatible scales. Numerically larger units can dominate; justify or fit Scaling exclusively on Training.

5. Refitting on Test. Even unlabeled Test Features change learned means and axes. Freeze all fitted preprocessing.

6. Confusing a sign flip with a changed model. PC axes have ± sign ambiguity; compare subspaces and reconstructions, not raw component signs.

7. Calling rare contrast noise automatically. Low variance may contain the important signal; investigate domain relevance before deletion.

8. Assuming PCA preserves every distance. Dropping PC2 collapsed S5 and S6 to distance zero; inspect geometry for the application.

9. Reading t-SNE islands as true clusters. Map separations depend on optimization and settings; validate any substantive segmentation externally.

10. Interpreting t-SNE axes as physical Features. Embedding axes can rotate/translate; do not give them original units.

11. Comparing stochastic maps without controls. Hold data/preprocessing constant and report the random seed and hyperparameters.

12. Calling UMAP superior from a pretty image. Different objectives require explicit criteria; appearance is not an accuracy metric.

13. Using a fixed EVR threshold universally. Compression, anomaly detection and prediction have different costs of information loss.

14. Ignoring missing values/outliers. They can distort means/covariance; use a Training-only imputation and robust diagnostics.

15. Assuming linear reconstruction proves causality. A principal direction summarizes joint variation without establishing causal mechanisms.

16. Overstating six-row conclusions. A hand-checkable dataset teaches algebra, not statistically stable industrial generalization.

Additional diagnostic: Whitening does not mean the sensors are more informative: it changes each retained PC variance to one by rescaling, potentially magnifying a low-variance noise direction. Likewise, a conditional t-SNE similarity is not a posterior probability of cluster membership, and it is not an UMAP edge weight.

25. Worked exercises: reproduce, interpret and transfer

Try each question without opening its answer. These exercises test arithmetic, assumptions, interpretation, diagnosis and transfer—not just memorized terminology. Intermediate values are shown inside each answer so you can check your own work.

Calculate the two means without software.

Sensor A sums to 60: \(13+7+12+8+11+9=60\); B also sums to 60: \(13+7+12+8+9+11=60\). Divide each by six to obtain \((10,10)\) index points.

Why do both centered columns sum to zero?

Subtracting the sample mean yields \(\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=\sum_i x_i-n\bar x=60-6(10)=0\); the same identity holds for B. This verifies Centering.

Derive the sample covariance between sensors.

Cross-products are \(9,9,4,4,-1,-1\), summing to 24. Divide by \(n-1=5\): \(\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)=24/5=4.8\) index points².

Solve the determinant equation for PCA.

Use \((5.6-\lambda)^2-4.8^2=0\). Then \(5.6-\lambda=\pm4.8\), giving \(\lambda=0.8\) or \(10.4\). Both are nonnegative.

Why is the PC1 direction (1,1)/√2?

With \(\lambda_1=10.4\), \((-4.8)v_A+4.8v_B=0\) requires equal coordinates. Normalizing \((1,1)\) yields \((1,1)/\sqrt2\).

Compute both Scores of S5.

S5 centers to \((1,-1)\). PC1 is \((1-1)/\sqrt2=0\); PC2 is \((1+1)/\sqrt2=\sqrt2\approx1.4142\).

Reconstruct S5 using one component.

PC1 score 0 means reconstructed centered vector \((0,0)\). Add Training mean \((10,10)\). The error vector from original \((11,9)\) is \((1,-1)\), squared length 2.

Compute total Reconstruction SSE and EVR.

Only S5 and S6 have nonzero reconstruction errors, each 2: SSE=4. Centered SSE=56, so captured fraction \((56-4)/56=52/56=13/14\approx92.86\%\).

Does 92.86% EVR imply 92.86% classification accuracy?

No. EVR summarizes variation of \(X\) without labels. Classification accuracy needs observed Targets, predictions and a held-out evaluation. Low-variance PC2 may even carry a label-related signal.

What is the original distance between S5 and S6?

Difference is \((2,-2)\). Euclidean distance \(\sqrt{2^2+(-2)^2}=\sqrt8\approx2.8284\) index points. PC1-only distance is 0 because both Scores equal zero.

Transform a new (14,12) reading using frozen PCA.

Center with Training mean: \((4,2)\). Dot with PC1: \((4+2)/\sqrt2\approx4.2426\). Reconstruction is \((13,13)\). Do not refit using the new reading.

Explain what changes if Sensor B is multiplied by ten.

Variance of B becomes \(100(5.6)=560\); covariance AB becomes \(10(4.8)=48\). PCA then emphasizes B’s numerical scale. A scientifically motivated Scaling decision should precede PCA.

Why can an Eigenvector sign reverse?

If \(Sv=\lambda v\), then \(S(-v)=\lambda(-v)\). Scores reverse sign, but projection \((z\cdot v)v\) remains unchanged, so reconstruction is identical.

What does t-SNE perplexity represent?

It controls an effective scale of local similarities, not a desired cluster count. Changing it can alter the map; interpret neighborhoods with sensitivity checks rather than reading blank space as physical distance.

Should UMAP replace PCA for model training automatically?

No. The objectives differ, so use a fair Pipeline comparison under the same folds, metric and preprocessing. UMAP may help represent nonlinear structure, but can distort aspects important to the Target.

Suggest a future Jupyter laboratory with a fair evaluation.

Construct labeled data with correlated Features and a rare contrast signal. Within each Training fold, fit Scaling and PCA; compare \(k=1\), \(k=2\) and no PCA using a held-out predictive metric. Report retained EVR and errors on rare cases separately.

Whiten S1 and S5 and verify the sample variance of both new coordinates.

First compute \(t_{11}=3\sqrt2\) and \(t_{52}=\sqrt2\). Divide by the Training standard deviations: \(w_{11}=3\sqrt2/\sqrt{10.4}\approx1.315587\); \(w_{52}=\sqrt2/\sqrt{0.8}\approx1.581139\). Other centered scores are \(\pm2\sqrt2,\ -3\sqrt2\) on PC1 and \(-\sqrt2\) on PC2. As the sample means of the scores are zero, variance W1 is \((52/10.4)/5=1\), variance W2 is \((4/0.8)/5=1\); the covariance is zero. Units disappear after Whitening, because we divide Scores by their standard deviations.

Change the fixed t-SNE bandwidth from 2 to 1 index point: what happens to p(S3 | S1)?

All distances stay the same; change only the denominator from \(2(2^2)=8\) to \(2(1^2)=2\). The unnormalized S3 weight becomes \(e^{-2/2}=e^{-1}=0.367879\). The others are \(e^{-72/2}=e^{-36}\), \(e^{-50/2}=e^{-25}\), and two copies of \(e^{-20/2}=e^{-10}\). Hence \(Z_1\approx0.367879+2(0.0000454)\approx0.367970\), giving \(p_{3\mid1}\approx0.999753\). The narrower bandwidth concentrates similarity around the closest neighbor; it does not add evidence of a true cluster.

Should a one-component whitened PCA recover the contrast between S5 and S6?

No. Both have PC1 Score zero; dividing both by the same nonzero Training standard deviation leaves each whitened PC1 Score zero. Thus their projected distance remains \(|0-0|=0\). Only preserving PC2 distinguishes them. Whitening rescales retained information; it cannot resurrect a component that was discarded.

26. Glossary, practical laboratory and Student Understanding Test

Centering: subtracting Training mean; Covariance Matrix: variances and joint variability; Eigenvector: direction preserved by a matrix, up to scaling; Eigenvalue: scaling and PCA variance along that unit direction; Loading: weight of an original Feature in a component; Score: projected coordinate of an observation; EVR: fraction of total sample variance retained; Reconstruction: approximation back in original Feature units; SVD: singular-vector factorization; Embedding: new coordinates representing selected relationships; Leakage: use of reserved evaluation information in fitting.

Final synthesis: PCA optimizes linear variance and squared reconstruction, whereas t-SNE and UMAP emphasize neighborhood representations.
