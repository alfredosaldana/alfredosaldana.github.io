1. From a quality-control decision to a separating boundary

A factory inspects ceramic components before shipping them. Two sensor readings—surface irregularity score (\(x_1\)) and vibration signature score (\(x_2\))—have been centered and expressed on convenient numerical scales. The target is whether an item requires additional inspection: \(y=+1\) means flagged and \(y=-1\) means routine. These are deliberately synthetic measurements, not validated industrial risk estimates. Our task is to learn a boundary from historical inspections and predict which new items should be flagged.

A linear classifier draws a line in this two-feature plane. But infinitely many lines might separate two clean clusters. Support Vector Machines (SVMs) add a precise criterion: among separating boundaries, prefer the one with the largest geometric margin, the smallest distance to the nearest Training observations. That choice tends to make the boundary less sensitive to tiny perturbations of nearby points. It does not guarantee perfect predictions on future factory batches.

The word support refers to the examples that pin down the boundary; vector is the numerical feature representation of a component. We will derive the solution rather than merely display a line and label it “optimal.” Later we will deliberately add a conflicting label, see why strict separation breaks, and repair the objective using Soft Margin. Finally we will examine Kernels, which permit curved boundaries in the original input space.

The central distinction: a separating line only classifies the observed points; a maximum-margin boundary solves a specific optimization problem. A high-margin solution is not a guarantee of causality, calibration, or acceptable deployment performance.

2. Learning goals, prerequisites and the information boundary

By the end, you should be able to calculate a decision score, derive distances to a hyperplane, verify a Hard-Margin solution, construct a Hinge Loss objective, explain the Dual and Support Vectors, compute a Kernel value, and evaluate an SVM without looking at Test during tuning. These verbs are deliberate: each identifies something you can reproduce rather than simply recognize.

We need only basic vectors (\(w=(w_1,w_2)\)), a Dot Product (\(w^\top x=w_1x_1+w_2x_2\)), Euclidean Norm (\(\|w\|=\sqrt{w_1^2+w_2^2}\)), derivatives of simple quadratics, and binary labels. Whenever those tools appear, their meaning will be reconstructed. A prior guide can provide extra background, but this chapter does not require it for the central example.

Training may determine sensor transformations and model parameters. Validation may select settings such as \(C\) and a Kernel. Test is held apart until choices are frozen. If a StandardScaler is used, its means and standard deviations are learned only from Training and then reused on Validation and Test. This is the same fit() / transform() separation established earlier in the course.

4. Linear scores, predicted labels and hyperplanes

An SVM's decision score is \(f(x)=w^\top x+b\), where \(w=(w_1,w_2)\) gives the orientation and scale of the boundary and \(b\) shifts its position. Our predicted label is \(\widehat y=+1\) when \(f(x)>0\) and \(-1\) when \(f(x)<0\); a score of exactly zero lies on the boundary and needs a declared tie convention.

A hyperplane is the set of points satisfying \(w^\top x+b=0\). With two features it is an ordinary line; with three features it is a plane. The parameter vector \(w\) is perpendicular to that surface. The magnitude of \(f(x)\) is not itself a probability or a physical distance: scaling both \(w\) and \(b\) by 5 multiplies \(f(x)\) by 5 while leaving the separating line unchanged.

For the candidate \(w=(1,0)\) and \(b=0\), we have \(f(x)=x_1\). Thus T3 has \(f=-1\) and is predicted Routine, while T5 has \(f=1\) and is predicted Flagged. The new point \((0.4,1.5)\) has \(f=0.4\) and would be flagged. The second coordinate does not affect this candidate's score; this is a property of the fitted rule, not evidence that vibration can never matter in the real world.

5. Distance to a line: derive the geometric margin

We need a distance that does not change merely because the same boundary was written with larger coefficients. Pick any point \(x\) and a point \(x_0\) on the hyperplane, so \(w^\top x_0+b=0\). The normal unit vector is \(n=w/\|w\|\). The signed displacement along the normal is \((x-x_0)^\top n\). Substitution gives:

\[d_{signed}(x)=\frac{w^\top(x-x_0)}{\|w\|}=\frac{w^\top x+b}{\|w\|}.\]

Hence the unsigned perpendicular distance is \(|w^\top x+b|/\|w\|\). For an observation with known label \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\), its signed geometric margin is \(\gamma_i=y_i(w^\top x_i+b)/\|w\|\). Positive means correctly placed on its side of the line; negative means misclassified.

For T3, \(y=-1\), \(x=(-1,0)\), and \(w=(1,0)\): \(\gamma_3=(-1)(-1)/1=1\). T5 yields \((+1)(+1)/1=1\). T1 yields \((-1)(-2)/1=2\). Thus the minimum geometric margin is \(\gamma=1\). A line farther from one observation but closer to the nearest one can still have a worse minimum margin.

Functional margin versus geometric margin

The functional margin is \(y_i f(x_i)\); it changes when we scale \(w,b\). The geometric margin divides by \(\|w\|\) and therefore measures a real perpendicular distance in the chosen feature coordinates. They agree numerically here only because \(\|w\|=1\). Confusing them would make “maximize margin” meaningless: we could multiply the coefficients indefinitely without moving the boundary.

6. Why maximizing the margin becomes minimizing a norm

For separable Training data, choose a normalization in which the closest correctly classified points have functional margin 1. This means \(y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\geq1\) for every Training observation. The two supporting hyperplanes are \(w^\top x+b=+1\) and \(w^\top x+b=-1\); their distance is \(2/\|w\|\), because each is \(1/\|w\|\) from the center boundary.

Maximizing that gap is equivalent to minimizing \(\|w\|\). We conventionally minimize \(\tfrac12\|w\|^2\) because it has the same minimizer and differentiates cleanly: \(\nabla_w(\tfrac12\|w\|^2)=w\).

\[\min_{w,b}\ \frac12\|w\|^2\qquad\text{subject to }y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\geq1\ \forall i.\]

Why do the constraints matter? Without them the minimum would be \(w=0\), a model with no useful separating direction. The constraints explicitly require each positive sample to score at least \(+1\) and each negative sample at most \(-1\). The margin is therefore not an arbitrary cosmetic band on a scatterplot; it is the consequence of a constrained optimization problem.

7. Solve the Hard-Margin example completely

We propose \(w=(1,0)\), \(b=0\). First check every point. T1 and T2 have \(y f=2\), T3 and T4 have \(y f=1\), T5 and T6 have \(y f=1\), and T7 and T8 have \(y f=2\). All eight meet the constraint \(y f\geq1\). The objective is \(J=\tfrac12(1^2+0^2)=0.5\). The geometric margin is \(1/\|w\|=1\), and the full band width is 2.

Feasibility alone is not proof of optimality. Use the two observations T3 \((-1,0)\), labeled \(-1\), and T5 \((1,0)\), labeled \(+1\). Their margin constraints are:

\[(-1)(-w_1+b)\geq1\quad\Rightarrow\quad w_1-b\geq1,\]
\[(+1)(w_1+b)\geq1\quad\Rightarrow\quad w_1+b\geq1.\]

Add them: \(2w_1\geq2\), so \(w_1\geq1\). Therefore \(\|w\|^2=w_1^2+w_2^2\geq1\), implying every feasible solution has \(\tfrac12\|w\|^2\geq0.5\). Our candidate attains exactly 0.5 and is therefore globally optimal. The two inequalities become equalities only with \(w_1=1\), \(b=0\), and the minimum norm requires \(w_2=0\).

What has been proved: the model \((w,b)=((1,0),0)\) is a global Hard-Margin optimum for these eight points—not merely a guess. Other Training points at \(x_1=\pm1\) also touch the margin; being on the margin does not require a strictly positive Dual coefficient in every valid solution.

An independent check on the norm constraint

Try \(w=(2,0)\), \(b=0\). It classifies all eight Training observations correctly, but its minimum functional margin is 2 rather than 1. Rescale by \(1/2\) and obtain \(w=(1,0)\) without moving the decision boundary. The geometric margin of the unrescaled candidate is \(2/\|w\|=2/2=1\)—exactly the same geometric distance. Our normalization prevents us from mistaking an arbitrary coefficient multiplier for an improved boundary.

Now shift the clean boundary to \(f(x)=x_1-0.25\) while keeping \(w=(1,0)\). T3 has \(y f=(-1)(-1.25)=1.25\), but T5 has \(yf=(1)(0.75)=0.75\): the second violates the normalized Hard-Margin constraint. Thus simply moving the line within the gap does not preserve the maximum normalized margin. The optimum centers the separating corridor where its nearest opposite-class points permit it.

8. Read the support vectors and make predictions

A Support Vector is a Training observation with an active margin or a nonzero role in the Dual representation. In the clean example, T3, T4, T5 and T6 satisfy \(y_i f(x_i)=1\); the other four have margin 2. The nearest samples control the width of the maximal empty corridor between classes. Removing a distant point such as T7 may not change the solution; moving a near point such as T3 may.

Item \(x_1\) \(f(x)\) Predicted class Perpendicular distance
T1 −2 −2 Routine 2
T3 −1 −1 Routine 1
T5 1 1 Flagged 1
T7 2 2 Flagged 2
New A −0.6 −0.6 Routine 0.6
New B 0.4 0.4 Flagged 0.4

The new items lie inside the empty Training margin corridor (\(|f|<1\)) even though the classifier can assign a side. That is not a mathematical mistake: Hard Margin constrains Training, not future observations. An SVM decision score of 0.4 is not 40% probability; any calibrated probability requires a separate validated procedure.

9. From the Primal to the Dual: why only some examples matter

Constrained optimization can be written with a Lagrangian. Give each margin constraint \(1-y_if(x_i)\leq0\) a nonnegative multiplier \(\alpha_i\). For our Hard-Margin problem:

\[L(w,b,\alpha)=\frac12\|w\|^2+\sum_i\alpha_i[1-y_i(w^\top x_i+b)],\quad\alpha_i\geq0.\]

Differentiate with respect to the Primal variables \(w\) and \(b\). Stationarity yields:

\[\nabla_wL=w-\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i=0\ \Rightarrow\ w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i,\]
\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=-\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0\ \Rightarrow\ \sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0.\]

Substituting back removes \(w\) and \(b\) and produces the Dual maximization:

\[\max_{\alpha\geq0}\ \sum_i\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j(x_i^\top x_j),\quad\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0.\]

This expression depends on observations only through Dot Products \(x_i^\top x_j\). That fact will unlock Kernels. Notice also \(w\) is a weighted combination of Training points: the model can be represented by a subset of observations rather than by all of them equally.

10. Verify the Dual solution and KKT conditions numerically

Choose the Dual multipliers \(\alpha_3=0.5\) and \(\alpha_5=0.5\) and all other \(\alpha_i=0\). Their labels are \(-1\) and \(+1\), so \(\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=-0.5+0.5=0\) as required.

The reconstructed normal vector is:

\[w=0.5(-1)(-1,0)+0.5(+1)(1,0)=(0.5,0)+(0.5,0)=(1,0).\]

The Dual objective is \(D=0.5+0.5-\tfrac12\|w\|^2=1-0.5=0.5\). The Primal objective was 0.5. Equality plus feasibility verifies the solution's optimum through strong duality for this convex problem.

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) complementary slackness condition is \(\alpha_i[y_if(x_i)-1]=0\). Thus if \(\alpha_i>0\), the Training item must lie on the margin (\(yf=1\)). A point can lie on the margin with \(\alpha_i=0\) because Dual multipliers need not be unique when several points support the same geometry. These distinctions are important when interpreting a software library's support_ output.

11. When separation fails: add one contradictory inspection

Real measurements can contain overlap, sensor noise, or labeling errors. Add a ninth Training record T9 with the same features as T5, \(x=(1,0)\), but the opposite label \(y=-1\). T5 requires \(f(1,0)\geq1\) while T9 requires \(f(1,0)\leq-1\). Both cannot hold. Therefore no Hard-Margin linear SVM—and indeed no deterministic classifier using only those two Features—can classify both duplicates correctly.

Item Features Actual label Requirement for Hard Margin
T5 \((1,0)\) +1 \(f(1,0)\geq1\)
T9 \((1,0)\) −1 \(f(1,0)\leq-1\)

Adding more iterations or trying a bigger Kernel cannot resolve exactly contradictory labels for identical inputs. The appropriate response is to acknowledge the data's ambiguity, improve measurements or labeling if possible, and accept an explicit trade-off between model complexity and violations.

12. Soft Margin, Slack Variables and Hinge Loss

A Slack Variable \(\xi_i\geq0\) permits point \(i\) to violate its margin constraint by an amount \(\xi_i\):

\[y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\geq1-\xi_i.\]

To penalize violations, Soft-Margin SVM solves:

\[\min_{w,b,\xi}\ \frac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i,\quad \xi_i\geq0,\quad y_if(x_i)\geq1-\xi_i.\]

The positive Hyperparameter \(C\) decides how costly violations are relative to a wider margin. With \(w,b\) fixed, the smallest permissible \(\xi_i\) is \(\max(0,1-y_if(x_i))\), called Hinge Loss. Substituting gives the equivalent unconstrained-in-slacks expression:

\[J(w,b)=\frac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i\max(0,1-y_i f(x_i)).\]

Interpret it on our clean boundary \(f=x_1\): T3 and T5 have Hinge Loss 0 (exactly on the margin); T1 has loss 0 (correct and beyond the margin); the contradictory T9 has \(yf=-1\) and Hinge Loss \(1-(-1)=2\). Thus the nine-point total is 2, and at \(C=0.25\) the candidate objective is \(0.5+0.25(2)=1.0\). This is a candidate's objective, not a claim that it optimizes the nine-point problem.

13. Interpret Slack carefully: three different situations

Define \(m_i=y_if(x_i)\), the functional margin. The minimum slack is \(\xi_i=\max(0,1-m_i)\). If \(m_i\geq1\), the observation is correctly classified outside or on the margin and \(\xi_i=0\). If \(0<m_i<1\), it is correctly classified but inside the margin, so \(0<\xi_i<1\). If \(m_i<0\), the observation is misclassified and \(\xi_i>1\). At \(m_i=0\) it lies on the decision boundary and \(\xi_i=1\).

Functional margin \(m_i\) Hinge Loss Meaning
2.0 0 Correct, comfortably beyond margin
0.5 0.5 Correct class, but inside margin
0.0 1 On boundary; class tie
−1.0 2 Misclassified

This is why margin violation is not synonymous with classification error. A point can be classified correctly and still contribute to the Hinge Loss. Conversely, an observed 100% Training Accuracy does not imply the Soft-Margin objective has no penalty.

14. How C changes the objective: solve a transparent slice

To see the trade-off with actual numbers, restrict attention to candidate lines with \(w=(t,0)\), \(b=0\), \(t\geq0\), using the nine-point dataset. This is a one-dimensional slice of the objective, not the unrestricted SVM optimum. Four clean points have \(|x_1|=2\), four have \(|x_1|=1\), and T9 has contradictory label at \(x_1=1\). Therefore:

\[S(t)=4\max(0,1-2t)+4\max(0,1-t)+(1+t),\]
\[J_C(t)=\frac12t^2+CS(t).\]

At \(t=0\), \(S=4+4+1=9\) and \(J_C(0)=9C\). At \(t=0.5\), \(S=0+2+1.5=3.5\) and \(J_C(0.5)=0.125+3.5C\). At \(t=1\), \(S=0+0+2=2\) and \(J_C(1)=0.5+2C\).

Candidate \(t\) \(J\) if \(C=0.05\) \(J\) if \(C=0.50\)
0 0.450 4.500
0.5 0.300 1.875
1 0.600 1.500

For small \(C\), the candidate with a smaller norm is favored among these three. For larger \(C\), the more strongly separating candidate wins despite a larger norm. We can go further than comparison: for \(0\leq t\leq0.5\), \(S(t)=9-11t\), and for \(0.5\leq t\leq1\), \(S(t)=5-3t\). Differentiate each quadratic piece; for \(C=0.05\) the minimum of this restricted slice occurs at the kink \(t=0.5\), while for \(C=0.50\) it occurs at \(t=1\). This illustrates the trade-off without claiming these restricted solutions are the general two-feature fitted model.

Differentiate the restricted objective, not just the plotted curve

At \(0\leq t\leq0.5\), the restricted objective is \(J_C(t)=\tfrac12t^2+C(9-11t)\). Its derivative is \(t-11C\). For \(C=0.05\) the unconstrained stationary point is \(t=11(0.05)=0.55\), outside this interval, and the derivative at \(t=0.5\) from the left is \(0.5-0.55=-0.05\). The function is decreasing as we approach the boundary \(t=0.5\).

At \(0.5\leq t\leq1\), the objective is \(\tfrac12t^2+C(5-3t)\) and the derivative is \(t-3C\). For \(C=0.05\), the derivative at \(t=0.5\) from the right is \(0.5-0.15=0.35\). The curve rises immediately after the kink. Therefore, the minimum of this restricted slice occurs at \(t=0.5\). For \(C=0.50\), the derivative on the middle interval is \(t-1.5<0\) up to \(t=1\), while to the right of \(t=1\) the objective is \(\tfrac12t^2+C(1+t)\) with derivative \(t+C>0\). Therefore its restricted minimum is \(t=1\). The kink is the consequence of Hinge Loss being piecewise linear, not a numerical accident.

Where the box constraint in the Soft-Margin Dual comes from

It is useful to see, rather than memorize, \(0\leq\alpha_i\leq C\). Introduce a second set of nonnegative multipliers \(\mu_i\) for \(-\xi_i\leq0\). The Lagrangian becomes:

\[L=\frac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i+\sum_i\alpha_i(1-\xi_i-y_if_i)-\sum_i\mu_i\xi_i.\]

Differentiating with respect to each slack variable gives \(\partial L/\partial\xi_i=C-\alpha_i-\mu_i=0\), so \(\alpha_i=C-\mu_i\). Since \(\alpha_i\geq0\) and \(\mu_i\geq0\), we obtain \(0\leq\alpha_i\leq C\). Derivatives with respect to \(w,b\) still give \(w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i\) and \(\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0\). Substitution produces the same quadratic Dual objective as before, now subject to the additional box constraints. This is the precise mathematical connection between Soft Margin's penalty \(C\) and the permitted influence of individual examples.

Complementary slackness now has two components: \(\alpha_i(1-\xi_i-y_if_i)=0\) and \(\mu_i\xi_i=0\). If \(0<\alpha_i<C\), then \(\mu_i=C-\alpha_i>0\), forcing \(\xi_i=0\); the point must lie exactly on the margin. If \(\alpha_i=C\), the observation may lie inside the margin or be misclassified. Hence, for Soft Margin, many Support Vectors can have a functional margin below 1. This is why merely counting correct predictions does not describe how a fitted SVM uses its observations.

15. Hard versus Soft, and the role of the optimization algorithm

The Hard-Margin formulation requires feasible perfect separation. Soft Margin allows overlapping labels through violations. Neither formulation is the same as “fit Logistic Regression.” Logistic Regression ordinarily minimizes Log Loss and returns conditional probabilities; an SVM typically minimizes a norm-plus-Hinge objective and returns signed scores. Both can produce linear boundaries, yet they optimize different losses.

For the Soft-Margin Dual, the derivation of stationarity also involves \(\xi_i\): minimizing its Lagrangian leads to the bound \(0\leq\alpha_i\leq C\). The upper bound prevents any one observation from exerting unlimited influence under the chosen penalty. Its Dual objective keeps the same Dot-Product form but gains this box constraint. Software usually solves a related numerical optimization rather than the simple algebra of our eight-point symmetric toy example.

A larger \(C\) means stronger penalty for Training violations, not an automatic guarantee of better generalization. A small \(C\) may underfit and a large \(C\) may respond too strongly to noise. The best value should be selected with Validation or appropriate Cross-Validation, not by inspecting Test.

16. A genuinely nonlinear pattern: the XOR counterexample

Four points can form an XOR pattern that no straight line separates. Assign \(y=+1\) to \((-1,-1)\) and \((1,1)\) (same-sign coordinates), and \(y=-1\) to \((-1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\) (opposite signs). Both classes have mean \((0,0)\). If an affine score \(w^\top x+b\) were positive at both positive points, its average \(b\) would be positive. To be negative at both negative points, its average—also \(b\)—would have to be negative. Contradiction.

A different representation makes it easy: define \(z=x_1x_2\). Then positive-class observations have \(z=+1\) and negative-class observations have \(z=-1\). The classifier \(\widehat y=\operatorname{sign}(z)\) separates them. The data are not “unlearnable”; they are not linearly separable in the original coordinates.

The XOR example is conceptually separate from the factory dataset. It demonstrates why nonlinear feature mappings might help, not a claim that a particular Kernel must always improve factory inspections.

17. The Kernel Trick: dot products without explicit features

Suppose we map the original input \(x\) to a new vector \(\phi(x)\) and fit a linear classifier in that transformed space. The SVM Dual and decision rule require only inner products \(\phi(x_i)^\top\phi(x_j)\). A Kernel is a function \(K(u,v)\) that computes such an inner product implicitly:

\[K(u,v)=\phi(u)^\top\phi(v),\qquad f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_iK(x_i,x)+b.\]

A valid SVM Kernel must define a positive semidefinite Gram matrix for every finite set of inputs (equivalently, satisfy the relevant mathematical Kernel conditions). Calling an arbitrary similarity score a Kernel does not ensure it can be used in a standard SVM optimization.

For a degree-2 homogeneous polynomial Kernel, \(K(u,v)=(u^\top v)^2\). One explicit feature map is \(\phi(x)=(x_1^2,\sqrt2x_1x_2,x_2^2)\). Expand its Dot Product:

\[\phi(u)^\top\phi(v)=u_1^2v_1^2+2u_1u_2v_1v_2+u_2^2v_2^2=(u_1v_1+u_2v_2)^2.\]

Thus computing \(K\) can avoid constructing \(\phi\) explicitly. It does not mean the transformed feature dimension is infinite in this polynomial example; it has three coordinates.

18. Solve XOR with a polynomial mapping, then check Kernel values

Use the XOR points again. Since \(x_1^2=x_2^2=1\), only the middle feature \(\sqrt2x_1x_2\) varies with the class. Choose \(w_\phi=(0,1/\sqrt2,0)\) and \(b=0\) in the transformed space. Then:

\[f(x)=w_\phi^\top\phi(x)=\frac1{\sqrt2}(\sqrt2x_1x_2)=x_1x_2.\]

Evaluate all four: for \((-1,-1)\), \(f=+1\); for \((1,1)\), \(f=+1\); for \((-1,1)\), \(f=-1\); and for \((1,-1)\), \(f=-1\). Multiplying by the respective \(y\) gives functional margin 1 for every point. The feature-space norm is \(\|w_\phi\|=1/\sqrt2\), so the geometric margin in feature space is \(\sqrt2\)—not a geometric distance to a straight boundary in the original plane.

Now calculate two Kernel values directly. Let \(u=(-1,-1)\), \(v=(1,1)\), and \(q=(1,-1)\). We have \(u^\top v=-2\), so \(K(u,v)=(-2)^2=4\); but \(u^\top q=0\), so \(K(u,q)=0\). The Kernel uses dot products of transformed features. Its values are neither probabilities nor guaranteed to lie between 0 and 1.

Complete the Dual prediction: four actual Support Vectors

The feature-space solution above describes the separating rule, but we can also reconstruct it using only Kernel evaluations. Number the four Training inputs as T1 = (−1,−1), T2 = (−1,1), T3 = (1,−1), T4 = (1,1), with labels \(y=(+1,-1,-1,+1)\). Choose \(\alpha_i=1/8\) for each point and \(b=0\). These are Dual coefficients, not predicted probabilities.

Check the Dual label constraint: \(\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=(1-1-1+1)/8=0\). Using \(\phi(x)=(x_1^2,\sqrt2x_1x_2,x_2^2)\), calculate the feature-space normal:

\[w_\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\alpha_i y_i\phi(x_i)=\tfrac18(0,4\sqrt2,0)=(0,1/\sqrt2,0).\]

This exactly matches the Primal vector from the preceding demonstration. It has squared norm \(1/2\); hence the Primal objective is \(P=\tfrac12(1/2)=1/4\). The Dual objective is \(D=4(1/8)-\tfrac12(1/2)=1/2-1/4=1/4\). All four labeled margins equal 1, all coefficients are nonnegative, and Primal and Dual objectives agree: this verifies the optimum. The four observations have strictly positive Dual coefficients and are Support Vectors for this solution.

19. RBF Kernel: a smooth measure of proximity

The Radial Basis Function (RBF) Kernel is \(K(u,v)=\exp(-\gamma\|u-v\|^2)\) with \(\gamma>0\). Here \(\gamma\) controls how rapidly similarity falls with squared Euclidean distance in the chosen scaled feature space. A larger \(\gamma\) makes the neighborhood more local, while a smaller \(\gamma\) makes each point influence a broader region.

For \(u=(-1,-1)\), \(v=(1,1)\), \(\|u-v\|^2=(-2)^2+(-2)^2=8\). At \(\gamma=0.5\), \(K(u,v)=e^{-4}\approx0.018316\). For \(q=(-1,1)\), the squared distance is \(4\), giving \(K(u,q)=e^{-2}\approx0.135335\). Notice how the second point is closer and receives a larger Kernel value. A score of 0.135335 is not a 13.5% class probability.

The RBF feature space is effectively very rich. This flexibility can capture nonlinear boundaries but also create overfitting, especially when \(C\) and \(\gamma\) are poorly chosen. RBF does not automatically repair bad labels or compensate for future data coming from a changed distribution.

When is a similarity function a valid Kernel? A small RBF Gram matrix

A Kernel must be positive semidefinite (PSD) on any finite Training set: for every real coefficient vector \(c\), the symmetric matrix of Kernel values must satisfy \(c^\top Gc\geq0\). To make this check concrete, use three one-dimensional sensor positions embedded in two dimensions: \(z_1=(0,0)\), \(z_2=(1,0)\) and \(z_3=(2,0)\). They are synthetic features, not additional observations in the Training/Validation/Test experiment.

The squared distances are \(d_{12}^2=1\), \(d_{23}^2=1\), and \(d_{13}^2=4\). Choose \(\gamma=\ln2\) solely to obtain exact fractions: \(e^{-(\ln2)\cdot1}=1/2\) and \(e^{-(\ln2)\cdot4}=1/16\).

\[G=\begin{bmatrix}1&1/2&1/16\\1/2&1&1/2\\1/16&1/2&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det(G)=\frac{135}{256}=0.52734375.\]

For a symmetric \(3\times3\) matrix, Sylvester's criterion says positive definiteness follows when all leading principal minors are positive. These determinants are \(1>0\), \(1-(1/2)^2=3/4>0\), and:

\[\det(G)=1-\tfrac14-\tfrac14-\tfrac1{256}+2(\tfrac12)(\tfrac12)(\tfrac1{16})=\tfrac{135}{256}>0.\]

So this particular Gram matrix is positive definite and therefore PSD. This calculation does not prove validity for every possible dataset; the Gaussian RBF Kernel is known mathematically to be PSD for all finite input sets when \(\gamma>0\). The local computation illustrates the property; the general theorem supplies the universal guarantee. Our Python check below independently computes the eigenvalues.

20. Why Feature Scaling changes SVM geometry

SVM distances, Dot Products and regularization are defined in feature coordinates. Suppose a feature \(x_1\) is multiplied by 100 due solely to a change of measurement units. Then Euclidean distances change, and both a linear maximum-margin solution and an RBF Kernel may change unless the representation or its parameters are adjusted consistently. A model that relies on geometry cannot be indifferent to arbitrary units.

A StandardScaler transforms \(z_j=(x_j-\mu_{j,train})/s_{j,train}\), where the Training mean and scale are computed separately for each Feature. New observations use the same frozen \(\mu_{j,train}\) and \(s_{j,train}\). For example, if Training has mean 10 and standard deviation 2 for a sensor, raw 13 becomes \((13-10)/2=1.5\); using a new Validation mean would contaminate the evaluation.

One nuance: \(C\) and \(\gamma\) are relative to the feature scale. Even if mathematical units can be converted back, results from different preprocessing choices should not be treated as directly comparable hyperparameter experiments without a consistent Pipeline.

A numerical unit-change experiment

Suppose two observations differ by \(\Delta x=(1,1)\). Their squared Euclidean distance is initially \(1^2+1^2=2\). If the first sensor's values are expressed in units 100 times smaller, that same physical difference is represented as \((100,1)\) and the squared distance becomes \(100^2+1^2=10001\). With RBF \(\gamma=0.5\), the pairwise Kernel changes from \(e^{-1}\approx0.367879\) to \(e^{-5000.5}\) (effectively zero at ordinary floating-point precision). Nothing physical changed—only representation changed.

Standardization fitted on Training can make the two coordinate scales more comparable. But it cannot decide for us how much each sensor should matter scientifically. Scaling is a modeling choice that must be documented, checked for leakage, and evaluated along with the classifier.

Compare against an operational Baseline, not just an Accuracy target

An always-Flagged Baseline predicts \(+1\) on all four Test cases. Because three Test items are actually positive, its Accuracy is also \(3/4=75\%\)—the same as our selected SVM. Yet its confusion counts are different: Baseline \(TP=3\), \(FP=1\), \(FN=0\), \(TN=0\); SVM \(TP=2\), \(FP=0\), \(FN=1\), \(TN=1\). Identical Accuracy conceals a potentially important trade-off.

For a hypothetical cost of 100 units per unnecessary inspection (FP) and 1,000 units per missed defect (FN), the always-Flagged Baseline costs \(1(100)+0(1000)=100\) units, while the SVM costs \(0(100)+1(1000)=1{,}000\) units. Under this cost structure the simpler policy is better on these four observations, although it consumes inspection capacity. We have not measured any real monetary savings. The lesson is to evaluate the model against feasible operating policies and actual business constraints, not celebrate 75% Accuracy in isolation.

23. Linear SVM vs. Logistic Regression: same inputs, different learning rule

Both can use \(f(x)=w^\top x+b\). A Linear SVM minimizes a margin-based Hinge Loss plus a norm penalty; Logistic Regression minimizes Log Loss (often with regularization). Thus similar-looking boundaries do not mean the objective or interpretation is the same. The SVM decision function produces a signed score, while Logistic Regression's sigmoid maps a score to a model-based class probability.

Property Linear SVM Logistic Regression
Main Training loss Hinge Log Loss
Native prediction Signed decision score Conditional probability under model
Distance / margin Explicit maximum-margin objective No identical maximum-margin constraint
Probabilities Require additional calibration Native output, still requires calibration assessment
Scaling Often important Often useful, especially with regularization

Calibration means predicted probabilities correspond approximately to observed frequencies in an appropriate evaluation sample. SVM calibration methods such as Platt Scaling use additional fitted parameters and must themselves avoid Test leakage. They cannot convert an SVM score into a justified probability merely by changing the number's formatting.

24. Beyond a single boundary: hyperparameters, imbalance and reliability

Key SVM settings include \(C\) (violation penalty), kernel (linear, polynomial, RBF), polynomial degree, and RBF \(\gamma\). For nonlinear models, these choices affect both flexibility and computation. Explore a principled candidate set on Validation or Cross-Validation; apply the same preprocessing inside each fold. If classes are imbalanced, consider Precision, Recall, PR-AUC and business costs, not Accuracy alone.

A Class Weight changes the penalty for errors from a given class; in scikit-learn this can alter the effective class-specific \(C\). That choice should reflect the error-cost problem and be tuned with held-out data. It is not equivalent to shifting the classification threshold: class weighting changes training, whereas a threshold changes the policy applied to trained scores.

Nonlinear SVM prediction can require comparing a new example with many Support Vectors; memory and computation may increase on large datasets. The Kernel Matrix is \(n\times n\) for \(n\) Training points, so naive full storage grows on the order of \(n^2\). Linear approximations, feature maps, or alternative classifiers can be preferable at scale. An SVM also does not establish causal relationships between sensor readings and defects.

25. Reproduce the linear experiment with Python

The program below creates exactly the nine Training, four Validation and four Test records shown in the chapter. It learns scaling from Training, compares predefined values of \(C\) using Validation, freezes the best candidate, then evaluates Test once. It intentionally does not calculate probability predictions, because SVC decision scores are not calibrated probabilities.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, precision_score, recall_score

X_train = np.array([[-2,-1],[-2,1],[-1,0],[-1,2],
                    [1,0],[1,2],[2,-1],[2,1],[1,0]], dtype=float)
y_train = np.array([-1,-1,-1,-1,1,1,1,1,-1])
X_val = np.array([[-0.7,-0.5],[0.6,0.4],[0.3,1.6],[1.3,-0.2]])
y_val = np.array([-1,1,-1,1])
X_test = np.array([[-1.2,0.2],[0.9,1.3],[-0.1,0.4],[1.7,-0.6]])
y_test = np.array([-1,1,1,1])

candidates = [0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 1.0, 10.0]
results = []
for c in candidates:
    pipe = Pipeline([('scaler', StandardScaler()),
                     ('svm', SVC(kernel='linear', C=c))])
    pipe.fit(X_train, y_train)
    predicted = pipe.predict(X_val)
    results.append((c, accuracy_score(y_val, predicted)))
    print(f'C={c:g}, validation={accuracy_score(y_val,predicted):.2f}, '
          f'predictions={predicted.tolist()}')

best_c = max(results, key=lambda pair: pair[1])[0]
final_model = Pipeline([('scaler', StandardScaler()),
                        ('svm', SVC(kernel='linear', C=best_c))])
final_model.fit(X_train, y_train)
y_pred = final_model.predict(X_test)
print('Selected C:', best_c)
print('Test predictions:', y_pred.tolist())
print('Accuracy:', accuracy_score(y_test, y_pred))
print('Precision:', precision_score(y_test, y_pred, pos_label=1))
print('Recall:', recall_score(y_test, y_pred, pos_label=1))

Read the key steps deliberately: pipe.fit(X_train,y_train) fits the Training scaler and the SVM; pipe.predict(X_val) uses the frozen scaler and model. A newly constructed Pipeline is fitted for each \(C\). pipe.predict(X_test) happens after the selection. The small example is suitable for a future Jupyter laboratory in which learners vary the duplicate's label and investigate how \(C\), class weights and an RBF Kernel change errors.

Independent check of the Kernel and Dual solution

The second, self-contained block reproduces the XOR calculation from Section 18 and the RBF Gram matrix from Section 19. It checks that the Kernel score agrees with the explicit feature map and that this matrix has positive eigenvalues.

import numpy as np

# The four XOR records, in the order used in the derivation
X = np.array([[-1, -1], [-1, 1], [1, -1], [1, 1]], dtype=float)
y = np.array([1, -1, -1, 1])
alpha = np.full(4, 1/8, dtype=float)
K = (X @ X.T)**2

# Verify a feasible Dual representation of the Hard-Margin optimum
assert np.allclose((alpha * y).sum(), 0)
train_score = (alpha * y) @ K
assert np.allclose(y * train_score, 1)
Phi = np.column_stack([X[:, 0]**2,
                       np.sqrt(2)*X[:, 0]*X[:, 1], X[:, 1]**2])
w_phi = (alpha * y) @ Phi
primal = np.dot(w_phi, w_phi)/2
dual = alpha.sum() - np.dot(w_phi, w_phi)/2
assert np.isclose(primal, dual)

q = np.array([1.0, 0.5])
contrib = alpha * y * (X @ q)**2
print('XOR contributions:', np.round(contrib, 5).tolist())
print('XOR score:', round(contrib.sum(), 5))
print('Primal and Dual:', round(primal, 5), round(dual, 5))

# Three-point RBF Gram matrix at gamma=ln(2)
Z = np.array([[0, 0], [1, 0], [2, 0]], dtype=float)
d2 = ((Z[:, None, :] - Z[None, :, :])**2).sum(axis=2)
G = np.exp(-np.log(2)*d2)
eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(G)
assert np.all(eigenvalues > 0)
assert np.isclose(np.linalg.det(G), 135/256)
print('RBF Gram rows:', np.round(G, 5).tolist())
print('RBF eigenvalues:', np.round(eigenvalues, 6).tolist())
print('RBF determinant:', round(np.linalg.det(G), 8))

Expected outputs: contributions [0.28125, −0.03125, −0.03125, 0.28125], score 0.5, Primal and Dual objectives 0.25, and RBF determinant 0.52734375. Assertions stop execution if an identity fails.

26. Common mistakes and how to correct them

Mistake 1 — Treating a decision score as probability. A score of 0.4 is not a 40% estimated risk. Distinguish SVM scores from separately calibrated probabilities.

Mistake 2 — Confusing functional and geometric margins. The functional margin scales with \(w,b\), while geometric margin divides by \(\|w\|\).

Mistake 3 — Assuming every Support Vector has a strictly positive multiplier. With redundant margin points, a point can lie on the margin and still have \(\alpha=0\) in a valid Dual solution.

Mistake 4 — Claiming the candidate objective is the optimum. Evaluating \(J=0.5+2C\) for the noisy data does not solve unrestricted Soft-Margin optimization.

Mistake 5 — Assuming a large \(C\) is always better. It penalizes margin violations more heavily, but can fit noise and reduce generalization.

Mistake 6 — Assuming “correct” means zero Hinge Loss. Correctly classified points inside the margin still incur loss.

Mistake 7 — Calling any similarity formula a valid Kernel. Standard SVM Kernels must satisfy positive-semidefinite Gram-matrix conditions.

Mistake 8 — Assuming RBF fixes contradictory duplicates. Identical inputs with opposite labels cannot both be predicted correctly by a deterministic rule using only those inputs.

Mistake 9 — Skipping Feature Scaling. Geometric methods are sensitive to units; fit preprocessing using only Training.

Mistake 10 — Choosing \(C\) based on Test. Test must remain a final independent assessment, not a tuning tool.

Mistake 11 — Mistaking perfect Validation for guaranteed performance. Four Validation cases have high sampling uncertainty; examine larger independent samples.

Mistake 12 — Treating a separating SVM as a causal model. A sensor may help predict inspection outcomes without itself causing defects.

Mistake 13 — Comparing Kernel scores as absolute probabilities. RBF values are pairwise feature-space similarities, not posterior probabilities.

Mistake 14 — Evaluating only Accuracy with costly missed defects. Use appropriate error metrics and clearly defined operational costs.

27. Worked exercises: understanding, reproduction and transfer

1. Using \(w=(1,0)\) and \(b=0\), calculate \(f(-0.6,1.4)\) and assign the label.

The score is \(1(-0.6)+0(1.4)+0=-0.6\), so the predicted label is \(-1\) (Routine). Its perpendicular distance is \(|-0.6|/1=0.6\). It is inside the original Training margin corridor, but still lies on the Routine side.

2. Why is the score \(f=4\) not necessarily farther from the boundary than a score \(f=2\) from another SVM?

Because distance is \(|f|/\|w\|\). If the first model has \(\|w\|=8\), distance is \(4/8=0.5\); if the second has \(\|w\|=1\), distance is \(2\). Raw scores from differently scaled models are not comparable as geometric distances.

3. Prove that the clean dataset's Hard-Margin optimum must have \(w_1\geq1\).

T3 gives \(w_1-b\geq1\) and T5 gives \(w_1+b\geq1\). Adding: \(2w_1\geq2\), hence \(w_1\geq1\). Thus \(\|w\|\geq1\) and the objective is at least 0.5. Candidate \((1,0),b=0\) attains it and is optimal.

4. Calculate the margin width when \(\|w\|=2\) under functional-margin normalization.

The two supporting hyperplanes \(f=\pm1\) are separated by \(2/\|w\|=2/2=1\) feature-distance unit. Each side is \(1/2\) from the central boundary.

5. Compute T9's Hinge Loss for the clean solution and explain its error.

T9 has \(x=(1,0)\), \(y=-1\), \(f=1\). Functional margin \(yf=-1\), so \(\max(0,1-(-1))=2\). Since \(yf<0\), the candidate predicts \(+1\) but the actual label is \(-1\): a classification error.

6. Can T5 and T9 both meet Hard-Margin constraints?

No. They have identical Features but opposite labels, requiring the same score to be \(\geq1\) and \(\leq-1\) simultaneously. That contradiction is independent of which Kernel is used.

7. For a correctly classified item with functional margin 0.3, calculate its minimum slack.

\(\xi=\max(0,1-0.3)=0.7\). The item is correctly classified because its margin is positive, but it sits inside the functional-margin band and contributes to Hinge Loss.

8. Recalculate \(J_C(0.5)\) on the restricted slice when \(C=0.25\).

At \(t=0.5\), \(S(t)=3.5\) and \(\tfrac12t^2=0.125\). Thus \(J=0.125+0.25(3.5)=0.125+0.875=1.0\). This is a restricted candidate, not an unrestricted optimum.

9. Reconstruct the Dual vector from \(\alpha_3=\alpha_5=0.5\).

\(w=0.5(-1)(-1,0)+0.5(1)(1,0)=(0.5,0)+(0.5,0)=(1,0)\). The label-weight constraint is \(-0.5+0.5=0\). Both the Primal and Dual objectives equal 0.5.

10. Explain why a strictly positive \(\alpha_i\) forces an active margin in Hard-Margin SVM.

Complementary slackness is \(\alpha_i[y_if(x_i)-1]=0\). If \(\alpha_i>0\), the second factor must be zero, yielding \(y_if(x_i)=1\). The converse need not hold because some active points can have multiplier zero.

11. Show why XOR cannot be separated by a single affine line.

The two positive points average to \((0,0)\) and the two negative points also average to \((0,0)\). The average affine score for either pair is \(b\). For strict separation it would need to be simultaneously positive and negative, impossible.

12. Compute \(K((-1,-1),(1,1))\) for \(K(u,v)=(u^\top v)^2\).

Dot Product \(=(-1)(1)+(-1)(1)=-2\). Squaring gives \(K=4\). This is a Kernel value, not a probability.

13. Compute RBF Kernel similarity for squared distance 4 and \(\gamma=0.5\).

\(K=e^{-\gamma d^2}=e^{-0.5(4)}=e^{-2}\approx0.135335\). Doubling \(\gamma\) to 1 would lower the value to \(e^{-4}\approx0.018316\).

14. Calculate Precision and Recall on the final Test set.

With \(TP=2\), \(FP=0\), \(FN=1\), Precision \(=2/(2+0)=1\) and Recall \(=2/(2+1)=2/3\approx0.6667\). The classifier missed one of three genuinely flagged items despite 75% overall Accuracy.

15. Why is it improper to switch to \(C=1\) after seeing Test results?

Test must estimate generalization after choices are frozen. Using Test to choose \(C\) lets evaluation information drive Model Selection, making subsequent Test performance optimistically selected. Use new independent data for any additional final assessment.

16. A colleague says an SVM score of 0.9 means a 90% chance of a defect. How would you respond?

The SVM score measures signed position relative to the fitted decision surface in its model scale; it is not a posterior probability. To estimate probabilities, fit a calibration procedure on appropriate held-out development data and independently evaluate calibration before any operational interpretation.

17. Reconstruct the Kernel score at \(q=(1,0.5)\) using the four XOR Support Vectors.

Dot products with T1, T2, T3 and T4 are \(-1.5,-0.5,0.5,1.5\); squaring yields \(2.25,0.25,0.25,2.25\). Multiply each by \(\alpha_i y_i\) with \(\alpha_i=1/8\) to obtain \(0.28125,-0.03125,-0.03125,0.28125\). The sum is \(0.5\), so the class is \(+1\). The explicit feature map independently gives \(1(0.5)=0.5\). This is not a calibrated probability.

18. Why does one Gram matrix with positive determinant not, by itself, establish a valid Kernel?

A single matrix might pass a check even if another selection of points does not. For our RBF example, leading principal minors are \(1\), \(3/4\) and \(135/256\), all positive; Sylvester's criterion proves this symmetric matrix is positive definite. A valid Kernel needs PSD for every finite set. The general result relies on the mathematical property of the Gaussian RBF Kernel, not merely this three-point computation.

19. Keeping \(q_1=1\) in XOR, change \(q_2=0.5\) to \(q_2=-0.5\). What is the new score?

The explicit map gives \(f(q)=q_1q_2=1(-0.5)=-0.5\), so the prediction is class \(-1\). The Kernel contributions become \(+0.03125,-0.28125,-0.28125,+0.03125\), adding to \(-0.5\). Its magnitude is a decision score, not a probability or a guarantee of generalization.

Soft Margin Maximum-margin trade-off that penalizes permitted violations.
