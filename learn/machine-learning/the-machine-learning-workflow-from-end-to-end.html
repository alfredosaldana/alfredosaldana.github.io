1. A Machine Learning project begins with a decision, not an algorithm

A telecommunications company is losing customers, but its retention team can contact only a few each week. Choosing Logistic Regression, Random Forest or XGBoost is premature: no algorithm can fix a poorly defined objective.

The useful question is: “At the start of each month, which active accounts will cancel within 30 days, based only on information available now?” Risk predictions might prioritize support outreach; they cannot prove an intervention prevents churn or justify discriminatory treatment.

Formal problem statement

Unit of prediction: one active customer account at a monthly snapshot. Prediction time: the snapshot date. Target: cancellation within the next 30 days (1 = cancels, 0 = remains). Inputs: historical measurements finalized by the snapshot. Output: a predicted class or, with an appropriate fitted model, an estimated risk. Success: performance on genuinely future customers, judged alongside outreach costs, subgroup effects, and uncertainty.

This is Binary Classification, a form of Supervised Learning, because historical observations have known outcome labels. If the business instead predicted days until cancellation, that would require a different target and potentially a time-to-event model. Define the question before choosing the model family.

Translate a business goal into a measurable learning task

“Reduce churn” conflates prediction (who will cancel?) with intervention (whose cancellation can outreach prevent?). A high-risk account may be impossible to retain; a lower-risk account may respond to support. We model risk, not intervention effectiveness, which requires credible comparisons such as a controlled experiment.

Define the intended decision in operational terms. Imagine 1,200 active accounts are eligible at the beginning of a month, but the support team can review only 120. The capacity fraction is \(120/1200=0.10=10\%\). An ideal workflow might produce validated risk scores and a decision policy that respects that capacity; it would not automatically contact every customer above an arbitrary threshold. If a simpler rule flags 300 accounts, the workflow must address the remaining 180 alerts rather than pretend staffing is unlimited. The numbers illustrate decision constraints, not the effectiveness of any real retention campaign.

Write down what success can and cannot mean

Specify the population, prediction horizon, available inputs, evaluation period and error consequences. Example: “For accounts active at each monthly snapshot, predict 30-day cancellation; evaluate on subsequent months using Precision, Recall and review workload.” “Cancel within 30 days” differs from “ever cancel”; a future-month evaluation differs from grading the training records.

Decision checkpoint. Before collecting data, ask: Who will use the output? What action can they actually take? What happens when the model is wrong? What would the organization do without the model? Answering these questions prevents a technically polished prediction from being mistaken for a useful or ethical decision system.

2. Draw the whole workflow before touching the data

A Machine Learning workflow converts a practical question into evidence about a predictive system. It is more than “load data → fit() → print Accuracy”: choices at each stage determine whether the next conclusion is trustworthy.

There are two feedback loops. During development, Validation may justify revisiting Features, quality or models without consulting Test. After launch, verified outcomes and changing behavior can trigger investigation or retraining. The steps are iterative; independence of the final evaluation must survive those iterations.

Scientific question

Will patterns learned from earlier customers generalize to future customers?

Business question

Could predictions improve a legitimate decision compared with existing practice?

Operational question

Can the same inputs, transformations and outputs be produced reliably after launch?

Follow one account through all eight stages

Consider a customer whose usage fell from 100 units to 60 units during the relevant comparison period. At the problem-definition stage, we decide that cancellation in the following 30 days is the event of interest. At data assembly, the usage decline becomes a 40% Feature and the customer's recent support contacts are counted. At preparation, we verify the 100-unit denominator, date windows and absence of future events. At Training, earlier accounts supply evidence for a candidate rule. At Validation, we decide whether that rule is preferable to alternatives. At Test, we evaluate the frozen choice on a later cohort. Deployment would reproduce the same Feature definitions for new accounts; monitoring would check whether they remain meaningful.

An early error affects every later stage. A usage value recorded after the snapshot creates Leakage; replacing “contacts in 30 days” with “lifetime contacts” changes the Feature being predicted from. Data contracts therefore document meanings, units and timestamps across development and deployment.

Why the process may loop without contaminating evaluation

Suppose Validation indicates that the current rule misses too many cancellations. The team may investigate data defects, revise Features and compare a new candidate using an authorized development process. But the resulting Validation score has been used for selection, so it is not an unbiased final score for the entire search. Test is reserved for a final check after the choice has been frozen. For an initial lesson, the rule is simple; in practice the same principle applies to hundreds of candidate hyperparameter settings. More experimentation is not inherently bad—unacknowledged reuse of evaluation data is the problem.

Three deliverables, not three buzzwords: a written prediction-and-decision definition, a reproducible dataset with stable Features and Labels, and an evaluation protocol that states exactly which data can influence each choice. Those artifacts make the workflow auditable by someone who never attended the original modeling meeting.

3. Define observations, Features, Labels and time

We use a complete synthetic dataset of 24 accounts throughout the guide. Its invented values support reproducible arithmetic, not claims about actual telecom customers.

Each account has two pre-snapshot Features. Usage drop (%) compares the most recent 30 days with the preceding 30 days; usage falling from 100 to 60 gives \((100-60)/100\times100=40\%\). Support contacts counts customer-initiated contacts in the recent 30 days. The Label is cancellation during the following 30 days.

\[y_i=\begin{cases}1 & \text{account }i\text{ cancels within 30 days after }t_0,\\0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]

Here \(i\) identifies the account and \(t_0\) the snapshot. Inputs must exist by \(t_0\), but the Label matures afterward. Including a cancellation request filed 12 days later among day-zero inputs would be Target Leakage.

Important nuance: support contacts might reflect dissatisfaction, billing issues, or accessibility barriers. Their association with churn is not proof that contacting support causes cancellation. The metric should be interpreted, audited, and used carefully.

Calculate a Feature carefully—and inspect its denominator

Usage drop is often written \(d=100(u_{\mathrm{old}}-u_{\mathrm{recent}})/u_{\mathrm{old}}\), where \(u_{\mathrm{old}}\) is usage in the earlier 30-day window and \(u_{\mathrm{recent}}\) is usage in the later window, both strictly before the prediction snapshot. If usage changed from 80 to 60 units, \(d=100(80-60)/80=25\%\). If it changed from 80 to 100, the result is \(-25\%\): usage increased, so a negative “drop” is mathematically meaningful if the Feature definition allows it. A positive decline of 40% is not the same as a decline of 40 usage units.

What if old usage is zero? The expression divides by zero and is undefined. A robust data specification must determine whether to mark the ratio missing, create a separate zero-baseline indicator, or use a different representation; silently substituting 100% would manufacture a measurement. Likewise, compare equal-duration windows so that a 28-day billing month is not automatically treated as lower engagement than a 31-day one.

Separate the prediction timestamp from the outcome timestamp

For an account observed at snapshot \(t_0\), the label needs sufficient follow-up to determine whether cancellation happened by \(t_0+30\) days. If the data extract is produced just five days after the snapshot, many “0” labels would mean “not observed yet,” not “did not cancel within 30 days.” This is label immaturity, a distinct data-quality problem. In a real project one would set a label-maturity rule, account for delayed recording, and check whether account histories are complete.

An account can also appear at multiple monthly snapshots. That may be appropriate for a production system, but the records are not independent simply because they occupy different rows. Group membership and time order must both be considered when constructing splits; in our 24-row example, we deliberately use unique accounts to keep the first calculations transparent.

Interpretation test: at prediction time you may know that an account contacted support four times in the previous month. You may not know that it will submit a cancellation request 12 days later. Both facts might exist in a final historical database; only one belongs in the input table for the day-0 prediction.

Check the shape and prevalence by hand

There are 24 observations, two predictor Features, and one Target column, plus account and cohort identifiers. Training accounts T1–T12 contribute 12 rows; Validation V1–V6 contributes 6; Test E1–E6 contributes 6. Thus the fractions are \(12/24=50\%\), \(6/24=25\%\), and \(6/24=25\%\). This is a deliberate teaching design, not a recommendation to use precisely these proportions on large datasets.

Across all 24 rows, 12 accounts cancel and 12 remain: \(12/24=50\%\) illustrative churn prevalence. But the meaningful development rate should first be computed from Training: \(6/12=50\%\). Do not compute a feature-selection criterion using the outcome labels of all 24 accounts simply because they appear together in one printed table. Displaying a completed teaching dataset is not authorization to use its future outcomes during rule construction.

What would happen with genuine business data?

Before modeling, verify stable account keys, plan changes, cancellations reversed within the observation window, the meaning of a “support contact,” and the time each event became available. If ten calls about one technical outage are recorded as ten complaints, that Feature measures both customer behavior and the organization's own incident. If inactive accounts appear in a population intended to contain active accounts, the Target definition becomes incoherent. In production, row counts should be documented alongside inclusion and exclusion criteria so another analyst can reproduce the same population.

Reproducibility checkpoint: a dataset schema must define each column in words, its unit, permitted range, timestamp meaning and missing-value policy. Without a schema, the same 24 numbers could be interpreted in incompatible ways—even if everyone agrees that the arithmetic is correct.

5. The split is a scientific boundary, not just a code command

Training teaches the model patterns and supplies any data-dependent preprocessing parameters. Validation compares candidate models, thresholds, feature sets and other choices. Test provides a final, independent assessment of the frozen choice. The same data cannot honestly fulfill all three roles.

For future monthly churn, chronological splitting usually better resembles deployment than randomly mixing dates. If customers have repeated snapshots, also examine group-aware splitting: the same account in Training and Test can make the evaluation overly optimistic. The split must match the intended prediction population.

Why chronological splits answer a different question from random splits

A random split asks whether a model performs on withheld observations drawn from approximately the same mixture of conditions as Training. A chronological split asks whether a model developed using earlier information generalizes to later conditions. Telecom usage can change after holidays, price increases, service disruptions or new competitor promotions. Since our operational objective concerns future months, the chronological question is often more relevant. Neither method is universally superior; choose based on the actual deployment scenario and whether observations are related.

April develops the rule, May compares alternatives and June evaluates the frozen choice. A rule learned in April can fail later if customer behavior changes. Validation offers an early check, but six May accounts cannot establish reliable generalization.

A concrete calendar: when is a Label actually available?

To make the date rules testable, imagine snapshots at 00:00 on the dates below, with a 30-day forward outcome window and, for this example only, immediate Label availability when that window finishes. Real providers may need extra days for ingestion, disputes or reconciliations: the data-available timestamp, not only the event timestamp, is what a valid backtest must obey.

Design around groups and delayed labels

Imagine account A produces an April snapshot and also a May snapshot. If one row is in Training and the other in Test, highly persistent customer information may make the second row easier to predict, causing evaluation to overstate performance on genuinely new accounts. A group-aware protocol may be needed, depending on whether the intended deployment predicts existing accounts or new accounts. Now add timing: the outcome of a May 31 snapshot is not mature until late June. It cannot legitimately inform a model deployed on June 1. The split should therefore define an as-of cutoff, the last moment from which information may be used.

Quantify why tiny splits are fragile

May has six accounts. One prediction changing from incorrect to correct alters Accuracy by \(1/6=16.67\) percentage points. On a hypothetical evaluation set of 600 otherwise comparable accounts, one changed prediction alters Accuracy by only \(1/600\approx0.167\) percentage points. The latter is not automatically unbiased, but it demonstrates why tiny samples produce coarse, unstable estimates. Good splitting requires both independence and enough representative observations to support the intended conclusions.

Critical separation: selecting Rule B because it wins on Validation is legitimate development; modifying Rule B repeatedly after inspecting June errors and still calling June “untouched Test” is not. The name of the column does not guarantee the independence of the evaluation procedure.

7. Define the baseline before claiming an improvement

A Baseline gives every candidate a clear reference. Without one, a reported 70% Accuracy may be no better than current practice. Our baseline predicts stay for every account; Training is evenly balanced, so this is a fixed reference rule, not a unique majority-class choice.

\[\hat y_i^{(0)}=0\quad\text{for every account }i\]

In the six Validation rows, V1, V2 and V6 cancel; V3, V4 and V5 stay. Our constant baseline therefore makes three correct predictions and three missed cancellations.

\[\mathrm{Accuracy}_{\mathrm{baseline}}=\frac{3}{6}=0.50=50\%\]

The Baseline tests whether our metric matches the decision. When cancellations are rare, predicting “stay” for everyone may yield high Accuracy and zero detected cancellations; Recall reveals what Accuracy conceals.

A baseline also has a Confusion Matrix

On the six May accounts, predicting 0 for everybody yields TP = 0, FP = 0, FN = 3, TN = 3. Verify each number: none of the three cancellations is identified, there are no false alerts, and the three accounts that remain active are correctly left alone. Thus Recall = 0/(0+3) = 0%. Precision is undefined, not 0%, because the baseline issued no positive predictions and the denominator \(TP+FP=0\). An implementation may choose a numerical convention, but the report should state it.

Transfer this lesson to a different population. Imagine 1,000 future accounts, of which 30 cancel and 970 remain. The identical always-stay baseline would achieve \(970/1000=97\%\) Accuracy while missing all 30 cancellations. This is a separate hypothetical population—not a rescaling of our balanced 24-row dataset. It shows why a baseline must be interpreted alongside class prevalence, Recall and the intended decision.

Work through what “correct” means for a baseline

Compare against an operational alternative

A strong baseline need not be “predict the majority class.” It could represent the retention team's established policy, such as contacting customers whose usage drops by at least 30%. That practice could have real workflow costs even without Machine Learning. In our chapter we call this explicit policy Rule A and compare it later with a more inclusive Rule B. The value of a new method must be judged relative to the actual credible alternative, not only a conveniently weak trivial rule.

Using our declared illustrative error costs from later sections—$10 for flagging a customer who stays and $60 for missing a cancellation—the constant baseline's May cost is \(10(0)+60(3)=\$180\). This is a different assessment from Accuracy: the constant strategy creates no outreach workload but ignores all three observed cancellations. The accounting does not include the cost of contacting true positives or the effect of intervention, so it remains deliberately incomplete.

Do not misuse majority-class performance

In the separate 1,000-account example with 30 cancellations, always predicting “stay” achieves 97% Accuracy. Nevertheless, its Recall is \(0/30=0\%\). If each missed event hypothetically costs $60, the assumed total is \(30\times60=\$1,800\), before considering any intervention. Compare this with our six-row cohort cautiously: the two populations have different event prevalence, so neither their Precision nor their costs can simply be scaled from one to the other.

Baseline checklist: explicitly state the strategy, apply it unchanged to the same evaluation rows as candidates, calculate the same metrics, and explain whether the baseline reflects a meaningful current practice. A candidate is an improvement only relative to the criterion that matters and the decision constraints that truly exist.

8. From Training observations to two candidate decision rules

A fitted classifier normally learns parameters by optimizing a Training objective. To expose the workflow without hiding estimation inside a library, we instead propose two hand-specified candidate rules informed by Training. This distinction matters: their cutoffs are hypotheses, not learned optimum parameters.

Rule A — Usage only: flag an account if its usage drop is at least 30%. Rule B — Usage or support: flag an account if its usage drop is at least 30% or it made at least three support contacts. In both rules, “flag” means predict future churn (1).

\[\hat y_i^{(A)}=\mathbb{1}(d_i\ge30),\qquad\hat y_i^{(B)}=\mathbb{1}(d_i\ge30\ \text{or}\ s_i\ge3)\]

Here \(d_i\) is the recorded usage drop in percentage points, \(s_i\) is the number of earlier support contacts, and \(\mathbb{1}(\cdot)\) returns 1 when a condition is true and 0 otherwise. For T3, \(d=10,s=4\), so A predicts 0 and B predicts 1. For T4, \(d=35,s=1\), both predict 1 even though this account stays: it is a False Positive.

What, exactly, has been “learned”?

The rule cutoffs themselves were set as candidate hypotheses; they were not learned by solving an optimization problem. We can nevertheless estimate simple risk rates from Training. Rule B flags eight Training accounts, six of whom cancel. The observed positive-group cancellation rate is \(6/8=0.75\). The other four Training accounts include no cancellations: \(0/4=0\). These are noisy Training estimates, not reliable customer-level probabilities.

\[\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{train}}(y=1\mid B=1)=\frac{6}{8}=0.75\]

Add-one smoothing estimates the flagged cancellation rate as \((6+1)/(8+2)=0.70\) and the unflagged rate as \((0+1)/(4+2)\approx0.167\). These estimates demonstrate learning from counts, not well-calibrated probabilities from eight synthetic flags.

Calculate every Training error before comparing rules

Apply Rule A to the Training accounts. Its usage threshold flags T1, T2, T4, T8 and T10. Four of these (T1, T2, T8, T10) cancel, giving TP = 4; T4 remains, giving FP = 1. The cancellations T3 and T7 were not flagged, giving FN = 2. The remaining five noncanceling, unflagged accounts give TN = 5. Check: \(4+1+2+5=12\).

Rule B also flags accounts with at least three support contacts, so it now includes T3, T7 and T12. The first two genuinely cancel, while T12 stays. The resulting counts are TP = 6, FP = 2, FN = 0, TN = 4. Its additional sensitivity is not free: compared with A, B correctly identifies two more cancels but creates one extra false alert.

Separate a decision rule from a fitted statistical model

Our 30% usage threshold is a hand-specified hypothesis, not a mathematically optimized fit; choosing it after reading all 24 outcomes would contaminate Validation and Test. By contrast, Logistic Regression estimates coefficients by optimizing a Training Loss Function, a Decision Tree learns successive splits, and a Random Forest aggregates trees. Their fitting methods differ, but all must respect the same data partitions.

Training Precision is not Probability Calibration: six cancellations among eight flags (75%) are only a small historical group frequency. A trustworthy claim about individual probability requires a fitted probabilistic model and independent Calibration checks.

10. Make model selection answer the decision—not just Accuracy

Assume each unnecessary outreach (False Positive) costs $10 and each missed cancellation (False Negative) $60; correct classifications cost zero in this simplified exercise. These are hypothetical penalties, not measured marketing expenses or causal retention benefits.

\[C=10\times FP+60\times FN\quad\text{(illustrative USD per cohort)}\]

Baseline: three cancellations are missed, so \(C_0=10(0)+60(3)=\$180\). Rule A: one unnecessary flag plus one missed cancellation gives \(C_A=10(1)+60(1)=\$70\). Rule B: two unnecessary flags and no misses give \(C_B=10(2)+60(0)=\$20\).

If this cost rule was fixed before observing Validation, Rule B wins on that cohort even though A and B have identical Accuracy. When the true costs are uncertain, vary their assumptions and compare complementary metrics instead of treating a single estimated cost as fact.

Important limitation: a hypothetical misclassification-cost table is not evidence of uplift, causality, or return on investment. Real retention outreach might have its own costs, response rates, customer preferences and unintended effects. A controlled intervention study could be needed to establish whether acting on predictions truly improves outcomes.

Find the point at which a different rule becomes preferable

Keep the False Positive cost at $10 but let the missed-cancellation cost be an unknown nonnegative amount \(c\). The Validation confusion counts give three cost functions: \(C_0=3c\) for the baseline, \(C_A=10+c\) for Rule A, and \(C_B=20\) for Rule B. This is sensitivity analysis: rather than claiming the illustrative $60 estimate is correct, we ask how the recommended decision changes across plausible assumptions.

First compare A and B. A is cheaper when \(10+c<20\), or \(c<10\). B is cheaper when \(c>10\). But the baseline also matters. Comparing the baseline and A gives \(3c<10+c\Rightarrow2c<10\Rightarrow c<5\). Therefore, among these three policies, the cheapest is the baseline when \(c<5\), Rule A when \(5<c<10\), and Rule B when \(c>10\); at the boundaries there are ties.

Quantify sampling uncertainty without turning six cases into certainty

On the June Test, Rule B classified five of six cases correctly, so the observed proportion is \(\hat p=5/6=0.8333\). A point estimate is not a measure of precision. To see the scale of sampling uncertainty, use a 95% Wilson score interval as a teaching calculation. It assumes independent, comparable Bernoulli outcomes (correct/incorrect), and it does not repair cohort bias, temporal dependence or Test reuse. Here \(n=6\) is the number evaluated, \(z=1.96\) is the conventional normal reference for a nominal 95% interval, and \(\hat p\) is the sample Accuracy.

\[\begin{aligned}D&=1+\frac{z^2}{n}=1+\frac{3.8416}{6}=1.64027,\\ M&=\frac{\hat p+z^2/(2n)}{D}=\frac{0.83333+3.8416/12}{1.64027}\\&=\frac{1.15347}{1.64027}=0.70322,\\ R&=\frac{z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n+z^2/(4n^2)}}{D}\\ &=\frac{1.96\sqrt{0.023148+0.026678}}{1.64027}=0.26673,\\ [\,M-R,\ M+R\,]&=[\,0.43649,\ 0.96995\,].\end{aligned}\]

The interval is approximately 43.6%–97.0% under its assumptions. Interpret it as a range generated by a method with nominal 95% long-run coverage under repeated valid sampling—not a 95% posterior probability that the true Accuracy is inside these endpoints. This extremely wide range makes the limitation visible: 83.33% is not a reliable claim of real future accuracy. These assumptions are especially doubtful for synthetic, deliberately balanced, time-dependent records; the interval is an illustration of uncertainty, not a validated business estimate.

14. Data Leakage: three concrete ways a promising project fails

Data Leakage occurs when an input uses information unavailable at prediction time, or when an evaluation set improperly influences development. It can make historical results look stronger than future performance.

Leak 1 — A future event disguised as a Feature

Suppose an analyst adds cancellation-request status recorded on day 12 after the snapshot to predict cancellation in the next 30 days. The target is nearly revealed by a post-snapshot field. An apparently excellent classifier may simply be reading the future.

Leak 2 — Preprocessing learned from all rows

If an imputation median, normalization range, Feature selector or target encoder is computed from Training, Validation and Test, model development has used evaluation information. The correct procedure fits transformations within the Training partition (or within each fold during Cross-Validation).

Leak 3 — Customer identity or duplicated snapshots across splits

If multiple nearly identical snapshots from one customer appear in Training and Test, the model may recognize the account rather than generalize to new customers. Whether this is a problem depends on the intended deployment population; use group-aware or time-aware splitting as appropriate and explicitly define the unit of independence.

Leakage audit question: “Could the team have known this exact field, in this exact form, for this account at the moment the prediction would have been made?” If not, exclude it or reconstruct the historical value from a valid timestamped source.

Repeatedly changing rules after examining Test Labels also contaminates evaluation—even if the physical files stay separate. Leakage concerns the flow of information, not folder names.

18. Common mistakes: diagnose the cause and correct the process

Many serious Machine Learning failures originate in workflow design rather than algebra. A more powerful algorithm cannot compensate for invalid Labels, contaminated evaluation or undefined decisions.

1. Choosing an algorithm before defining a decision

“Use XGBoost” does not tell us the target, prediction date, allowable inputs or acceptable cost of errors. Correction: write the decision specification first; choose methods only after defining evidence requirements.

2. Treating all columns in a database as legitimate Features

Some columns summarize events that occur after the prediction date, such as processed cancellation requests. Correction: rebuild every candidate Feature as it existed at the snapshot timestamp and audit its source.

3. Using Validation and Test interchangeably

Trying dozens of cutoffs on Test creates optimistic selection bias even if the files remain physically separate. Correction: use Validation for choices, freeze the complete process, and assess Test once.

4. Mistaking Training performance for generalization

Rule B identifies all six Training cancellations, but this cannot establish its future Recall. Correction: report held-out results, time context, and sample size.

5. Reporting Accuracy without a baseline

In heavily imbalanced applications an all-negative predictor may look successful. Correction: compare with constant and current-practice baselines, and report class-specific errors.

6. Assuming identical Accuracy means identical decisions

Rules A and B both reach 4/6 Accuracy on Validation, yet A misses one cancellation and B misses none. Correction: inspect the Confusion Matrix, costs and capacity constraints.

7. Presenting assumed costs as measured economic value

Our $10 and $60 are teaching inputs, not empirical estimates of retention ROI. Correction: label assumptions, conduct sensitivity analysis and test whether interventions change behavior.

8. Claiming a six-customer Test proves reliability

One mistake changes Accuracy by 16.67 percentage points. Correction: evaluate on sufficient representative cohorts and communicate uncertainty.

9. Recomputing preprocessing on evaluation data

Fitting a scaler or imputation rule independently on Test breaks the evaluation protocol. Correction: fit transformations on Training and reuse them unchanged.

10. Confusing correlation with causality

Frequent support contacts might coincide with churn, but contacting support does not necessarily cause churn. Correction: distinguish prediction from causal claims and test interventions appropriately.

11. Releasing a model without a defined fallback

Input schemas change, data pipelines fail, and customer rights matter. Correction: specify validation checks, access control, human review, logging and rollback.

12. Believing the workflow ends after deployment

New billing policies, usage patterns and customer segments can invalidate old evidence. Correction: monitor data quality, drift, delayed labels, error patterns, and intervention outcomes.

13. Treating score distributions as evidence of Accuracy

A sudden 45% flag rate may indicate new customer behavior or broken support-count logging; without outcomes it says nothing conclusive about correctness. Correction: separate input-health monitoring from label-dependent performance checks.

14. Compressing documentation into “trained a model”

That statement hides the cohort definition, label window, leakage controls, baseline, selection procedure, cost assumptions and limitations. Correction: produce an honest model card and reproducible experiment record.

Worked diagnostic: separate a software error from a modeling error

E3 has drop 33 and two contacts. Because \(33\ge30\), Rule B flags it, yet its observed Label is 0: a False Positive, not a programming bug. Investigate data and Label validity; do not revise June Test retrospectively.

Now suppose software reads 32% as 0.32 and compares 0.32 >= 30, wrongly returning 0. This unit mismatch is an implementation failure before any Label arrives. Fix the input contract, test and version the change; retraining is not the remedy. Separate data, software, generalization and business-definition failures before choosing a correction.

19. Knowledge checks: calculate, interpret and defend each answer

Try each problem before expanding the solution. These questions cover the whole workflow—not just the early definitions—and each answer explains the reasoning and what conclusion remains unsupported.

1. Write the target and identify the prediction moment.

At the monthly snapshot t₀ we predict whether an active account will cancel during the next 30 days. The Label is 1 for cancellation and 0 otherwise. We must use only information recorded no later than t₀. A future cancellation date cannot be an Input.

2. Compute a usage decline from 100 units to 60 units.

The absolute drop is 100 − 60 = 40 units. Divide by the earlier usage baseline: 40/100 = 0.40. Multiply by 100 to obtain a 40% usage drop. The numerator and denominator refer to earlier periods, both before the prediction snapshot.

3. Why separate April, May and June accounts?

We are estimating future behavior. April supplies Training; May lets us select between hypotheses; June provides an untouched final evaluation. The chronological split makes the sequence of information available at development closer to the forecast setting. It still cannot guarantee that later customers follow the same distribution.

4. Calculate the Training churn rate and explain why it may mislead.

There are six cancellations among 12 Training customers, so 6/12 = 50%. This dataset was intentionally balanced for arithmetic clarity. It is not evidence that half of real telecommunications customers churn each month; the prevalence in a real deployment population could be very different.

5. Calculate the difference in mean usage drop between churners and non-churners.

For the six churners: (50+40+10+15+30+45)/6 = 190/6 = 31.67%. For the six non-churners: (35+5+0+25+20+12)/6 = 97/6 = 16.17%. Difference: 31.67−16.17 ≈ 15.50 percentage points. This describes Training association, not a causal effect.

6. Predict V2 under both rules and explain its errors.

V2 has drop 10 and contacts 4; its true Label is 1. A requires drop ≥30, so A predicts 0 and creates a False Negative. B requires drop ≥30 or contacts ≥3; because 4≥3, B predicts 1 and makes a True Positive.

7. Construct the Validation Confusion Matrix for Rule A.

A flags V1, V3 and V6. V1 and V6 cancel, giving TP=2. V3 stays, giving FP=1. V2 cancels but is not flagged, giving FN=1. V4 and V5 stay unflagged, giving TN=2. Check: 2+1+1+2=6.

8. Why is Rule B preferable by the stated Validation cost even when Accuracy ties?

Both rules correctly classify 4 of 6 accounts, so Accuracy is 66.7%. A has FP=1 and FN=1, costing $10+$60=$70. B has FP=2 and FN=0, costing $20+$0=$20. With the stated illustrative costs, B is cheaper on Validation. This is not proof that retention calls save money.

9. What is the observed Precision for Rule B on Validation?

Rule B flags five accounts: three actually cancel (TP=3) and two remain (FP=2). Therefore Precision = TP/(TP+FP) = 3/(3+2) = 3/5 = 60%. It means 3 out of 5 flagged accounts canceled in this synthetic cohort, not in a real population.

10. Calculate Rule B Test Accuracy, Precision and Recall.

Test gives TP=3, FP=1, FN=0 and TN=2. Accuracy=(3+2)/6=5/6=83.33%; Precision=3/(3+1)=75%; Recall=3/(3+0)=100%. These numbers are arithmetically exact for six synthetic cases but statistically unstable.

11. If the False Negative cost drops to $5, which Validation rule is cheaper?

Assume FP remains $10. Rule A costs 1×$10+1×$5=$15. Rule B costs 2×$10+0×$5=$20. Under these new hypothetical costs, Rule A becomes preferable. Model choice depends on the legitimate decision criterion, which should be specified before evaluation.

12. Would a cancellation request entered 12 days after the snapshot be a valid Feature?

No. The request was not known at the original prediction time. Using it to predict cancellation during the next 30 days reads the future and creates Target Leakage. A valid Feature must be reconstructed from data as of the snapshot; the later cancellation belongs to the Target window.

13. A flag rate rises from 20% to 45%. What can we calculate, and what can we not conclude?

Absolute change: 45−20=25 percentage points. Relative increase: 25/20=1.25=125%. This may reflect Feature Shift, logging changes or customer behavior. Without matured Labels, it does not establish that Precision, Recall or causal retention impact improved or worsened.

14. What would a defensible model card say about the six-account Test?

It should report the Test cohort dates and size, all four confusion counts, the decision and cost assumptions, input/timestamp definitions and limitations. It should state that the data are synthetic and balanced, uncertainty is enormous, the rules are not calibrated or production-ready, and no causal effect of outreach has been demonstrated.

It cannot tell us whether the flagged account will cancel, whether the rule generalizes, or whether outreach improves outcomes. Software correctness and predictive validity are separate questions.

18. A team changes its FN cost assumption from $60 to $5 after seeing June Test. What is the methodological problem?

Under the revised costs, Validation Rule A costs $10 for one FP plus $5 for one FN = $15, while Rule B costs $20 for two FPs. Selecting A would be a different development decision. If the team uses June outcomes to motivate or evaluate that selection, June has influenced the procedure and is no longer an untouched Test for the revised experiment. Costs should be specified on independent decision grounds; a genuinely new evaluation cohort is needed for a fully independent check of a post-Test redesign.

Step 1 — Apply the rule without changing it. Rule B flags when usage drop ≥30% or support contacts ≥3. The table yields flags J1=1, J2=1, J3=1, J4=0, J5=1, J6=0, J7=1, J8=0. Thus 5 of 8 accounts are flagged.

Step 2 — Read each outcome. J1 and J2 cancel and were flagged → TP=2. J3, J5 and J7 remain active but were flagged → FP=3. No actual cancellation was missed → FN=0. J4, J6 and J8 remain and were not flagged → TN=3. Confirm the total: 2+3+0+3=8.

Step 3 — Calculate the metrics and units. Accuracy=(TP+TN)/8=(2+3)/8=5/8=62.5%. Precision=TP/(TP+FP)=2/(2+3)=2/5=40%. Recall=TP/(TP+FN)=2/(2+0)=100%. Flag Rate=(TP+FP)/8=(2+3)/8=62.5%; the observed event prevalence is (TP+FN)/8=2/8=25%.

Step 4 — Compare against the baseline on the same cases. Always predict 0: TP=0, FP=0, FN=2, TN=6. Its Accuracy=(0+6)/8=75%, larger than Rule B's 62.5%. But at the assumed $10 per FP and $60 per FN, the baseline has 0×$10+2×$60=$120 error cost, versus Rule B's 3×$10+0×$60=$30 (illustrative dollars per eight-account cohort).

Step 5 — Make the appropriate inference. Two of eight July customers cancel compared with three of six June customers; this difference alone cannot establish distribution or concept drift. The cohort was invented, tiny and differs in event prevalence. Rule B is more favorable under the stated toy error costs, despite lower Accuracy; nothing here proves that contacting customers prevents cancellation. Because the model was held fixed, July outcomes can be inspected for monitoring once labels mature. Any rule change motivated by these outcomes requires another properly designed evaluation.

20. Glossary, model card and a final end-to-end synthesis

The glossary connects each term to its role in the churn example rather than offering isolated vocabulary to memorize.

Problem formulation
Explicit decision, user, target, prediction time, and acceptable use of errors.
Observation / Snapshot
One account as it existed at a specified prediction date; the unit for one row.
Feature
A measurement legitimately known when the prediction must be issued.
Target / Label
The future cancellation indicator observed only after the 30-day outcome window.
Training
The partition used to learn parameters and fit preprocessing transformations.
Validation
Held-out partition supporting candidate selection and configuration decisions.
Test
Untouched final partition used to assess the selected, frozen procedure.
Baseline
Simple comparison rule, such as predicting that every customer stays.
Parameter
Quantity estimated using Training, such as a regression coefficient or a fitted rate.
Hyperparameter
Configuration decided through development, such as maximum tree depth.
True Positive
Canceled account correctly flagged as likely to cancel.
False Positive
Account incorrectly flagged for cancellation though it remains.
False Negative
Cancellation that the predictor misses.
True Negative
Account correctly predicted to remain active.
Precision
Among all flags, the share that actually belonged to the positive class.
Recall
Among actual cancellations, the share that the model identified.
Data Leakage
Use of future or improperly shared evaluation information in model development.
Generalization
Useful performance on observations not used to fit and select the procedure.
Pipeline
Reproducible chain of data checks, fitted transformations, model and output.
Model Selection
Choosing a candidate based on legitimate Validation evidence and objectives.
Calibration
Agreement between estimated probabilities and observed event frequencies.
Distribution Shift
Change in data distributions between development and subsequent use.
Concept Drift
Change over time in the relationship between predictors and future outcomes.
Deployment
Controlled delivery of a tested prediction process into an operating environment.
Monitoring
Ongoing inspection of input health, model behavior, and label-dependent performance.
Model Card
Document describing intended use, evidence, limitations, risks and oversight.
