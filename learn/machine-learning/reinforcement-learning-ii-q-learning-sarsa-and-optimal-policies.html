1. Learning from actions rather than a known map

In the previous guide, the robot could calculate an optimal Policy because the transition probabilities and Rewards were given. A real warehouse robot rarely receives such a complete map. It may know that driving through a junction is possible, yet not know which action will be most rewarding after repeated operations. Reinforcement Learning (RL) addresses this problem by updating estimates from observed transitions.

We will follow one synthetic logistics case throughout: dispatch a parcel directly, or pass through a junction where another decision becomes available. We will construct exact reference values to check our learning algorithm; the learner itself will not be given those reference values. This distinction between an analyst's verification and an Agent's available information is essential.

Our main questions are: How does one experience change \(Q(s,a)\)? What distinguishes SARSA from Q-Learning? Why can exploration change the best behavior? And how should we test a learned Policy without confusing simulation with industrial evidence?

2. The complete MDP and its units

An MDP (Markov Decision Process) specifies States, Actions, Transition Probabilities, Rewards, and a discount factor. Here D is the depot, J the junction and T the absorbing terminal state. The Markov assumption says the chosen State contains enough relevant information to determine the distribution of the next transition from the current State and Action; it does not mean history is irrelevant to every real problem.

Current State Action Next State Immediate Reward (points)
D direct T +5
D via J J −1
J express T +7
J standard T +5

Every transition has probability 1 in the main teaching case. This intentional simplicity lets us verify learning arithmetic exactly. Rewards represent invented utility points, not money, probabilities or measured productivity. An episode ends upon reaching T; after T the future value is zero. Later we will introduce stochastic express outcomes with the same expected Reward, clarifying why matching means does not imply matching risk.

We use \(\gamma=0.9\): one utility point received one step later contributes 0.9 points to the present Return. The time index starts at the current decision; immediate Reward is not discounted.

3. Return: compute each possible route without guessing

The Return \(G_t\) is the discounted sum of Rewards from time \(t\): \(G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2R_{t+3}+\cdots\). The Reward associated with the action at time \(t\) has exponent zero. Because the episode terminates after one or two actions, the series is finite here.

Direct shipping has one Reward, \(G(D,\mathrm{direct})=5\). Going through J and choosing express yields two Rewards: first \(-1\), then \(+7\). Therefore \(G(D,\mathrm{viaJ},\mathrm{express})=-1+0.9(7)=-1+6.3=5.3\). If the second action is standard: \(-1+0.9(5)=-1+4.5=3.5\). These three complete trajectories make the tradeoff tangible.

The first route receives 5 points now. The express route sacrifices 1 point now to earn 7 later; its discounted total is 5.3, only 0.3 points above direct. The standard route yields 3.5, below direct. Without the discount factor the express route would produce 6 rather than 5.3. Hence specifying the decision horizon is not optional.

4. Action Values Q(s,a) versus State Values V(s)

The Action Value \(Q^\pi(s,a)\) asks: “If I take action \(a\) in State \(s\) and then follow Policy \(\pi\), how much discounted Return should I expect?” The State Value \(V^\pi(s)\) asks for the expected Return when the Policy chooses the current action as well. A Policy \(\pi(a|s)\) is the probability of choosing action \(a\) from State \(s\).

\[V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a|s)Q^\pi(s,a)\]

For a greedy deterministic Policy, \(V^\pi(s)\) simply equals the chosen Action Value. If \(Q(J,\mathrm{express})=7\) and \(Q(J,\mathrm{standard})=5\), always choosing express makes \(V(J)=7\). If a Policy chooses express 75% of the time and standard 25%, then \(V(J)=0.75(7)+0.25(5)=5.25+1.25=6.50\) points.

Never confuse \(Q\) with the immediate Reward. \(Q(D,\mathrm{viaJ})\) includes the eventual consequence of the next action; its immediate Reward is only \(-1\). In model-free learning, the Agent estimates \(Q\) from samples rather than receiving it from the MDP table.

5. The optimal Policy as an independent reference

To verify learning later, we as analysts solve the small MDP. Because all J actions end at T, \(Q^*(J,\mathrm{express})=7+0.9(0)=7\) and \(Q^*(J,\mathrm{standard})=5+0.9(0)=5\). The star indicates the optimal action-value function: maximal expected Return when actions after the first one are chosen optimally.

At D, direct is 5. The via-J action gives \(-1+0.9\max(7,5)=-1+6.3=5.3\). Thus

\[Q^*(D,\mathrm{direct})=5,\qquad Q^*(D,\mathrm{viaJ})=5.3.\]

The resulting optimal deterministic Policy is via J at D and express at J. Reconstruct its Return from the complete trajectory: \(-1+0.9(7)=5.3\), identical to the Bellman backup. This solution is a mathematical verification target, not a table secretly supplied to the learner. We will use it to judge whether Q-Learning approaches the right answer.

6. Why Trial-and-Error Learning is different from planning

Planning assumes the transition law and Reward model are available: it computes expectations over possible next States. Model-Free Reinforcement Learning updates value estimates using transitions actually experienced: \((s,a,r,s')\). One transition records the State before the action, the action chosen, the Reward received and the next State. SARSA also records the next chosen action \(a'\). Neither method needs to know the probability table to make an individual update.

For example, observing \((D,\mathrm{viaJ},-1,J)\) tells the Agent that visiting J incurs one point now. By itself that observation does not reveal which J action is best or the long-run value of routing through J. Learning requires subsequent observations, numerical estimates and sufficient exploration. The same four-column transition can occur many times and repeatedly adjust the estimates.

The distinction matters in application: knowing a warehouse route exists does not guarantee knowing its expected operational value. Model-Free algorithms learn from records of interaction, but deploying those experiments blindly in a physical system can be costly or unsafe; validated simulators, logs, and constrained trials may be appropriate.

7. Exploration versus Exploitation, computed

Exploitation means choosing the currently highest-valued action; Exploration means sometimes trying alternatives to improve knowledge. An epsilon-greedy Policy chooses a random action with probability \(\varepsilon\) and a greedy action with probability \(1-\varepsilon\). The random choice includes the greedy action, so its total probability is not merely \(1-\varepsilon\).

At J there are two actions and express is greedy. With \(\varepsilon=0.5\), selecting the greedy branch contributes \(1-0.5=0.5\) probability of express, while random exploration contributes \(0.5\times(1/2)=0.25\) probability. Thus \(P(\mathrm{express}|J)=0.5+0.25=0.75\) and \(P(\mathrm{standard}|J)=0.25\). Check: \(0.75+0.25=1\).

The expected reward actually generated by that exploratory behavior at J is \(0.75(7)+0.25(5)=6.5\), not 7. Exploration carries a tangible opportunity cost of \(7-6.5=0.5\) expected points per visit to J. In a different environment, exploration can have even larger safety or fairness implications.

8. Temporal-Difference Error: learning from an unfinished future

A Temporal-Difference (TD) target combines an observed immediate Reward with an estimate of later value. The difference between this target and the old estimate is the TD Error, denoted \(\delta\). It is not the same thing as an ordinary regression residual, although both are differences.

\[\delta =\underbrace{r+\gamma\widehat V_{\mathrm{next}}}_{\mathrm{TD\ target}}-\underbrace{Q(s,a)}_{\mathrm{old\ estimate}}\]

The update is \(Q_{\rm new}(s,a)=Q(s,a)+\alpha\delta\), where \(\alpha\in(0,1]\) is a Learning Rate controlling how strongly the new observation changes the estimate. The quantity \(\widehat V_{\mathrm{next}}\) depends on the algorithm: SARSA uses the value of the next action actually selected; Q-Learning uses the largest estimated next Action Value.

If the transition reaches T, there is no next decision and the next-state term equals zero. An observed terminal Reward \(r=7\), old \(Q(J,\mathrm{express})=3\) and \(\alpha=0.5\) give \(\delta=7-3=4\) and \(Q_{\rm new}=3+0.5(4)=5\). We did not need to wait for additional episodes.

9. SARSA: derive the on-policy update

The name SARSA refers to the five pieces of experience: State, Action, Reward, next State, next Action. Its TD target evaluates the next action chosen by the same behavior Policy that produces the data. Thus it is called On-Policy TD Control.

\[Q_{\rm new}(s,a)=Q(s,a)+\alpha\big[r+\gamma Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\big]\]

Consider one fully specified snapshot of the current Q-table: \(Q(D,\mathrm{viaJ})=2\), \(Q(J,\mathrm{express})=7\), \(Q(J,\mathrm{standard})=5\). The Agent chooses via J, observes Reward \(r=-1\), reaches J and then selects standard, perhaps as exploration. Set \(\alpha=0.5\) and \(\gamma=0.9\).

The Target is \(-1+0.9(5)=3.5\). The TD Error is \(3.5-2=1.5\). The update gives \(2+0.5(1.5)=\boxed{2.75}\). Notice that we use the selected standard Action, even though express has a higher current estimate. The ensuing standard action will later generate Reward +5 and its own update. The current SARSA update does not replace its chosen \(a'\) with an imaginary greedy action.

10. Q-Learning: derive the off-policy update

Q-Learning uses the same observed transition \((s,a,r,s')\) but backs up the maximum estimated value available in \(s'\), whether or not the behavior Policy selects that maximizing action. It therefore seeks an optimal greedy target Policy while it may collect data with a different exploratory behavior Policy: Off-Policy TD Control.

\[Q_{\rm new}(s,a)=Q(s,a)+\alpha\big[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\big]\]

Reuse the identical numbers: old \(Q(D,\mathrm{viaJ})=2\); next values are express 7 and standard 5; observed \(r=-1\); \(\alpha=0.5\) and \(\gamma=0.9\). The maximum is \(\max(7,5)=7\). The target is \(-1+0.9(7)=5.3\), TD Error \(5.3-2=3.3\), and new value \(2+0.5(3.3)=\boxed{3.65}\).

SARSA produced 2.75; Q-Learning produced 3.65, from the same physical transition. What changed was the future action used to compute the Target. Neither number is an unconditional promise: both depend on the existing, possibly inaccurate Q estimates. An early overestimate can distort many subsequent backups.

11. A table that distinguishes behavior from target Policy

Behavior Policy is the rule that chooses actions during data collection. Target Policy is the rule whose values we are trying to learn. For SARSA, these are the same evolving Policy; for one-step Q-Learning the behavior Policy can be exploratory while the backup is greedy.

Question SARSA Q-Learning
What is the next backup term? \(Q(s',a')\) for the chosen \(a'\) \(\max_b Q(s',b)\)
Policy relationship On-Policy Off-Policy
Can it explore with \(\varepsilon>0\)? Yes Yes
Is the update always different? No, greedy next action can match No, equal estimates can match
Does the algorithm prove operational safety? No No

A misconception is that Q-Learning must take the greedy action at every step. That would often prevent exploring unknown actions. It can execute standard while its Target uses express. Conversely, SARSA is not merely a differently named Q-Learning equation: because its next action is sampled, the persistent Exploration rate can affect which Policy it learns to favor.

12. Expected SARSA is useful for checking—not replacing—sampled SARSA

Ordinary SARSA updates with the single next action actually sampled. Expected SARSA, a related algorithm, replaces this sample by the average over actions under the Policy. It is useful here as an independent analytical benchmark for understanding how persistent exploration affects value.

\[\mathrm{Expected\ SARSA\ target}=r+\gamma\sum_{a'}\pi(a'|s')Q(s',a')\]

With \(\varepsilon=0.5\) and J estimates at 7 and 5, express probability is 0.75 and standard probability is 0.25. Consequently \(V^\pi(J)=0.75(7)+0.25(5)=6.5\). The expected via-J backup is \(-1+0.9(6.5)=\boxed{4.85}\), whereas Q-Learning's greedy backup is \(-1+0.9(7)=5.30\).

From old \(Q(D,\mathrm{viaJ})=2\) and \(\alpha=.5\), an Expected SARSA update would be \(2+.5(4.85-2)=3.425\). This is not the result of a single ordinary SARSA update after choosing standard (2.75) or express (3.65). Indeed, \(0.25(2.75)+0.75(3.65)=3.425\). We have independently verified the expectation by averaging the possible sample updates.

13. An important distinction: optimal versus exploratory behavior

Our two algorithms answer different asymptotic questions if epsilon remains at 0.5 and appropriate learning conditions hold. The optimal greedy value from D is 5.3 via J, as verified earlier. However, a behavior Policy that explores at J earns 6.5 on average there, producing only \(-1+0.9(6.5)=4.85\) through the junction. Direct shipping still earns 5. Thus under persistent epsilon-greedy exploration, a Policy that is greedy with respect to its own on-policy Action Values prefers direct.

That is not evidence that SARSA fails to find good actions or that Q-Learning is universally better. With epsilon decaying to zero, SARSA can also approach the optimal greedy Policy under standard tabular convergence assumptions. Moreover, executing the exploratory Q-Learning Behavior Policy still exposes it to suboptimal actions, even when its target Q-values are optimal.

14. One exact exploration threshold: when does the decision reverse?

The expected value at J under epsilon-greedy with two actions (when express is greedy) is \([1-\varepsilon/2](7)+[\varepsilon/2](5)=7-\varepsilon\). This follows because exploration chooses standard with probability \(\varepsilon/2\) and sacrifices 2 points relative to express.

The via-J value becomes \(Q^\pi(D,\mathrm{viaJ})=-1+0.9(7-\varepsilon)=5.3-0.9\varepsilon\). To find when the current Policy is indifferent between via J and direct, solve \(5.3-0.9\varepsilon=5\). Subtract 5: \(0.3=0.9\varepsilon\). Divide by 0.9: \(\boxed{\varepsilon=1/3\approx0.3333}\). Above one third, direct has larger expected value under this exploratory continuation Policy; below it, via J has larger value.

Check \(\varepsilon=.2\): via J \(5.3-.9(.2)=5.12>5\), so it wins. Check \(\varepsilon=.5\): via J \(5.3-.9(.5)=4.85<5\), so direct wins. The threshold depends on the invented Rewards and gamma; it is not a universal cutoff for SARSA.

15. Follow three SARSA episodes and every intermediate operation

Start with all Q-values equal to zero, \(\alpha=0.5\), \(\gamma=.9\), and use the explicitly selected episodes below. These are teaching transitions, not a random performance sample. For terminal actions, the next-value contribution is zero. We update online immediately after each observed transition.

Episode Transition and selected next action TD target Old Q New Q
1a D via J, r=−1, next express (Q=0) −1+0.9×0=−1 0 −0.5
1b J express, r=+7, terminal 7 0 3.5
2a D via J, r=−1, next standard (Q=0) −1 −0.5 −0.75
2b J standard, r=+5, terminal 5 0 2.5
3a D via J, r=−1, next express (Q=3.5) −1+3.15=2.15 −0.75 +0.70

Verify episode 3a in full: \(\delta=2.15-(-0.75)=2.90\); \(Q_{\rm new}=-0.75+0.5(2.90)=-0.75+1.45=0.70\). The J express update comes after D's backup, so D uses the old 3.5, not the later 5.25. This is a common source of invisible errors. Three episodes do not demonstrate convergence; their purpose is to establish operational understanding.

16. Q-Learning on those same three episodes

Repeat the three chosen episodes with Q-Learning, zero-initialized Q and \(\alpha=.5\). At episode 1, \(Q(D,via)\) becomes \(0+.5(-1)=-.5\); the first terminal express transition sets \(Q(J,express)=3.5\).

In episode 2, the Agent again takes via J, but will next execute standard. Q-Learning nevertheless uses the current maximum at J: \(\max(3.5,0)=3.5\). Its D target is \(-1+.9(3.5)=2.15\). Old Q is −0.5: \(Q_{new}=-.5+.5[2.15-(-.5)]=-.5+1.325=\boxed{0.825}\). SARSA instead produced −0.75 in episode 2 because its chosen standard action had value zero. J standard then updates to 2.5.

Episode 3 sees \(Q(J,express)=3.5\) and \(Q(J,standard)=2.5\). The Q-Learning D target remains 2.15, so \(Q(D,via)=0.825+.5(2.15-.825)=\boxed{1.4875}\). Then express terminal updates its value from 3.5 to 5.25. The fact that Q-Learning and SARSA use the same experienced sequence but produce different tables establishes the algorithmic distinction. These three demonstrations are not evidence of eventual accuracy.

17. Monte Carlo Return versus TD Bootstrapping

Monte Carlo Learning waits for the completed episode's observed Return; Temporal-Difference Learning uses an immediate Reward plus a current estimate of future value. Using an estimate within another estimate is called Bootstrapping. The two approaches learn from experience, but use information at different times.

For D→J (−1), J→T express (+7), the full observed Return at D is \(G=-1+.9(7)=5.3\). With old Q(D,via)=2 and \(\alpha=.5\), a Monte Carlo update is \(2+.5(5.3-2)=3.65\). By contrast, if SARSA selected standard at J with current estimate 5, its immediate TD target is 3.5 and update is 2.75; the later actual Reward is not yet available. These results answer different update questions, not contradictory arithmetic.

On terminal steps, no future-value estimate exists: \(Q(T,\cdot)=0\) is a terminal convention rather than an actionable pair. For a J→T express reward +7, SARSA and Q-Learning both use target 7. Ordinary MC methods generally require completed episodes; TD methods can update during an ongoing trajectory. Neither description alone determines their statistical bias, variance, or superiority in every environment.

18. Learning Rate alpha: calculate the sensitivity

For old Q=2 and Q-Learning target 5.3, \(Q_{new}=2+\alpha(5.3-2)=2+3.3\alpha\). Thus alpha controls the size of the update, while the TD Error measures the new information relative to the estimate.

\(\alpha\) Explicit substitution New Q
0 2+0×3.3 2.000
0.1 2+0.1×3.3 2.330
0.5 2+0.5×3.3 3.650
1.0 2+1×3.3 5.300

We include alpha=0 solely as the no-learning boundary case, not a useful training choice. A larger alpha reacts quickly but can make noisy updates fluctuate; a smaller alpha is more conservative but slower to track a changed environment. Alpha does not change which immediate Reward was observed. It changes how much of the current target is incorporated in the Q estimate.

19. Epsilon schedules and their practical meaning

A fixed epsilon keeps exploring indefinitely. A decay schedule reduces epsilon gradually. For example, \(\varepsilon_k=\max(0.05,0.5\times0.99^k)\) starts at 0.5 and approaches the floor 0.05. It never becomes zero, so it does not satisfy every theorem that requires a behavior Policy becoming greedy in the limit.

At epsilon 0.05, express is chosen at J with probability \(1-.05/2=.975\); standard with .025. The expected Reward at J is \(.975(7)+.025(5)=6.825+.125=6.95\). Hence via J under this exploratory continuation earns \(-1+.9(6.95)=5.255\), above direct=5. At epsilon=.5, the corresponding route value was 4.85, below direct. This verifies the switching behavior numerically.

Decay too quickly and an action may never receive enough visits for its Q estimate to become useful. Decay too slowly and actions deliberately chosen for information can impose persistent operational costs. A training Exploration schedule must be documented separately from the evaluation Policy: changing epsilon at test time changes the Policy being evaluated.

20. Initialization, coverage and tie-breaking

When all Q entries start at zero, the initial greedy actions can tie. One reproducible convention is random tie-breaking among maximizing actions. Always choosing the first action in a hard-coded list can distort early visit counts. In the manual episodes, the next action is explicitly provided, so tie-breaking cannot secretly change a displayed calculation.

Optimistic Initialization gives untried actions a high initial value so the learner may inspect them. An Action initialized to 10 can attract exploration when other estimates are near 5, but this is not universally safe: an untried maneuver could cause substantial real damage. Initial values are assumptions, not prior observations.

A coverage audit counts State–Action visits. Our toy example has four valid pairs: (D,direct), (D,via), (J,express), (J,standard). If (J,standard) is never visited, its displayed zero is an initialization, not evidence that the action is worthless. In large or continuous spaces, complete coverage may be impossible, so the scope of observed experience must be reported honestly.

21. Convergence assumptions: what is actually guaranteed?

Tabular Q-Learning has convergence results for suitable finite stationary MDPs when Rewards are bounded, State–Action pairs receive enough visits, and step sizes decay appropriately. A classical sufficient step-size pattern for each visited pair uses \(\sum_n\alpha_n=\infty\) and \(\sum_n\alpha_n^2<\infty\). These equations express continuing learning without accumulating unlimited squared update noise.

For SARSA, convergence to optimal greedy behavior can require adequate exploration while epsilon approaches zero, often described as GLIE (Greedy in the Limit with Infinite Exploration) plus suitable Learning Rates. If epsilon stays at 0.5 forever, the ongoing SARSA Policy does not become greedy, and its On-Policy values need not equal the optimal greedy Q-values. This explains our 4.85-versus-5.3 distinction.

These are conditional mathematical results, not proof that a particular run will converge after 100 episodes. They should not be transferred without examination to Neural Network Function Approximation, changing reward systems, invalid Markov States, uncontrolled exploration or correlated Offline data. The hypotheses are part of the statement of the result.

22. Evaluation of greedy and exploratory Policies must be separated

A training Behavior Policy chooses actions to collect experience; a final Evaluation Policy must be specified and frozen before measuring performance. A greedy Policy sets epsilon=0. A Behavior Policy with epsilon=.5 still chooses exploratory actions. These are different Policies and must not share an unqualified performance score.

Our optimal greedy route is via J followed by express and yields 5.3. Under constant epsilon=.5 SARSA, the greedy action at D can be direct, yielding 5 when deployed without exploration. If the learned SARSA Behavior Policy is instead executed with epsilon=.5 at D, it chooses greedy direct with probability .75 and via J with .25. The via-J value includes exploration at J and equals 4.85; therefore its full exploratory starting value is \(0.75(5)+0.25(4.85)=3.75+1.2125=\boxed{4.9625}\). This is neither 5 nor 5.3.

To compare algorithms credibly, define both the training and testing Policies, report epsilon, number of episodes and Random Seeds, and evaluate on independent scenarios not repeatedly reused for Hyperparameter tuning. A finite simulator may produce highly accurate arithmetic while misrepresenting real warehouse conditions.

23. Stochastic rewards: expectation is not a safety guarantee

Our main teaching MDP is deterministic. For a separate synthetic sensitivity case, let express at J produce +10 with probability .9 and −20 with probability .1, keeping standard at +5. The express Expected Reward becomes \(0.9(10)+0.1(-20)=9-2=\boxed{7}\), numerically identical to deterministic express (+7). A risk-neutral optimal planner still chooses via J and express because the expected D Return remains \(-1+.9(7)=5.3\).

But the possible realized via-J Returns are \(-1+.9(10)=8\) after success and \(-1+.9(-20)=-19\) after failure. Verify the average: \(.9(8)+.1(-19)=7.2-1.9=5.3\). Equal means do not imply equal risk; a warehouse manager might reasonably impose a maximum-loss constraint that rules out express, but that is a different optimization problem.

This stochastic extension is not silently mixed into the main laboratory's deterministic samples. When Rewards are random, Q estimates may be noisy and their reported variability matters. Negative outcomes may also be ethically or operationally unacceptable even when expected utility is positive.

24. Noisy maxima and the motivation for Double Q-Learning

Q-Learning backs up \(\max_a Q(s',a)\). If Q estimates contain noise, selecting the highest estimate can also select an unusually positive estimation error. Consider two actions both worth 5 in truth, temporarily estimated as 7 and 3. The maximum estimate is 7, exceeding the correct maximum by 2. This arithmetic example does not establish a universal bias size; it shows the mechanism.

Double Q-Learning separates selection and evaluation across two tables. One update can select \(a^*=\arg\max_a Q_A(s',a)\) but evaluate it using \(Q_B(s',a^*)\). If table A estimates (7,3) and B estimates (4,6), A selects action 1 but B assigns that action value 4; the update need not reuse A's maximum 7. Updates may alternate which table is trained.

This idea motivates Double DQN when Q-values are estimated by Neural Networks. Yet one example with two tables does not prove that every Double Q estimate is unbiased. The important distinction is which estimator selects the action and which estimator evaluates it, another version of the careful policy/target separation learned earlier.

25. Reward misspecification and discount sensitivity

A learner optimizes the numeric Reward specified by its designer, not a human objective that was left unstated. A real logistics Reward might reflect time, fuel, damage, safety and customer consequences. Combining these into utility points requires documented weights, constraints and units. If damaged goods receive no penalty, an apparently profitable route could systematically ignore a substantial real cost.

Discounting can also reverse the choice. Our optimal via-J trajectory is \(-1+7\gamma\) and direct is 5. Set them equal: \(-1+7\gamma=5\). Adding 1 gives \(7\gamma=6\); divide by 7: \(\boxed{\gamma=6/7\approx0.85714}\). At gamma=.8, via J gives \(-1+5.6=4.6<5\); at gamma=.9, it gives 5.3>5.

The formula's result says nothing about the correct ethical or financial discount factor: gamma is a modeling choice reflecting the task's horizon and objectives. Nor does a high simulated Reward prove a safer business process. Changing reward weights or gamma repeatedly after looking at held-out test outcomes would invalidate a claim of independent evaluation.

26. From a four-entry Q table to Deep Q-Networks

Our tabular environment has only four State–Action pairs. Real systems may have thousands of State Features, continuous quantities and many actions, making a table impractical. Function Approximation estimates \(Q_\theta(s,a)\) using learned parameters \(\theta\), often via Neural Networks.

A Deep Q-Network (DQN) fits predicted Action Values to bootstrapped Targets. An illustrative squared TD objective is \(L(\theta)=[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\theta^-}(s',a')-Q_\theta(s,a)]^2\), where \(\theta^-\) represents a Target Network frozen for an interval of training. Experience Replay samples stored transitions rather than relying only on immediately consecutive ones; it can reduce correlations but does not magically make all experience independent.

DQN adds challenges absent in the four-number example: approximator bias, optimization instability, moving Targets, network architecture, memory and Generalization. The mathematical distinction between \(Q(s',a')\) (SARSA) and \(\max_a Q(s',a)\) (Q-Learning) must be understood first. Standard DQN also assumes discrete actions; continuous control often requires other algorithm families.

27. Executable lab I: exact references and hand traces

This first laboratory uses only Python's standard library. Paste it into an empty Jupyter cell. It reconstructs the optimal Values, the two one-step backup calculations and every update of our hand-selected three-episode SARSA and Q-Learning traces. No file downloads, global notebook variables or hidden data are required.

# Standalone laboratory I: exact arithmetic and selected trajectories
GAMMA = 0.9
ALPHA = 0.5
q_j_express, q_j_standard = 7.0, 5.0
optimal_via = -1 + GAMMA * max(q_j_express, q_j_standard)
onpolicy_via = -1 + GAMMA * (0.75*q_j_express + 0.25*q_j_standard)
print(f"Greedy via-J value = {optimal_via:.6f}")
print(f"Epsilon=.5 on-policy via-J value = {onpolicy_via:.6f}")
assert abs(optimal_via - 5.3) < 1e-12
assert abs(onpolicy_via - 4.85) < 1e-12

old_q = 2.0
qlearn_target = -1 + GAMMA * max(7, 5)
sarsa_target = -1 + GAMMA * 5
qlearn_new = old_q + ALPHA*(qlearn_target-old_q)
sarsa_new = old_q + ALPHA*(sarsa_target-old_q)
expected_sarsa_new = old_q + ALPHA*(onpolicy_via-old_q)
print(f"Q-Learning sample update = {qlearn_new:.6f}")
print(f"SARSA sample update = {sarsa_new:.6f}")
print(f"Expected SARSA update = {expected_sarsa_new:.6f}")
assert abs(qlearn_new-3.65)<1e-12
assert abs(sarsa_new-2.75)<1e-12
assert abs(expected_sarsa_new-3.425)<1e-12

# Every episode fixes its actions to make the trace auditable.
episodes = [('via','express'), ('via','standard'), ('via','express')]
for method in ('SARSA','Q-Learning'):
    q = {('D','direct'):0., ('D','via'):0.,
         ('J','express'):0., ('J','standard'):0.}
    print('\n',method)
    for i,(_, next_action) in enumerate(episodes,1):
        if method == 'SARSA':
            boot = q['J',next_action]
        else:
            boot = max(q['J','express'],q['J','standard'])
        target = -1 + GAMMA*boot
        q['D','via'] += ALPHA*(target-q['D','via'])
        # The chosen terminal action is learned only AFTER the D update.
        reward = 7 if next_action == 'express' else 5
        q['J',next_action] += ALPHA*(reward-q['J',next_action])
        print(f"episode {i}: D/via={q['D','via']:.4f}; "
              f"J/express={q['J','express']:.4f}; "
              f"J/standard={q['J','standard']:.4f}")
    correct = 0.70 if method=='SARSA' else 1.4875
    assert abs(q['D','via'] - correct)<1e-12

The program's assertions check exact reference values and intermediate updates. Transfer check: set the single-update Learning Rate to .1. For Q-Learning, \(2+.1(5.3-2)=2.33\); for SARSA after selecting standard, \(2+.1(3.5-2)=2.15\). Their difference is 0.18 instead of 0.90 because alpha was divided by five while the two TD targets stayed fixed.

28. Executable lab II: learn values from sampled episodes

The next laboratory actually trains two tabular Agents, instead of returning only reference formulas. Both use the same deterministic four-action environment, gamma .9, epsilon .5 and fixed random seeds. A per-State–Action update step of \(\alpha_n=1/n\) uses the visit count, so repeated evidence is averaged rather than indefinitely overweighting recent samples.

import random
GAMMA=0.9
EPSILON=0.5
ACTIONS={'D':('direct','via'), 'J':('express','standard')}

def run(algorithm,seed=300,episodes=100000):
    rng=random.Random(seed)
    Q={(s,a):0.0 for s in ACTIONS for a in ACTIONS[s]}
    N={(s,a):0 for s,a in Q}
    def choose(s):
        actions=ACTIONS[s]
        if rng.random()<EPSILON: return rng.choice(actions)
        top=max(Q[s,a] for a in actions)
        options=[a for a in actions if abs(Q[s,a]-top)<1e-12]
        return rng.choice(options)
    for _ in range(episodes):
        state='D'; action=choose(state)
        while True:
            reward, next_state = ({'direct':(5,'T'),'via':(-1,'J')}
                                    if state=='D' else
                                    {'express':(7,'T'),'standard':(5,'T')})[action]
            next_action=choose(next_state) if next_state!='T' else None
            bootstrap=0.0 if next_state=='T' else (
                Q[next_state,next_action] if algorithm=='SARSA'
                else max(Q[next_state,a] for a in ACTIONS[next_state]))
            key=(state,action);N[key]+=1
            alpha=1.0/N[key]
            Q[key]+=alpha*(reward+GAMMA*bootstrap-Q[key])
            if next_state=='T': break
            state,action=next_state,next_action
    return Q,N

for method in ('Q-Learning','SARSA'):
    Q,N=run(method)
    print(method,[(k,round(Q[k],6),N[k]) for k in Q])
print('reference greedy 5.3 / ongoing behavior 4.85')

Inspect visit counts as well as Q-values: an unvisited pair is not an empirically supported estimate. Q-Learning's learned greedy backup for via J should approach 5.3, while SARSA's On-Policy value under continuing epsilon=.5 should approach 4.85, subject to finite-sample fluctuations and the algorithm's learning conditions. These results concern a synthetic deterministic environment and must not be advertised as validation in a warehouse.

Interpretation challenge: explain why changing epsilon from 0.5 to zero during evaluation changes which Policy is being measured. If the algorithm's final Q table contains a high value for via J, that alone does not tell us how often the actual Behavior Policy chose standard at J.

Laboratory III — Inventory replenishment: from one update to repeated-run learning curves

Why a second application? The depot example isolates the difference between On-Policy and Off-Policy TD backups. We now transfer exactly those ideas to a synthetic inventory simulator, the second application specified by the course Blueprint. The three stock states, legal orders and random customer demands are not observations from any store. They let us test arithmetic, reproducibility and generalization of reasoning—not claim an operating profit.

State and action. At the beginning of a day, inventory \(s\in\{0,1,2\}\) units remains. Order \(a\in\{0,\ldots,2-s\}\) units, which arrive before demand; the shelf capacity is exactly two units. The legal State–Action pairs are \((0,0),(0,1),(0,2),(1,0),(1,1),(2,0)\). A stock of two cannot order a third unit. The next state is the number left after sales. We assume successive daily demands are independent of each other and of actions, with \(P(d=0)=0.2,\ P(d=1)=0.5,\ P(d=2)=0.3\). These probabilities describe the simulator for the analyst; the learning agent observes individual demands rather than receiving their probability table.

Synthetic inventory environment: all amounts are US dollars per day, except counts and probabilities
QuantityDefined valueWhy it matters
Sale revenue$7 per unit soldBenefits successful fulfillment
Order cost$1 fixed if \(a>0\), plus $1.50 per ordered unitAvoids cost-free replenishment
Holding cost$1 per leftover unitPenalizes stock carried to tomorrow
Lost-sales penalty$5 per unit of unmet demandPenalizes stockouts beyond lost revenue
Learning\(\gamma=0.9,\ \varepsilon=0.2,\ \alpha_n=n^{-0.6}\)Discount, exploration and pair-specific learning rate

To compute a reward, first calculate \(x=s+a\) available units, sales \(u=\min(x,d)\), remaining stock \(s'=x-u\) and lost demand \(\ell=d-u\). The immediate contribution, in dollars, is

\[r=7u-\left(\mathbf 1_{a>0}+1.5a\right)-1(s')-5\ell .\]

The indicator \(\mathbf 1_{a>0}\) equals 1 when an order is placed and 0 otherwise. Consider opening stock 0, ordering 1 and demand 1. Available stock is \(0+1=1\); sales \(\min(1,1)=1\); leftover \(1-1=0\); lost sales \(1-1=0\). Therefore \(r=7(1)-(1+1.5(1))-1(0)-5(0)=7-2.5=\boxed{4.5}\) dollars and next stock is 0. Starting from all-zero Q-values, the first Q-Learning update for this pair uses \(n=1,\alpha_1=1\), TD target \(4.5+0.9\max_b0=4.5\), TD Error \(4.5-0=4.5\) and updated \(Q(0,1)=0+1(4.5)=\boxed{4.5}\). This is one sampled update, not the long-run value of that action.

Independently check the stochastic Reward. Keep opening stock 0 and order 1 but change demand. For demand 0: nothing sells; one unit remains; \(r=0-2.5-1= -3.5\). For demand 1: \(r=4.5\). For demand 2: sell one, lose one; \(r=7-2.5-5=-0.5\). The expected immediate Reward is \(0.2(-3.5)+0.5(4.5)+0.3(-0.5)=-0.7+2.25-0.15=\boxed{1.4}\) dollars. It is not the Q-value: Q also discounts later stock-dependent consequences. Observe how knowing the true demand probabilities is useful for our audit but not required by either model-free learning algorithm.

Evaluation contract. Eight independent training seeds (310–317) each generate 3,000 simulated episodes for each algorithm, with 15 days per episode; the initial stock is reset to 1 at each training episode boundary. The underlying discounted task is continuing: a reset here is an artificial rollout truncation, not a terminal state. Consequently, even on day 15 the TD update bootstraps from the next stock value; incorrectly setting that value to zero would change the task. The two algorithms receive the same demand stream for a given seed, while their exploratory action streams and trajectories can differ. All six legal action pairs can be explored. We use \(\alpha_n=n^{-0.6}\) where \(n\) counts visits to a particular pair, rather than applying one global count.

At checkpoints 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 100, 400, 1,200 and 3,000, we freeze the current Q-table and evaluate its Greedy Policy, using no exploratory actions and no learning updates. All checkpoints use the same 200 reserved synthetic demand paths (12 days each, initialized with stock 1); they are not fed into training. The plotted metric is the arithmetic mean of the undiscounted dollar contribution per evaluation day. Training still optimizes the discounted Return at \(\gamma=0.9\); the evaluation metric is an operational summary and is not itself the TD target. Reusing these scenarios for repeated reporting is transparent but does not constitute fresh, independent tests after choosing among checkpoints.

Here is a compact numerical check from the actual saved runs. Values below are averages across eight seeds, in dollars per evaluation day; the ± values are sample standard deviations. At episode 0, both methods have the deterministic baseline \(-4.425\). At episode 1, Q-Learning has \(3.26930\pm0.63648\) and SARSA \(3.22443\pm0.84850\). At episode 25, both average \(4.13734\pm0.22200\). From episode 100 onward, both score \(4.21583\) with zero variation in this finite set of evaluated Greedy policies; zero variation does not mean the Q estimates or future operating outcomes are certain.

Why SARSA and Q-Learning differ numerically. In the final table for seed 310, Q-Learning estimates \(Q(0,2)\approx41.725\) discounted dollars, while SARSA estimates \(Q(0,2)\approx38.390\). SARSA samples the next \(\varepsilon=0.2\) behavior action in its backup; Q-Learning uses the highest-valued legal next action. The two policies agree here because ordering two units from zero stock ranks first in both tables. This single simple simulator does not establish that one algorithm dominates another in general.

Reproducible standalone laboratory. This Python cell relies only on the standard library, fixes every random seed and reconstructs the plotted learning curves and heatmaps. It prints the same checkpoints and seed-310 Q-tables used for the SVGs; plotting is not required for the numerical test. With stochastic rewards, repeating only one trajectory is inadequate evidence of stability.

import random, statistics
GAMMA = 0.9
EPSILON = 0.2
TRAIN_DAYS = 15
EVAL_DAYS = 12
CHECKPOINTS = (0,1,2,5,10,25,100,400,1200,3000)
SEEDS = tuple(range(310,318))
ACTIONS = {0:(0,1,2),1:(0,1),2:(0,)}

def inventory_step(stock, order, demand):
    assert order in ACTIONS[stock] and demand in (0,1,2)
    available=stock+order
    sales=min(available,demand)
    remaining=available-sales
    lost=demand-sales
    order_cost=(1+1.5*order) if order else 0.0
    reward=7.0*sales-order_cost-remaining-5.0*lost
    return remaining,reward

def demand_from(rng):
    u=rng.random()
    return 0 if u<0.2 else 1 if u<0.7 else 2

def choose(stock,q,rng):
    a=ACTIONS[stock]
    if rng.random()<EPSILON:return rng.choice(a)
    mx=max(q[(stock,act)] for act in a)
    ties=[act for act in a if abs(q[(stock,act)]-mx)<1e-10]
    return rng.choice(ties)

# Frozen, common, unseen evaluation demand traces; no training updates.
evaluation_paths=[]
for j in range(200):
    eval_rng=random.Random(100_000+j)
    evaluation_paths.append([demand_from(eval_rng) for _ in range(EVAL_DAYS)])

def greedy_action(stock,q):
    return max(ACTIONS[stock], key=lambda a:(q[(stock,a)],-a))

def score(q):
    total=0.0
    for path in evaluation_paths:
        stock=1
        for demand in path:
            stock,reward=inventory_step(stock,greedy_action(stock,q),demand)
            total+=reward
    return total/(len(evaluation_paths)*EVAL_DAYS)

def train(method,seed):
    q={(stock,action):0.0 for stock,actions in ACTIONS.items() for action in actions}
    visits={k:0 for k in q}
    act_rng=random.Random(seed)
    demand_rng=random.Random(seed+10000)
    scores={0:score(q)}
    history={0:q.copy()}
    for episode in range(1,max(CHECKPOINTS)+1):
        stock=1
        action=choose(stock,q,act_rng)
        for day in range(TRAIN_DAYS):
            demand=demand_from(demand_rng)
            next_stock,reward=inventory_step(stock,action,demand)
            next_action=choose(next_stock,q,act_rng)
            bootstrap=(q[(next_stock,next_action)] if method=='SARSA' else
                       max(q[(next_stock,a)] for a in ACTIONS[next_stock]))
            k=(stock,action)
            visits[k]+=1
            alpha=visits[k]**(-0.6)
            q[k]+=alpha*(reward+GAMMA*bootstrap-q[k])
            stock,action=next_stock,next_action
        if episode in CHECKPOINTS:
            scores[episode]=score(q)
            history[episode]=q.copy()
    return scores,history,visits

runs={m:[train(m,seed) for seed in SEEDS]

All values are synthetic dollar-denominated contributions; they omit real supplier lead times, perishability, backorders, nonstationary demand and service-level constraints. We assume immediate fulfillment and a shelf capacity of two. The environment probabilities and costs must be revalidated in any operational application. A Greedy evaluation with fixed paths is a didactic comparison, not an online A/B test or permission to deploy ordering decisions. If the arrival lead time became two days, stock alone would no longer be a sufficient State: the Agent would also need outstanding orders and their arrival times.

29. Scientific limitations, safety and Offline evaluation

Historical Offline RL logs were produced by an existing Behavior Policy; they are not necessarily evidence for actions never taken. If standard was never tried at J, its outcome is unsupported by those logs. The mathematically undefined ratio \(\pi_{\rm target}(a|s)/\pi_{\rm behavior}(a|s)\) when the behavior probability is zero illustrates a basic coverage limitation of importance-weighted Off-Policy Evaluation. Missing support is not repaired merely by running more optimizer iterations.

Before any real deployment, specify safety constraints, maximum acceptable loss, human overrides, emergency fallbacks and representative evaluation scenarios. Distinguish the validity of a hand calculation, successful simulation, statistical generalization from logs and measured physical-world benefit. Each requires separate evidence. Our logistics Rewards are synthetic and intentionally simple; no claim about actual parcel-handling effectiveness follows.

Student transfer: suppose standard is the only route legally permitted for hazardous cargo. The optimal feasible Policy at J is then standard, even though its Reward 5 is lower than express 7. This is a constrained action set, not a mathematical contradiction: maximizing over allowed actions changes the Bellman backup. The corresponding D via-J value is \(-1+0.9(5)=3.5\), so direct=5 wins if direct is also permitted.

31. Nineteen solved exercises and the final Student Understanding Test

Work through the questions before opening their fully reasoned solutions. The first group reconstructs basic quantities, the middle group compares algorithms, and the final group changes assumptions, constraints and evaluation conditions. Merely memorizing the labels “On-Policy” and “Off-Policy” is not sufficient to pass this assessment.

1. Identify the nonterminal States and total valid State–Action pairs.

D allows direct and via J; J allows express and standard. Therefore 2+2=4 valid pairs. T is terminal, with no action and value zero by convention.

2. Compute the via-J express Return for gamma=.9.

Reward −1 occurs now, express +7 occurs one step later: −1+.9×7=−1+6.3=5.3 points. Do not multiply −1 by .9.

3. Calculate the via-J standard Return.

−1+.9×5=−1+4.5=3.5. Since direct is 5, via J followed by standard is 1.5 points worse than direct.

4. Compute epsilon-greedy probabilities at J for epsilon=.5.

Exploit probability .5 plus random branch .5×1/2=.25 yields express .75; standard receives .25. Their sum is 1.

5. Compute the expected J Reward under that behavior Policy.

0.75×7+0.25×5=5.25+1.25=6.5 utility points; this is .5 below always choosing express.

6. Work the SARSA transition D→J with Q(D,via)=2 and sampled next standard Q=5.

Target=−1+.9×5=3.5; TD Error=3.5−2=1.5; new Q=2+.5×1.5=2.75.

7. Work Q-Learning from the same transition and compare.

Target=−1+.9×max(7,5)=5.3; TD Error=5.3−2=3.3; new Q=2+.5×3.3=3.65; difference from SARSA is .90.

8. Compute Expected SARSA new Q without confusing it with sampled SARSA.

Expected J value=6.5, target=−1+.9×6.5=4.85; updated Q=2+.5×(4.85−2)=3.425, equal to .75×3.65+.25×2.75.

9. Derive the epsilon threshold when via J equals direct=5.

Via J expected=5.3−.9 epsilon. Equate to 5: .9 epsilon=.3, hence epsilon=1/3. For .5 prefer direct; for .2 prefer via J.

10. Calculate the SARSA episode-3a update with old D Q=−.75 and J express=3.5.

TD target=−1+.9×3.5=2.15. TD error=2.15−(−.75)=2.90. New D Q=−.75+.5×2.90=.70.

11. Calculate Q-Learning episode-2 D value from old −.5 and J estimates 3.5,0.

Target=−1+.9×3.5=2.15. New D Q=−.5+.5[2.15−(−.5)]=−.5+1.325=.825.

12. Compute the starting expected Return of ongoing epsilon=.5 SARSA behavior.

At D greedy direct probability .75, exploratory via probability .25. Via-J continuation value is 4.85. Total .75×5+.25×4.85=3.75+1.2125=4.9625.

13. Transfer: change gamma to .8. Which greedy route wins?

Via express=−1+.8×7=−1+5.6=4.6; direct=5. Direct wins by .4. Gamma matters because express reward is delayed.

14. Transfer: make express +10 with probability .9 and −20 with probability .1.

Mean J express Reward=.9×10+.1×(−20)=7. Via D expected return=−1+.9×7=5.3, but realized returns are 8 and −19: matching expectation is not matching risk.

15. Transfer: express becomes prohibited for hazardous cargo. What is the optimal feasible first action?

At J only standard=5 is allowed, so via J=−1+.9×5=3.5. Direct=5 wins if direct is legal. The action feasibility restriction changes the maximum.

16. Why might Offline Q-Learning fail for an unobserved action and how would you evaluate safely?

Unobserved actions have no supported outcome in the logs; an importance-weight ratio is undefined if behavior probability is zero. Collect authorized coverage or constrain actions, validate the simulator, separate training and evaluation, and use human safety oversight.

17. Transfer: compute the entire one-day stock transition and the first Q update.

Opening stock 0, order 1 and demand 1 give available \(0+1=1\), sales \(\min(1,1)=1\), closing stock \(1-1=0\) and lost demand 0. Revenue is $7, order cost \(1+1.5=2.5\), holding and stockout costs are zero, so immediate reward is $4.5. For the first visit \(\alpha_1=1\), old \(Q(0,1)=0\) and next Q maximum zero, Target is \(4.5+0.9(0)=4.5\). Updated Q is \(0+1(4.5-0)=4.5\). It is one sample, not expected long-run return.

18. Transfer: change the cost of lost demand to $8. Recalculate the expected immediate reward of ordering one unit with stock zero.

For demand 0 the reward remains −$3.5 (one leftover and $2.50 order cost). For demand 1 it remains $4.5. For demand 2 one item sells and one is lost: \(7-2.5-8=-3.5\). Weight by probabilities \(0.2(-3.5)+0.5(4.5)+0.3(-3.5)=-0.7+2.25-1.05=\boxed{0.50}\) dollars; previously it was $1.40. A larger unmet-demand cost reduces this action's expected immediate Reward by $0.90 but does not alone prove that the same optimal long-term order remains best: future inventory and the competing orders must also be evaluated.

19. Why can equal Greedy evaluation scores coexist with unequal Q estimates, and what changes if lead time becomes two days?

Greedy evaluation uses only the highest-ranked legal action at each state. For seed 310, Q-Learning estimates \(Q(0,2)\approx41.725\) and SARSA approximately 38.390, but both rank ordering two first when stock is zero; the final Greedy actions at stocks 0, 1 and 2 are 2, 1 and 0. The fixed evaluation paths consequently yield the same average. The Q tables have different training targets: maximum next Q for Q-Learning, sampled exploratory next action for SARSA. With two-day lead time, state must track outstanding orders and expected arrivals, not just on-hand units. Otherwise the Markov assumption fails and the current six-pair table cannot represent the real problem.

Final Student Understanding Test. Can you explain (1) the MDP and its Rewards, (2) why \(-1+.9(7)\) discounts only the second Reward, (3) how SARSA updates to 2.75 while Q-Learning updates to 3.65 on the same transition, (4) why exploration epsilon=0.5 produces an on-policy via-J value of 4.85, (5) what changes at epsilon one third, and (6) why these simulated results do not certify real-world safety? You should be able to reproduce the arithmetic and state the limitations with your own words.

A final principle carries into Deep Learning and AI: separate the Behavior Policy, the Target used for learning, the objective being optimized and the Policy actually evaluated. Algorithms cannot compensate for an undefined objective or invalid evidence.
