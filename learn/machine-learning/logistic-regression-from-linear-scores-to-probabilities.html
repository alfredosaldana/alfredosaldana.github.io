1. Start with a decision: will a hotel booking be cancelled?

A hotel receives reservations long before some guests arrive. A cancellation may leave a room unsold, but contacting every guest is expensive and sometimes intrusive. The learning task is not “predict whether a guest is good or bad.” It is: at the time a reservation is confirmed, estimate the probability that this booking will be cancelled before check-in, using information actually available at confirmation. The prediction may help plan staffing or review appropriate, consent-based reminders. It does not establish that sending a reminder prevents a cancellation.

This is Binary Classification: the Target \(y\) equals 1 for a booking cancelled before check-in and 0 otherwise. The output of Logistic Regression is an estimated probability \(\hat p=P(y=1\mid x)\), not directly a class label. A later Decision Threshold turns it into an operational classification. We must keep these stages separate: predict risk → evaluate it → choose an action. The model can only be assessed after every booking's cancellation outcome has matured. Booking confirmation is the prediction timestamp, so post-booking changes, future customer messages, and final cancellation status must never become predictor variables for that original prediction.

Our worked dataset is deliberately small and synthetic, enabling a reader to check every calculation. It is not representative of hotel booking cancellation rates, and its hypothetical costs do not measure actual business benefits. The practical questions to keep asking are: What information exists when the prediction is made? What precisely counts as cancellation? What does a probability mean? How do errors affect guests and operations?

3. Why not just use Linear Regression for a binary outcome?

Recall Linear Regression: \(\hat y=b_0+b_1x\). A straight line can take any real value. With a binary Target, a score of 1.3 or −0.2 cannot be interpreted literally as a probability, because valid probabilities must lie between 0 and 1. Ordinary Least Squares may still have uses for binary outcomes (the linear probability model), but it does not enforce that probability bound and requires careful interpretation. Logistic Regression keeps the linear combination of Features while transforming it into a probability.

For illustration, imagine a provisional score \(z=-2+1.1x\). At \(x=0\), it gives \(z=-2\); at \(x=4\), it gives \(z=2.4\). These are scores, not negative or greater-than-one probabilities. The Sigmoid Function maps every real score into the interval (0,1), allowing a consistent probabilistic interpretation when the model is appropriately specified and its probabilities have been evaluated. The later MLE example will use coefficients actually learned from Training, not these provisional values.

The word “Regression” in Logistic Regression refers to a parametric model for the log-odds, not to predicting an unrestricted continuous Target as in ordinary Linear Regression. The standard application here is binary classification with a probability output. In both approaches Features have coefficients; what differs is the assumed relationship between the linear score and the predicted Target.

4. Build the Linear Score carefully

For one Feature, the model begins with \(z=b_0+b_1x\). Here \(b_0\) is the Intercept, the score when \(x=0\); \(b_1\) is the change in score for a one-unit increase in \(x\); and \(z\) is an unrestricted real number. Since \(x\) counts 30-day lead-time units, a one-unit increase corresponds to approximately 30 additional days. Neither coefficient itself is a probability.

We will eventually estimate the coefficients from the 12 Training bookings. The fitted values (rounded for display) are \(\hat b_0=-2.222688\) and \(\hat b_1=1.127565\). These are the result of optimization, not numbers we are asking you to guess; Sections 9–11 explain how the algorithm learns them and checks its answer.

For a booking with \(x=2\) (60 days before check-in), substitute every value:

\[z=-2.222688+1.127565(2)=-2.222688+2.255130\approx0.032442.\]

The score \(0.032442\) is close to zero. It is not a 3.2442% cancellation probability. Interpreting scores as percentages is a common mistake: we must first apply the Sigmoid. A change in the original unit of \(x\), such as representing lead time in days instead of 30-day increments, also changes the numerical coefficient needed to describe the same predictive function.

5. The Sigmoid: derive, calculate and interpret probabilities

The Sigmoid Function, also called the logistic function, is

\[\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},\qquad \hat p=\sigma(b_0+b_1x).\]

The number \(e\approx2.71828\) is the base of natural logarithms. For any real \(z\), \(e^{-z}>0\), so the denominator exceeds 1 and the result lies strictly between 0 and 1. When \(z=0\), \(\sigma(0)=1/(1+1)=0.5\). Very negative scores approach 0, and very positive scores approach 1, without becoming exactly 0 or 1 at finite scores.

For our \(x=2\) booking, the completed calculation is:

\[\hat p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-0.032442}}\approx\frac{1}{1+0.968079}\approx0.508110.\]

Interpretation: the fitted model assigns approximately a 50.81% cancellation probability to bookings with this lead time. It does not assert that this particular booking is certain to cancel; nor does one example establish that predicted probabilities are well calibrated. For a whole group of bookings assigned probabilities near 0.51, calibration asks whether approximately 51% actually cancel over the defined horizon.

Do not skip the derivative that powers learning

Differentiate Sigmoid explicitly, rather than treating its derivative as a memorized identity. With \(u(z)=1+e^{-z}\), the Power Rule and Chain Rule give \(d(u^{-1})/dz=-u^{-2}du/dz\), and \(du/dz=-e^{-z}\). Hence

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d\sigma(z)}{dz}&=-(1+e^{-z})^{-2}(-e^{-z})\\&=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}\\&=\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}}_{\sigma(z)}\underbrace{\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}}_{1-\sigma(z)}\\&=\boxed{\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))}.\end{aligned}\]

At \(z=0\), this derivative is \(0.5(1-0.5)=0.25\): near the center, a small score change of 0.1 changes probability by approximately \(0.25(0.1)=0.025\). This is a local linear approximation, not an exact 2.5-percentage-point rule for every score. For very positive or negative scores the derivative approaches zero; the S-shaped graph flattens. Section 10 uses precisely this derivative to learn the model coefficients.

6. Probability, odds and log-odds are different quantities

If the estimated probability of cancellation is \(p\), then the probability of not cancelling is \(1-p\). The odds of cancellation are \(p/(1-p)\), while the log-odds, or logit, are the natural logarithm of those odds:

\[\mathrm{odds}=\frac{p}{1-p},\qquad \mathrm{logit}(p)=\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right).\]

Take a hypothetical probability \(p=0.75\). The complementary probability is 0.25, the odds are \(0.75/0.25=3\), and the log-odds are \(\ln3\approx1.0986\). Odds of 3 mean “three to one,” not 300% probability. Inverting the odds gives \(p=3/(1+3)=0.75\). For our actual \(x=2\) score, the odds are \(e^{0.032442}\approx1.03297\), corresponding to \(p\approx0.50811\).

Why use logarithms at all? Odds are positive, but log-odds range over the entire real line, so a linear function can model them:

\[\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)=b_0+b_1x.\]

By exponentiating and solving for \(p\), we recover the Sigmoid. This is the conceptual bridge between a linear score and a probability. It is also the key to interpreting Logistic Regression coefficients without confusing them with changes in probability.

7. Interpret the coefficients without making a causal claim

Our fitted coefficient \(\hat b_1=1.127565\) means that increasing lead time by one 30-day unit increases the model's log-odds of cancellation by approximately 1.127565, holding any other included Features constant. Exponentiating converts that statement to an Odds Ratio:

\[\mathrm{OR}=e^{\hat b_1}=e^{1.127565}\approx3.08813.\]

Thus, under this fitted relationship, a one-unit increase in lead time multiplies the odds by about 3.09. It does not multiply the probability by 3.09. Compare the actual fitted probabilities: at \(x=1\), \(p\approx0.250655\); at \(x=2\), \(p\approx0.508110\). The probability ratio is about 2.03, while the odds ratio is about 3.09. The probability difference, approximately 25.75 percentage points, is also not constant as \(x\) changes.

The Intercept \(-2.222688\) represents the log-odds when \(x=0\). Its corresponding probability is \(\sigma(-2.222688)\approx0.097732\), or 9.77%. This interpretation depends on \(x=0\) being a meaningful observation, as it is in our dataset. The coefficient represents association in the toy data; it does not prove that changing a reservation's lead time would cause its cancellation risk to change. Refund policies, seasonality and customer characteristics could confound the relationship.

8. From individual bookings to Bernoulli Likelihood

A Bernoulli random variable takes values 0 and 1. Conditional on Features, Logistic Regression models \(P(Y_i=1\mid x_i)=p_i\) and \(P(Y_i=0\mid x_i)=1-p_i\). We can write the probability of the observed label \(y_i\) compactly as

\[P(y_i\mid x_i)=p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}.\]

Check both cases rather than memorizing the exponents. If \(y_i=1\), the expression becomes \(p_i^1(1-p_i)^0=p_i\). If \(y_i=0\), it becomes \(p_i^0(1-p_i)^1=1-p_i\). Assuming observations are conditionally independent for this likelihood, the Likelihood of all Training outcomes is the product of these factors:

\[L(b_0,b_1)=\prod_{i=1}^{12}p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}.\]

Compute a miniature likelihood before multiplying 12 factors. Consider just three hypothetical observed rows: a booking with \(x=0,y=0\), one with \(x=1,y=1\), and one with \(x=2,y=0\). Using the fitted probabilities from Section 12, the probability assigned to these three labels is approximately \((1-0.097732)(0.250655)(1-0.508110)\approx0.111245\). The result is a joint likelihood for these three particular labels, not a cancellation probability for one customer. Multiplying by further row factors gives the 12-row likelihood. We optimize the same coefficients across all rows; we do not choose a separate slope for every booking.

This is a function of the unknown coefficients because \(p_i=\sigma(b_0+b_1x_i)\). Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) chooses coefficients that give the observed Training labels as high a joint probability as possible under the model. In real data, dependence among repeat bookings by a customer or correlated reservations must be handled when interpreting uncertainty and choosing validation splits. Our training bookings are constructed as independent examples for instructional clarity.

9. Why Negative Log-Likelihood becomes Log Loss

Products of many probabilities can become extremely small. Taking natural logarithms turns the product into a sum, which is easier to optimize. Maximizing Log-Likelihood is equivalent to minimizing its negative. Dividing by \(n\) produces the mean Binary Cross-Entropy, often called Log Loss:

\[J(b_0,b_1)=-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\Big].\]

Take a Training booking with \(x=2\) and \(y=1\). Our fitted probability is 0.508110. Its individual loss is \(-\ln(0.508110)\approx0.677057\). If another booking with the same \(x=2\) remains active (\(y=0\)), its loss is \(-\ln(1-0.508110)\approx0.709500\). Both outcomes can occur at the same Feature value, and the model cannot correctly assign probability 1 to both. Unlike Accuracy, Log Loss evaluates the entire probability, so two models predicting the same class can receive very different losses.

At the naive starting parameters \(b_0=b_1=0\), every booking has \(p=0.5\), so each loss is \(-\ln(0.5)=0.693147\). The final fitted Training mean loss is approximately 0.511249, a genuine reduction of the Training objective. But lower Training Loss alone does not establish generalization; later sections evaluate the fitted model on held-out bookings.

10. Derive the Logistic Regression Gradient from the Chain Rule

Now we connect the preceding sections to Gradient Descent. For observation \(i\), define its score \(z_i=b_0+b_1x_i\), probability \(p_i=\sigma(z_i)\), and loss \(\ell_i=-y_i\ln p_i-(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\). To learn the coefficients, we need the Partial Derivatives of mean loss with respect to \(b_0\) and \(b_1\).

Step 1 — Derivative of Sigmoid. The logistic function satisfies \(\frac{dp_i}{dz_i}=p_i(1-p_i)\). The reader can verify this by differentiating \((1+e^{-z_i})^{-1}\) with the Chain Rule.

Step 2 — Derivative of loss with respect to probability. Differentiating the two logarithms gives

\[\frac{d\ell_i}{dp_i}=-\frac{y_i}{p_i}+\frac{1-y_i}{1-p_i}.\]

Step 3 — Multiply through the Chain Rule. Multiply that expression by \(dp_i/dz_i=p_i(1-p_i)\). The terms simplify:

\[\frac{d\ell_i}{dz_i}=\left(-\frac{y_i}{p_i}+\frac{1-y_i}{1-p_i}\right)p_i(1-p_i)=p_i-y_i.\]

Step 4 — Continue to each coefficient. Since \(dz_i/db_0=1\) and \(dz_i/db_1=x_i\), averaging the per-row derivatives gives

\[\boxed{\frac{\partial J}{\partial b_0}=\frac1n\sum_i(p_i-y_i),\qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial b_1}=\frac1n\sum_i(p_i-y_i)x_i.}\]

The quantity \(p_i-y_i\) is a probability error, not the binary prediction error produced after applying a threshold. This is why Gradient Descent trains directly on probabilities without needing to make a hard 0/1 decision at each step.

Why is this particular optimization problem convex?

Unlike an arbitrary Neural Network objective, the unregularized Logistic Regression mean Log Loss is convex in its coefficient vector for a fixed Feature matrix. This follows from one more derivative—not from assuming that an optimizer must find the correct answer. Write the Feature row (including the Intercept) as \(v_i=(1,x_i)^\top\), with shape \(2\times1\), and coefficients \(b=(b_0,b_1)^\top\). The score is \(z_i=b^\top v_i\), and the gradient from above is \(\nabla J(b)=n^{-1}\sum_i (p_i-y_i)v_i\). Differentiating each term once more gives the Hessian, the \(2\times2\) matrix of second derivatives:

\[H(b)=\nabla^2J(b)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n p_i(1-p_i)\,v_iv_i^\top=\frac1nX^\top W X,\quad W=\operatorname{diag}\{p_i(1-p_i)\}.\]

Here \(X\) has \(n\) rows and 2 columns; \(W\) has shape \(n\times n\). Since \(0\lt p_i\lt1\), every weight \(p_i(1-p_i)\) is positive for finite coefficients. For any direction \(a=(a_0,a_1)^\top\),

\[a^\top H a=\frac1n\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(a_0+a_1x_i)^2\ge0.\]

Each term is a nonnegative weight times a square: the Hessian is positive semidefinite, establishing convexity. If the Feature columns are linearly independent, the Hessian is positive definite at finite coefficients. But convexity alone does not guarantee finite fitted coefficients: for perfectly separable binary data, the unpenalized likelihood may improve indefinitely as coefficient magnitudes grow. Our hotel Training data include opposite labels at the same lead time and have a finite optimum; the fitted near-zero gradient in Section 12 is meaningful in that setting. Regularization or separation-aware fitting is often used for unstable cases.

How do we check a gradient instead of simply trusting it?

Use a central finite difference. For any coefficient \(b_j\), perturb only that coefficient by a small value \(\varepsilon\) and compare \(\{J(b+\varepsilon e_j)-J(b-\varepsilon e_j)\}/(2\varepsilon)\) with the analytic gradient. For the four-booking teaching example after its first update, \(b=(0,0.2)\), \(\varepsilon=10^{-5}\), we obtain:

12. Fit the full Training model and verify the learned coefficients

Return to the 12 Training reservations from Section 2. A numerical optimizer repeatedly calculates predicted probabilities and the Gradient until the parameters approximately minimize mean Log Loss. Unlike simple Ordinary Least Squares, Logistic Regression generally has no finite closed-form coefficient formula like \((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\). We therefore use an iterative numerical algorithm. Show the first actual Training updates. At \(b_0=b_1=0\), all 12 probabilities equal 0.5. The dataset has \(\sum y_i=5\), \(\sum x_i=19\), and \(\sum x_i y_i=12\). 13. A Decision Boundary is created by the threshold, not by training

A probability does not by itself tell staff which reservations to mark. Let the Decision Threshold be \(t\), between 0 and 1. Our policy predicts cancellation when \(\hat p\ge t\), otherwise no cancellation. For \(t=0.5\), the Sigmoid is at least 0.5 precisely when the score is nonnegative, so the boundary in lead-time units is

\[\hat b_0+\hat b_1x=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=-\frac{\hat b_0}{\hat b_1}\approx\frac{2.222688}{1.127565}\approx1.9712.\]

Thus among the displayed categories, \(x\ge2\) is flagged at threshold 0.5. The model coefficients do not change when we change the threshold; only the decision policy changes. A threshold of 0.25 also flags \(x=1\), because that category's fitted probability is about 0.250655. In general, the score boundary for threshold \(t\) is \(\ln(t/(1-t))\), not always zero.

This distinction becomes even more important when a hotel can contact only a fixed number of guests. The probability model may rank bookings, while the contact policy must consider capacity, consent, possible harm and actual intervention effectiveness. A binary warning is not automatically a recommendation to overbook or penalize a guest.

15. Derive the Confusion Matrix and evaluation metrics

A True Positive (TP) is a cancelled booking predicted to cancel; a False Positive (FP) is a booking that stays but is flagged; a False Negative (FN) is a cancellation missed by the policy; and a True Negative (TN) is an unflagged booking that stays. All four counts must sum to eight on Validation.

For threshold \(t=0.50\): V4, V6 and V8 are TP (3); V5 and V7 are FP (2); V3 is FN (1); V1 and V2 are TN (2). Hence \((TP,FP,FN,TN)=(3,2,1,2)\). At threshold \(t=0.25\), V2 and V3 both become flagged, giving \((4,3,0,1)\). Derive metrics from counts rather than trusting a summary table:

\[\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad \mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad \mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{N}.\]
Validation metric Threshold 0.50 Threshold 0.25
TP, FP, FN, TN 3, 2, 1, 2 4, 3, 0, 1
Accuracy 5/8 = 62.50% 5/8 = 62.50%
Precision 3/5 = 60.00% 4/7 ≈ 57.14%
Recall 3/4 = 75.00% 4/4 = 100.00%
Specificity 2/4 = 50.00% 1/4 = 25.00%

Both thresholds have the same Accuracy, but their consequences differ. A lower threshold detects the one previously missed cancellation while adding an unnecessary alert. Precision depends in part on how often cancellation occurs in the evaluated population, and this synthetic Validation set is far too small to establish an operational rate.

16. Select a threshold based on a transparent decision criterion

Suppose the hotel assigns a hypothetical cost of $8 per unnecessary alert (FP) and $30 per missed cancellation (FN). These are invented penalties for comparing rules, not measured losses or evidence that an intervention works. Define the Validation cost as \(C=8FP+30FN\). For threshold 0.50, \(C=8(2)+30(1)=\$46\). For threshold 0.25, \(C=8(3)+30(0)=\$24\). A stricter threshold of 0.60 yields TP=2, FP=1, FN=2, TN=3 and cost \(8(1)+30(2)=\$68\).

It flags seven of eight Validation bookings: capacity and guest experience might make that unacceptable. Different costs, contact limits and prevalences can reverse the recommendation; changing them repeatedly after reading Test would contaminate evaluation.

Optional decision-theory derivation: what threshold do the assumed costs imply?

Distinguish a model-derived probability from a decision rule. Under an intentionally simplified cost table—\(\$8\) for a false alert, \(\$30\) for a missed cancellation, and zero cost for the two correct decisions—suppose a probability \(p\) is accurately calibrated for the decision population and the action does not itself change that event probability. The conditional expected cost of alerting is \(8(1-p)\); that of not alerting is \(30p\). Alert when

\[\begin{aligned}8(1-p)&\lt30p\\8-8p&\lt30p\\8&\lt38p\\p&\gt\frac8{8+30}=\frac8{38}\approx0.210526.\end{aligned}\]

The theoretical threshold is therefore approximately 0.211 under these assumptions. It is not the same procedure as selecting the cheapest of the predeclared Validation candidates \(\{0.25,0.50,0.60\}\), where 0.25 was selected. Nor do these hypothetical penalties prove that contacting guests saves money: reminders may change outcomes, probabilities may be miscalibrated, capacity may be limited, and costs may vary across reservations. An actual intervention decision requires measuring its effects, not only its classification errors.

After seeing E6, we may analyze limitations; we must not quietly change the threshold and still claim these same Test bookings are untouched.

18. Calibration, Log Loss and Brier Score evaluate probabilities—not just flags

Calibration asks whether, across many cases assigned a probability near \(p\), the observed event fraction is also near \(p\). Discrimination asks whether positives generally receive higher scores than negatives. Both matter, and neither is established by six Test bookings. With few observations, a reliability diagram has very uncertain bins; we should not treat a smooth-looking curve as proof of calibration.

The Brier Score for binary outcomes is the mean squared probability error:

\[\mathrm{Brier}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(\hat p_i-y_i)^2.\]

For Test case E6, \(\hat p=0.907843\) and \(y=0\), so its contribution is approximately \((0.907843-0)^2\approx0.82418\). Across all six Test cases the Brier Score is approximately 0.325390. Test Log Loss is approximately 0.921670. Unlike Precision and Recall, neither Brier Score nor Log Loss depends on our classification threshold; both use the original probabilities. Lower is better for both scores, but reporting either alone without a baseline, sample size and study design is incomplete.

A subtle point: a class label is a deterministic decision after thresholding; the underlying probability remains a model estimate with uncertainty. A model can correctly classify E6 at a sufficiently high threshold, but its assigned probability may still be overly confident. Such distinctions motivate calibration diagnostics and uncertainty assessment on much larger held-out populations.

19. Extend to two Features and understand the new boundary

A real hotel usually has more information at booking time than lead time. Suppose we add \(r\), an indicator equal to 1 for a refundable booking and 0 otherwise, known at confirmation. A two-Feature Logistic Regression would use

\[z=b_0+b_1x+b_2r,\qquad p=\sigma(z).\]

To illustrate interpretation—not to claim another fitted model—take hypothetical coefficients \(b_0=-2.2\), \(b_1=1.1\), \(b_2=0.6\). For a non-refundable booking with \(x=2,r=0\), \(z=-2.2+1.1(2)=0\) and \(p=0.5\). For a refundable booking with identical lead time \(x=2,r=1\), \(z=0.6\) and \(p=1/(1+e^{-0.6})\approx0.645656\). The refundable indicator multiplies the odds by \(e^{0.6}\approx1.8221\), holding the other Feature fixed.

At threshold 0.5, the boundary is \(-2.2+1.1x+0.6r=0\), or \(x=(2.2-0.6r)/1.1\). When \(r=0\), it is \(x=2\); when \(r=1\), it is approximately \(x=1.4545\). That straight line in Feature space separates predicted classes, even though the probabilities vary smoothly on either side. This is a property of a linear-score Logistic Regression, not a demonstration that the true relationship is necessarily linear in log-odds.

20. Assumptions, edge cases and failure modes

Logistic Regression does not require predictors to be normally distributed or each class to have equal variance. Its modeling assumptions concern, among other issues, a suitable form for the conditional log-odds, adequately measured Features, appropriate sampling and dependencies, and whether the likelihood is well behaved. A linear log-odds relationship can be checked with transformations or nonlinear terms when supported by evidence; blindly assuming one linear slope may misfit threshold effects.

Complete separation: if a Feature perfectly distinguishes 0s from 1s, unregularized Maximum Likelihood coefficients may diverge toward infinite magnitude; a finite optimum need not exist. Multicollinearity: highly related Features can make individual coefficient estimates unstable even when predictions remain reasonable. Class Imbalance: if cancellations are rare, a model predicting “not cancelled” for everybody can attain high Accuracy while failing at detection; evaluate class prevalence, appropriate metrics and calibration. Regularization: practical implementations often apply a penalty to control coefficient magnitude. Penalized and unpenalized coefficients need not match, so specify the software settings when reproducing numbers.

Also distinguish association and causation: a refundable booking may have different cancellation behavior, but this model cannot prove that changing the policy for a guest would change the guest's cancellation outcome. Finally, privacy, fairness and consent are not optional considerations; a useful score does not justify punitive or misleading decisions about customers.

21. Data Leakage, reproducibility and a complete Python example

For this case, a leakage-safe Workflow requires an explicit prediction timestamp: booking confirmation. Accept only Features known then, keep Training/Validation/Test partitions conceptually separate, and preserve fitted preprocessing parameters. If we later use One-Hot Encoding for room types, an Imputer for missing lead times, or StandardScaler for numerical Features, fit those data-dependent transformations on Training only; transform Validation and Test with the frozen fitted settings. Recomputing transformations using Test—even without labels—can contaminate a held-out evaluation.

The following code recreates all three partitions and fits the unpenalized model used for our hand calculations. It then selects the threshold from Validation using the declared $8/$30 cost and reports one Test evaluation. scikit-learn numerical optimization may differ in the final displayed decimals depending on tolerance and version; the meaningful checks are the coefficients, probabilities and metrics to stated precision.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expit
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

x_train = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4]).reshape(-1, 1)
y_train = np.array([0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1])
x_val = np.array([0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4]).reshape(-1, 1)
y_val = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1])
x_test = np.array([0, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4]).reshape(-1, 1)
y_test = np.array([0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0])

model = LogisticRegression(penalty=None, solver="lbfgs", tol=1e-10, max_iter=5000)
model.fit(x_train, y_train)
print("Intercept / slope:", model.intercept_[0], model.coef_[0, 0])

p_val = model.predict_proba(x_val)[:, 1]
p_test = model.predict_proba(x_test)[:, 1]

def counts(y, probabilities, threshold):
    prediction = probabilities >= threshold
    tp = int(((prediction == 1) & (y == 1)).sum())
    fp = int(((prediction == 1) & (y == 0)).sum())
    fn = int(((prediction == 0) & (y == 1)).sum())
    tn = int(((prediction == 0) & (y == 0)).sum())
    return tp, fp, fn, tn

for t in (0.25, 0.50, 0.60):
    tp, fp, fn, tn = counts(y_val, p_val, t)
    print("Validation", t, (tp, fp, fn, tn), "assumed cost", 8 * fp + 30 * fn)

selected_threshold = min(
    (0.25, 0.50, 0.60),
    key=lambda t: 8 * counts(y_val, p_val, t)[1] + 30 * counts(y_val, p_val, t)[2],
)
print("Selected threshold:", selected_threshold)
print("Test counts:", counts(y_test, p_test, selected_threshold))
print("Test Brier:", np.mean((p_test - y_test) ** 2))

The code should be read as a reproducible verification of the theory, not as a replacement for the earlier derivations. In an actual project, time-aware or group-aware data splitting, nested model selection when necessary, outcome maturity and a more representative evaluation would be essential.

Reproduce both coefficient updates and an independent gradient check

Run the following NumPy/SciPy code independently of the hotel example. It recomputes the three losses, validates both gradient components by finite differences, and evaluates only the two illustrative Validation accounts. The code deliberately does not report a fully fitted subscriber model, because only two optimization steps are performed.

# Fully reproducible synthetic subscription-churn example.
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expit

x = np.array([0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5], dtype=float)
y = np.array([0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1], dtype=float)
learning_rate = 0.2
b = np.array([0.0, 0.0])  # intercept, slope

def stable_loss(beta, x_values, targets):
    score = beta[0] + beta[1] * x_values
    # log(1+exp(score)) - target*score is stable BCE.
    return float(np.mean(np.logaddexp(0.0, score) - targets * score))

def gradient(beta):
    p = expit(beta[0] + beta[1] * x)
    return np.array([np.mean(p - y), np.mean((p - y) * x)])

history = []
for stage in range(3):
    p = expit(b[0] + b[1] * x)
    g = gradient(b)
    loss = stable_loss(b, x, y)
    history.append((b.copy(), p.copy(), g.copy(), loss))
    print(f"Step {stage}: intercept={b[0]:.8f}; slope={b[1]:.8f}; "
          f"loss={loss:.8f}; gradient={g.round(8)}")
    if stage < 2:
        b -= learning_rate * g

assert np.allclose(history[0][2], [0, -7/12], atol=1e-12)
assert np.allclose(history[1][2], [0.0717083095, -0.3212208673], atol=1e-9)
assert np.allclose(b, [-0.0143416619, 0.1809108401], atol=1e-9)
assert np.allclose([h[3] for h in history],
                   [0.69314718056, 0.64053317466, 0.62271137889], atol=1e-9)

# Independent central finite-difference check for both derivatives.
epsilon = 1e-5
analytic = gradient(b)
for j in (0, 1):
    direction = np.eye(2)[j]
    numerical = (stable_loss(b + epsilon*direction, x, y) -
                 stable_loss(b - epsilon*direction, x, y))/(2*epsilon)
    assert abs(numerical - analytic[j]) < 1e-8

x_validation = np.array([1.0, 4.0])  # illustrative later cohort
validation_label = np.array([0, 1])
validation_p = expit(b[0] + b[1] * x_validation)
validation_pred = (validation_p >= 0.5).astype(int)
assert np.allclose(validation_p, [0.541546, 0.670247], atol=1e-6)
assert validation_pred.tolist() == [1, 1]
print('Illustrative Validation probabilities:', np.round(validation_p, 6))
print('Illustrative Validation TP, FP:',
      int(np.sum((validation_pred == 1) & (validation_label == 1))),
      int(np.sum((validation_pred == 1) & (validation_label == 0))))
print('Odds multiplier per one additional interruption:', round(float(np.exp(b[1])), 6))

23. Common mistakes: identify the reasoning error and correct it

1. “Logistic Regression predicts a continuous Target because its name says Regression.” It models a probability for a categorical event through a linear log-odds relationship; the standard binary application uses 0/1 labels.

2. “The Linear Score is a probability.” A score can be negative or greater than 1. Apply Sigmoid to obtain an estimate in (0,1).

3. “A coefficient of 1.13 increases probability by 113 percentage points.” It adds 1.13 to log-odds per unit of the Feature. Its exponential is an Odds Ratio; changes in probability depend on starting probability.

4. “Odds and probability are interchangeable.” Probability 0.75 implies odds 3, not probability 3. Explain the denominator of each quantity.

5. “The default threshold 0.50 is mathematically optimal.” It is a conventional starting point, not necessarily the best policy under capacity and error costs. Choose thresholds using development data and an explicit decision objective.

6. “Changing threshold retrains the model.” The probabilities remain fixed. Only the binary classification policy changes.

7. “High Accuracy means good detection.” With rare cancellations, always predicting no cancellation may be highly accurate and still yield Recall 0. Compare with a meaningful Baseline and examine class prevalence.

8. “Low Training Log Loss proves generalization.” Training was used to estimate coefficients. Validation/Test offer independent checks only when protected from repeated tuning.

9. “One large probability error is equivalent to one small error.” Log Loss penalizes confident mistakes strongly; Brier Score penalizes squared probability differences. Specify what the objective measures.

10. “A negative gradient tells us to decrease that coefficient.” Gradient Descent subtracts the gradient: subtracting a negative derivative increases the coefficient. Check the sign with a numeric step.

11. “A single 100% Recall on three positive Test cases proves reliability.” With three positives, each missed event changes Recall by 33.33 percentage points. Report uncertainty and sample size.

12. “A high risk estimate guarantees an event.” A 0.91 predicted probability still allows a non-cancellation. Calibration is assessed across many predictions, not by demanding certainty on each booking.

13. “All regressors must be numeric and standardized before Logistic Regression.” Categorical Features need valid Encoding, scaling may help optimization, but preprocessing choices are not universal requirements; fit learned transforms only on Training.

14. “An Odds Ratio above 1 proves a causal effect.” Observational association does not establish intervention effects. Confounders and timing matter.

15. “The final model is automatically ready to deploy.” It still needs data contracts, monitoring, consent-aware interventions, capacity planning, versioning and reliable independent evaluation.

24. Check your understanding: solve, then inspect the reasoning

1. For a booking with lead time \(x=2\), reproduce the linear score and probability.

Insert the fitted coefficients: \(z=-2.222688+1.127565(2)=0.032442\). Then \(p=1/(1+e^{-0.032442})\approx0.508110\). The score is not 3.24%; the output is approximately 50.81% after Sigmoid.

2. Why can a Linear Score be negative while a probability cannot?

The linear score lies on the real line, so a negative Intercept or a combination of Features can give any real value. Sigmoid maps this score into (0,1), where a probability is meaningful. Conflating the two would assign impossible negative probabilities.

3. A model predicts \(p=0.75\). Find the complementary probability, odds and log-odds.

The complement is \(1-0.75=0.25\); odds = \(0.75/0.25=3\); log-odds = \(\ln3\approx1.0986\). Odds of 3 are not a 300% event probability.

4. Interpret the fitted slope \(b_1=1.127565\) in the context of this dataset.

One more lead-time unit—30 additional days—raises estimated log-odds by 1.127565 and multiplies odds by \(e^{1.127565}\approx3.088\), within this fitted model. It does not multiply probability by 3.088 or demonstrate causation.

5. Compute the individual Log Loss for \(y=1\), \(p=0.508110\).

For a positive label the loss is \(-\ln p\). Substituting gives \(-\ln(0.508110)\approx0.677057\). The loss is positive because the model did not assign certainty to the observed cancellation.

6. Compute the loss for \(y=0\) at the same probability and explain the change.

For a negative label the loss is \(-\ln(1-p)=-\ln(0.491890)\approx0.709500\). A model gives the same probability for both identical \(x\) values; their different outcomes produce different losses.

7. Starting from all \(p=0.5\) for labels \((0,0,1,1)\), show the Intercept gradient.

Use \(\frac14\sum_i(p_i-y_i)=[0.5+0.5-0.5-0.5]/4=0\). Therefore the Intercept does not change in the first update, even though the slope does.

8. For \(x=(0,1,2,3)\), find the slope gradient and first update with \(\alpha=0.4\).

The weighted errors are \(0,0.5,-1,-1.5\), summing to −2. Divide by four to obtain −0.5. Subtracting \(0.4(-0.5)\) updates the slope from 0 to 0.2. The negative gradient causes an increase, not a decrease.

9. Why do the four-row coefficients differ from the 12-row fitted coefficients?

They use different datasets and different optimization stages. The four rows demonstrate individual iterations; the 12-row full Training set is optimized numerically to a near-zero gradient. Never present intermediate teaching values as the final MLE fit for another dataset.

10. At threshold 0.50, construct Validation TP, FP, FN, TN from the eight rows.

V4,V6,V8 are TP=3; V5,V7 are FP=2; V3 is FN=1; V1,V2 are TN=2. Sum 3+2+1+2=8. Precision = 3/5, Recall = 3/4 and Accuracy = 5/8.

11. Repeat the Validation confusion counts at threshold 0.25.

V2 and V3 both switch from negative to positive because their fitted probability is 0.250655. V3 becomes an additional TP and V2 an additional FP, so TP=4, FP=3, FN=0, TN=1. Accuracy stays at 5/8, but Recall becomes 100%.

12. Compute and compare hypothetical Validation costs at thresholds 0.25 and 0.50.

With cost \(8FP+30FN\), threshold 0.25 costs \(8(3)+30(0)=24\) dollars; threshold 0.50 costs \(8(2)+30(1)=46\). On these invented costs and declared candidates, the first threshold is preferred, subject to contact capacity and ethical constraints.

13. What is the Test Precision at the frozen 0.25 threshold?

The Test counts are TP=3, FP=2, FN=0, TN=1. Precision = \(TP/(TP+FP)=3/(3+2)=0.60\). The number is highly uncertain because it is based on just five positive flags in a six-row synthetic cohort.

14. What is the Brier contribution of Test booking E6?

E6 has \(y=0\) and fitted probability approximately 0.907843. Its squared probability error is \((0.907843-0)^2\approx0.82418\). A highly confident wrong probability is strongly penalized regardless of whether a chosen threshold generates a particular class.

15. With hypothetical two-feature coefficients \((-2.2,1.1,0.6)\), compute \(p\) for \(x=2,r=1\).

The score is \(-2.2+1.1(2)+0.6(1)=0.6\). Sigmoid gives \(p=1/(1+e^{-0.6})\approx0.645656\). The second Feature increases log-odds by 0.6, not probability by 60 percentage points.

16. Identify leakage in this proposed hotel Feature: “guest requested cancellation seven days after booking”.

At booking confirmation, this future request does not yet exist. Including it in the day-zero predictor table lets the model see part of the future Target. Rebuild Features as of booking confirmation and exclude post-confirmation events; separately verify outcome maturity.

16. Using the six synthetic subscription accounts, recompute the very first slope gradient. Why is it negative?

At zero coefficients all probabilities equal 0.5. The Feature sum is 15; positive-label Feature sum is 11. Therefore \(g_1=[0.5(15)-11]/6=-3.5/6=-0.5833333\). Gradient Descent subtracts the negative value and increases the slope by \(0.2(0.5833333)=0.1166667\). The sign means the initial prediction errors are correlated with the higher observed interruption counts, not that outages are a proven cause.

17. With interim coefficients from update 2, compute the churn probability for three interruptions and distinguish odds from probability.

\(z=-0.0143417+0.1809108(3)=0.5283907\); hence \(p=1/(1+e^{-0.5283907})\approx0.629108\) (62.91%). Its odds are \(p/(1-p)\approx1.696\) to one. The slope is a constant change in log-odds, not a constant change of 18.09 percentage points in probability. These are interim Training estimates, not validated real-world rates.

18. Derive the theoretical cost threshold when false alerts cost $8 and missed events cost $30. Why does this not contradict selecting 0.25 on Validation?

Given calibrated probabilities and zero costs for correct decisions, the expected cost of alerting is \(8(1-p)\); the expected cost of not alerting is \(30p\). Solve \(8-8p<30p\) to obtain \(p>8/38\approx0.210526\). This theoretical rule assumes those cost values and calibrated probabilities. Validation compared only three prespecified candidates 0.25, 0.50 and 0.60 and chose the cheapest observed one; these are different procedures.

19. Explain why a near-zero gradient for unpenalized Logistic Regression does not justify ignoring perfect separation or held-out evaluation.

Convexity follows because \(a^\top Ha=n^{-1}\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(a^\top v_i)^2\ge0\). With linearly independent Features and finite coefficients, curvature is strictly positive. But perfectly separated data may have no finite optimum, despite a convex loss. Moreover, finding a low Training loss or small gradient is about optimization on Training; only untouched Validation/Test can support claims about generalization, and calibration needs adequate data.

25. Glossary, connections and next steps

Binary Classification: a supervised problem with two possible event labels. Target: the event to be predicted, here cancellation before check-in. Feature: information available at the stated prediction time. Linear Score: \(z=b_0+b_1x\), an unrestricted number. Sigmoid: transformation \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) into (0,1). Odds: \(p/(1-p)\). Log-Odds / Logit: \(\ln(p/(1-p))\). Odds Ratio: factor \(e^{b_j}\) multiplying odds for a one-unit Feature increase. Bernoulli Likelihood: product of probabilities assigned to observed binary labels, under the model. Maximum Likelihood Estimation: selecting coefficients maximizing that likelihood. Log Loss / Binary Cross-Entropy: negative average Bernoulli Log-Likelihood. Gradient: vector of Partial Derivatives of the objective. Decision Threshold: policy value mapping a probability into a predicted class. Calibration: agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event frequencies across many cases. Data Leakage: inappropriate use of unavailable future information or evaluation data in development.

The conceptual chain is now complete: Features → Linear Score → Sigmoid Probability → Likelihood → Log Loss → Gradient → Fitted Coefficients → Threshold → Evaluation → Decision. Each arrow is a distinct learning step. A student should be able to reproduce a probability, justify the gradient update, interpret an Odds Ratio, reconstruct a Confusion Matrix, and explain why the final Test evaluation must remain independent. The next guides will revisit these foundations when studying Generalized Linear Models, probabilistic learning, regularization and evaluation—but this chapter provides an independent starting point rather than asking readers to memorize formulas without a reason.
