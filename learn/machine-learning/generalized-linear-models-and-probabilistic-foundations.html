1. A prediction can be a count, a probability, or a continuous quantity

Imagine a city's public-transit operations team monitoring minor equipment alerts generated during station inspections. On some shifts the team sees only one alert; on others it sees several. The supervisor asks: “Given how long we observe the station and whether the window is peak activity, how many alerts should we expect?” An alert is a recorded event, not necessarily a safety incident or a mechanical failure. The goal is to forecast inspection workload, not to determine that busy periods cause equipment problems.

This question is different from predicting continuous energy consumption (Guide 7) or whether a hotel booking will be cancelled (Guide 10). Here the Target is a nonnegative integer count: 0, 1, 2, … . A normal Linear Regression can predict a negative count and assumes a constant conditional variance that may not describe event data. Logistic Regression outputs a probability for a binary outcome, but does not directly model the number of events. We need a broader framework: Generalized Linear Models (GLMs).

Our main example is a Poisson GLM with a log link and an exposure offset. We will also derive how ordinary Gaussian Linear Regression and Bernoulli Logistic Regression belong to the same family. Everything is built from a small, fully disclosed synthetic dataset. By the end, you should be able to identify a response distribution, construct and interpret the link, calculate a likelihood, estimate coefficients, verify fitted predictions, evaluate held-out counts, and recognize violations of model assumptions.

The 16 windows below are invented for teaching: eight Training, four Validation, and four Test. The tiny sample is deliberately unsuitable for a real transit safety or staffing decision. In particular, its unusually regular variation cannot establish that Poisson variance assumptions hold outside this exercise.

2. Read the complete dataset before writing equations

A window is one period during which the station is monitored. The Feature Peak is 1 for a high-activity period and 0 otherwise; it is a category established before monitoring begins. Exposure is the number of monitored hours, not a count of events. The observed Target \(y\) is the number of alerts during those hours. Thus a two-hour window is not directly comparable to a one-hour window without considering duration.

3. Why extending Linear Regression requires more than a new formula

Simple Linear Regression writes \(E[Y\mid x]=\beta_0+\beta_1x\) and, in its classical Gaussian formulation, assumes a normally distributed outcome around that mean. The straight line can produce any real value. That can be useful for temperature or energy consumption, but predicting \(-1.5\) alerts is nonsensical if we intend the output to be an expected count. Also, the Poisson model describes a distribution whose variance increases with its mean; ordinary constant-variance Gaussian errors do not express that structure automatically.

Logistic Regression solves a different issue: for a Bernoulli Target, it transforms the linear predictor into a number between 0 and 1 with the Sigmoid. Notice the common pattern. We start with a linear score \(\eta=\beta_0+\beta_1x\), then use a mathematical transformation appropriate for the type of outcome. A GLM makes that pattern explicit and couples it with an appropriate conditional probability distribution.

Three separate questions must be answered. What values can the outcome take? Counts need nonnegative integer support; binary targets need 0 or 1; continuous measurements may be real-valued. How is the conditional mean connected to Features? That is the role of the link function. How will parameters be estimated and uncertainty quantified? That requires the likelihood and assumptions of the chosen distribution.

Do not confuse a GLM with arbitrary “nonlinear regression.” In a GLM, the predictor is linear in its coefficients on the link scale, but the predicted mean may be a nonlinear function of Features. And not every predictive model is a GLM: Random Forest and neural networks do not become GLMs simply because they predict probabilities or counts.

4. The three ingredients of a Generalized Linear Model

A standard GLM combines three ingredients:

  1. Random component: the conditional distribution of \(Y_i\) belongs to an appropriate exponential-family model, such as Normal, Bernoulli or Poisson.
  2. Systematic component: Features form a linear predictor \(\eta_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{i1}+\cdots+\beta_px_{ip}\). The coefficients are learned from Training.
  3. Link function: a function \(g\) connects the conditional mean \(\mu_i=E[Y_i\mid x_i]\) to that predictor: \(g(\mu_i)=\eta_i\). Its inverse \(g^{-1}\) converts a score back into a mean.
\[\boxed{\quad \eta_i=\mathbf x_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta,\qquad \mu_i=g^{-1}(\eta_i)\quad}\]

Here \(\mathbf x_i\) contains an initial 1 for the intercept plus the observation's Features; \(\boldsymbol\beta\) contains the coefficients. A mean is not necessarily an individual observation. For example, \(\mu=3.67\) alerts is a perfectly valid expected count even though no individual window can contain 3.67 events.

A link is not another probability distribution. The Poisson distribution describes uncertainty in the count; the log link converts its positive mean into an unconstrained linear score. The same link need not be used for every distribution, and the choice affects interpretation and the numerical fitting procedure.

5. Meet the three foundational outcome distributions

Gaussian / Normal. A continuous \(Y\) can be modeled as \(Y_i\mid x_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma^2)\). Its density is determined by the mean and variance. With the identity link, \(\mu_i=\eta_i\). For known equal variances, maximizing the Gaussian likelihood gives the familiar Ordinary Least Squares solution. Example: predicted hourly energy use of 14.2 kWh can meaningfully be non-integer.

Bernoulli. A binary \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) has \(P(Y_i=1\mid x_i)=p_i\), mean \(\mu_i=p_i\), and variance \(p_i(1-p_i)\). With the logit link, \(\log[p_i/(1-p_i)]=\eta_i\), so \(p_i=1/(1+e^{-\eta_i})\). Example: the probability that a booking will cancel. The estimated probability differs from a final 0/1 decision.

Poisson. A count \(Y\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\) follows \(P(Y_i=y)=e^{-\mu_i}\mu_i^y/y!\) for \(\mu_i>0\). Its mean and variance are both \(\mu_i\). With the log link, \(\log\mu_i=\eta_i\), or \(\mu_i=e^{\eta_i}>0\). Example: the expected number of alerts in one monitored window. A Poisson process interpretation typically also needs a suitably stable event intensity and conditional independence over disjoint intervals.

Family / Target Conditional mean Conditional variance Canonical link Inverse link
Normal / continuous \(\mu\) \(\sigma^2\) Identity: \(\eta=\mu\) \(\mu=\eta\)
Bernoulli / 0 or 1 \(p\) \(p(1-p)\) Logit: \(\eta=\log[p/(1-p)]\) \(p=1/(1+e^{-\eta})\)
Poisson / count \(\mu\) \(\mu\) Log: \(\eta=\log\mu\) \(\mu=e^\eta\)

The expressions in the variance column are model assumptions, not automatically true facts about any dataset. Later we will see why event clustering can violate Poisson's equality of mean and variance.

6. Counts and rates are different: why exposure is essential

Suppose an off-peak window produces two alerts during one hour, while another produces four during two hours. The raw counts differ, but both represent two alerts per hour. If the model ignores exposure, it might attribute the higher count to the peak indicator instead of observing for longer. We therefore distinguish expected count \(\mu_i\) from expected rate \(\lambda_i\):

\[\boxed{\mu_i=e_i\lambda_i}\]

Here \(e_i>0\) is exposure in hours, \(\lambda_i>0\) is expected alerts per hour, and \(\mu_i\) is the expected number of alerts during the whole window. For example, at a rate of 2.5 alerts/hour, one hour gives \(\mu=1(2.5)=2.5\) alerts while two hours give \(\mu=2(2.5)=5\). Neither expectation must be an integer.

The Offset is a known quantity inserted in the linear predictor with a coefficient fixed to 1, not estimated from data:

\[\log\mu_i=\underbrace{\log e_i}_{\text{Offset}}+\beta_0+\beta_1x_i.\]

Exponentiating both sides gives \(\mu_i=e_i\exp(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)\). If exposure doubles while all other Features remain unchanged, the expected count doubles, while the predicted rate stays constant. This is a modeling assumption: when event intensity changes sharply within windows, one should use finer windows, time-varying covariates, or a different model. Exposure must be positive because \(\log 0\) is undefined; a zero-hour record cannot be treated as an ordinary monitored window.

7. Build our Poisson Regression equation and interpret its coefficients

For the station, \(x=0\) means normal activity and \(x=1\) means peak activity. Our model describes the expected rate, not just the count:

\[\log\lambda(x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x,\qquad \lambda(x)=e^{\beta_0+\beta_1x}.\]

Substituting this into the exposure equation yields the full count model:

\[Y_i\mid x_i,e_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\mu_i),\qquad\mu_i=e_i\exp(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i).\]

The Intercept \(\beta_0\) is the log rate for a normal-activity window (\(x=0\)); its exponent \(e^{\beta_0}\) is the corresponding positive rate. The peak coefficient \(\beta_1\) is a difference of log rates. Exponentiating it gives a Rate Ratio:

\[\frac{\lambda(1)}{\lambda(0)}=\frac{e^{\beta_0+\beta_1}}{e^{\beta_0}}=e^{\beta_1}.\]

If \(e^{\beta_1}=2.75\), the model's peak rate is 2.75 times its normal rate—175% higher, not 2.75 percentage points higher. For a continuous Feature, increasing that Feature by one unit multiplies the modeled rate by \(e^{\beta_j}\), holding the other Features and exposure fixed. Such an association is not, by itself, a causal effect of moving an operation to peak hours.

8. Establish an honest baseline before fitting a peak effect

The simplest valid rate model predicts one common alerts-per-hour rate, regardless of peak status. We estimate that rate using Training totals: 30 alerts occurred during 12 hours, so \(\hat\lambda_0=30/12=2.5\) alerts/hour. For a one-hour window, the baseline expects 2.5 alerts; for a two-hour window it expects 5. This is different from predicting 2.5 alerts for every window, which would incorrectly ignore exposure.

\[\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{baseline}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathrm{Training}}y_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathrm{Training}}e_i}=\frac{30}{12}=2.5.\]

The baseline's intercept on the log scale is \(\hat\beta_0^{(0)}=\log(2.5)\approx0.916291\). The superscript \((0)\) indicates a model without a peak coefficient, not an iteration of an optimizer. To predict a two-hour peak window with this baseline, calculate \(\mu=2\exp(0.916291)\approx2(2.5)=5\).

This baseline gives us a fair reference later. The richer model should earn its complexity through better Validation performance, not simply by fitting Training more closely. Evaluations must use the same held-out windows, definition of exposure, and metric. For predicting counts, a baseline with exposure is often more meaningful than the naive constant average count per row.

9. Estimate the two group rates directly from Training

Our single binary Feature makes the Training likelihood easy to solve analytically. Normal windows have eight alerts in six hours; peak windows have 22 in six hours. Their estimated conditional rates are:

\[\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{normal}}=\frac{1+3+2+2}{1+2+1+2}=\frac86=\frac43\approx1.333333\ \text{alerts/hour},\]
\[\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{peak}}=\frac{4+7+3+8}{1+2+1+2}=\frac{22}6=\frac{11}3\approx3.666667\ \text{alerts/hour}.\]

Because \(x=0\) has \(\lambda(0)=e^{\beta_0}\), we obtain \(\hat\beta_0=\log(4/3)\approx0.287682\). For \(x=1\), \(\lambda(1)=e^{\beta_0+\beta_1}=11/3\), so

\[\hat\beta_1=\log(11/3)-\log(4/3)=\log(11/4)=\log(2.75)\approx1.011601.\]

The fitted rate ratio is \(e^{1.011601}\approx2.75\). These are fitted coefficients, calculated from Training; we did not invent them. In Sections 11–14, we will derive why the Poisson likelihood produces precisely these group-rate estimates and how an optimization algorithm can reach them when there are more Features.

To see why we use total events divided by total hours, compare the average of the normal windows’ individual rates: \(1/1,3/2,2/1,2/2\) give \(1,1.5,2,1\), whose unweighted mean is \((1+1.5+2+1)/4=1.375\) alerts/hour. That is not the grouped estimate of \(8/6=1.3333\), because a two-hour window supplies twice the exposure of a one-hour window. The pooled rate weights windows in proportion to their hours and is the Poisson maximum-likelihood estimate under a common hourly intensity. In a more elaborate setting, unequal exposure can be essential even when all windows share the same Features.

Finally, test both fitted equations in a hypothetical two-hour window. Normal predicts \(2(4/3)=8/3\) alerts; Peak predicts \(2(11/3)=22/3\). The quotient \( (22/3)/(8/3)=22/8=2.75\) confirms the Rate Ratio also on the count scale when exposure is equal. It would be incorrect to subtract these counts and call the difference the coefficient \(\beta_1\): that coefficient lives on the log-rate scale.

First, return from the log scale to the rate scale: \(e^{0.287682}\approx1.333333\) for normal activity, and \(e^{0.287682+1.011601}\approx3.666667\) for peak activity. Next, multiply each rate by the actual exposure hours. This completes the path Features → linear score → inverse link → rate → expected count.

11. From a probability of a count to a Likelihood

The Poisson Probability Mass Function (PMF) assigns a probability to each possible nonnegative integer count:

\[P(Y=y\mid\mu)=\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^y}{y!},\quad y=0,1,2,\ldots,\quad\mu>0.\]

Here \(y!\) is the factorial, with \(0!=1\). Suppose a one-hour normal window has expected count \(\mu=4/3\). The probability of zero alerts is \(P(Y=0)=e^{-4/3}\approx0.263597\); therefore the probability of at least one is \(1-e^{-4/3}\approx0.736403\). The probability of exactly three alerts is \(e^{-4/3}(4/3)^3/3!\approx0.104137\). These are count probabilities, not the probability of cancellation from Logistic Regression.

When the outcome \(y\) has already been observed, we regard the PMF as a function of the unknown parameters, called the Likelihood. For Training observations assumed conditionally independent, multiply their individual probabilities. Because products of many small numbers are inconvenient, take logs:

\[\ell(\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum_i\left[y_i\log\mu_i-\mu_i-\log(y_i!)\right].\]

The factorial terms depend on observed counts, not on \(\boldsymbol\beta\), so they can be ignored when locating the maximizing coefficients, but must be retained for a correctly reported full Log-Likelihood. The negative Log-Likelihood is a natural training Loss; reducing it means assigning more probability, in aggregate, to the observed counts.

12. Derive the Maximum Likelihood Estimate rather than memorizing it

Consider one group with a common hourly rate \(\lambda\), observed counts \(y_1,\ldots,y_m\), and exposure hours \(e_1,\ldots,e_m\). Since \(\mu_i=e_i\lambda\), the group's Log-Likelihood is:

\[\ell(\lambda)=\sum_i\{y_i\log(e_i\lambda)-e_i\lambda-\log(y_i!)\}.\]

Separate the terms that contain \(\lambda\). Because \(\log(e_i\lambda)=\log e_i+\log\lambda\), we obtain \(\ell(\lambda)=\big(\sum_i y_i\big)\log\lambda-\lambda\sum_i e_i+\text{constant}\). Differentiate with respect to \(\lambda\):

\[\frac{d\ell}{d\lambda}=\frac{\sum_i y_i}{\lambda}-\sum_i e_i.\]

Set the derivative to zero and solve all the way through:

\[0=\frac{\sum_i y_i}{\hat\lambda}-\sum_i e_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\hat\lambda=\frac{\sum_i y_i}{\sum_i e_i}}.\]

The second derivative is \(d^2\ell/d\lambda^2=-\sum_i y_i/\lambda^2<0\) when the group has a positive total count, confirming an interior maximum. For the normal group this yields \(8/6\); for peak, \(22/6\), exactly as calculated in Section 9. Boundary case: if a group has zero observed events, the likelihood is maximized at \(\lambda=0\), corresponding to log rate approaching \(-\infty\); a finite unregularized log-link coefficient may not exist. More complex designs may also have identification or separation problems. The formula is not a universal magic shortcut for arbitrary multivariable Poisson Regression.

13. See the Likelihood maximum instead of trusting a black box

A graph helps connect the derivative to the optimization objective. For the normal group, \(\sum y_i=8\) and \(\sum e_i=6\). After discarding terms independent of \(\lambda\), its Log-Likelihood is \(\tilde\ell(\lambda)=8\log\lambda-6\lambda\). At \(\lambda=4/3\) it reaches its maximum, because \(8/\lambda-6=0\).

Compare candidate rates by subtracting the log-likelihood at the maximum:

\[\tilde\ell(\lambda)-\tilde\ell(4/3)=8\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{4/3}\right)-6\left(\lambda-\frac43\right).\]

At \(\lambda=1.0\), this difference is approximately −0.3015; at \(\lambda=1.6\), approximately −0.1414. Both produce a lower log-likelihood than \(4/3\). This comparison is on the log scale; a difference of −0.3 is not “30% less accurate.” Exponentiating differences gives relative likelihood values when such comparisons are appropriate, but these are not probabilities that a candidate parameter is true.

This is the same principle encountered in Linear Regression and Logistic Regression: establish an objective, find the coefficients that optimize it, and evaluate generalization separately. Maximizing Training Likelihood does not guarantee the model will predict future data well.

14. General Gradient, Hessian and the path to iterative fitting

For many Features, direct group totals no longer solve every coefficient independently. The log-link Poisson model is \(\mu_i=e_i\exp(\mathbf x_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta)\). Substitute it into the Log-Likelihood and differentiate with respect to a coefficient \(\beta_j\). Since \(\partial\mu_i/\partial\beta_j=\mu_i x_{ij}\), the Score (Log-Likelihood gradient) is:

\[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_j}=\sum_i x_{ij}(y_i-\mu_i),\qquad \nabla\ell= X^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu).\]

The Hessian (matrix of second derivatives) is \(H=-X^{\mathsf T}WX\), where \(W=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)\). The negative sign indicates the Log-Likelihood is concave in \(\boldsymbol\beta\) for the usual Poisson log-link model. This facilitates stable optimization if the design is identifiable. The matrix formula is a compact way to express the same residual-based adjustment as the scalar derivative.

For an approachable numerical example, start with the intercept-only model at rate 2 alerts/hour: \(\beta^{(0)}=\log2\), total exposure 12 hours, total count 30. The fitted total count is \(12(2)=24\), so the gradient is \(30-24=6\) and the Hessian is \(-24\). Newton–Raphson adds \(-H^{-1}\times\text{gradient}=-(1/-24)(6)=0.25\) to the log rate. Thus the first new estimate is \(\beta^{(1)}=0.693147+0.25=0.943147\).

The example here demonstrates an optimizer completely, without pretending the intercept-only updates fit the peak coefficient.

15. The exponential-family connection: the mathematics behind GLMs

The family name Generalized Linear Model is not arbitrary. A broad class of distributions can be written in exponential-family form:

\[f(y\mid\theta,\phi)=\exp\left(\frac{y\theta-b(\theta)}{a(\phi)}+c(y,\phi)\right).\]

Here \(\theta\) is the natural parameter, \(\phi\) is a dispersion parameter where applicable, and \(a,b,c\) are functions that describe the distribution. The distinction between \(\theta\) and the ordinary model coefficients \(\boldsymbol\beta\) matters: the natural parameter belongs to a distribution for one observation, whereas \(\boldsymbol\beta\) determines how Features influence the natural parameter through a link.

For a suitable exponential-family model, the mean and variance follow from \(b\):

\[E[Y]=b'(\theta),\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y)=a(\phi)b''(\theta).\]

Those derivatives are not merely decorative. They explain how the probability distribution determines the relation between mean and variance. We can verify each familiar family rather than asking the reader to accept the abstract notation on faith.

  • Poisson: \(\theta=\log\mu\), \(b(\theta)=e^\theta\), \(a(\phi)=1\), \(c(y,\phi)=-\log(y!)\). Then \(b'(\theta)=e^\theta=\mu\) and \(b''(\theta)=e^\theta=\mu\).
  • Bernoulli: \(\theta=\log[p/(1-p)]\), \(b(\theta)=\log(1+e^\theta)\). Differentiating gives \(b'(\theta)=p\) and \(b''(\theta)=p(1-p)\).
  • Normal with known variance \(\sigma^2\): \(\theta=\mu\), \(b(\theta)=\theta^2/2\), \(a(\phi)=\sigma^2\), hence \(b'(\theta)=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\sigma^2\).

One derivative in slow motion: for Bernoulli, begin with \(b(\theta)=\log(1+e^\theta)\). By the Chain Rule, the derivative of the outer logarithm is the reciprocal of its argument; multiply by the derivative of the inner expression, \(e^\theta\). Therefore \(b'(\theta)=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)=p\). Differentiate once more using the Quotient Rule: \(b''(\theta)=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)^2\). Now substitute \(p=e^\theta/(1+e^\theta)\) and \(1-p=1/(1+e^\theta)\); their product is precisely \(p(1-p)\). The familiar Bernoulli variance has emerged from the distribution's mathematical form, rather than being an unrelated formula to memorize.

For the Normal example, \(d(\theta^2/2)/d\theta=\theta\) and the second derivative equals 1; multiplication by \(a(\phi)=\sigma^2\) then gives the variance \(\sigma^2\). For Poisson, differentiating \(e^\theta\) twice gives the same expression both times, explaining the mean–variance equality. This is the deeper reason why the choice of outcome distribution has consequences for Loss Functions, uncertainty and diagnostics.

This mathematics explains why the canonical link sets the natural parameter equal to the linear predictor. It is a common, useful choice—not a requirement that every GLM always use its canonical link.

16. Canonical links, inverse links, and the meaning of a coefficient

The Link \(g\) maps a conditional mean to the linear scale: \(g(\mu)=\eta\). The Inverse Link maps a fitted score back into the original scale: \(g^{-1}(\eta)=\mu\). Reversing those directions causes interpretation errors, especially with a log or logit link.

Identity (Normal): if \(\eta=2+0.5x\), then \(\mu=2+0.5x\). Increasing \(x\) by one adds 0.5 units to the predicted mean. Logit (Bernoulli): if \(\eta=-1+0.8x\), then at \(x=0\), \(p=1/(1+e^1)\approx0.26894\), while at \(x=1\), \(p=1/(1+e^{0.2})\approx0.45017\). The coefficient 0.8 adds to log-odds, and \(e^{0.8}\approx2.2255\) multiplies the odds, not the probability. Log (Poisson): a coefficient 0.8 multiplies the expected rate by the same \(e^{0.8}\approx2.2255\), but here it is a Rate Ratio, not an Odds Ratio.

Do not mistake a log link for merely “take the log of the observed Target and run Linear Regression.” The latter creates a different error model, encounters problems with zero counts and may require retransformation corrections. A Poisson GLM models the original integer Target with a Poisson likelihood and uses the log of its conditional mean inside the link equation.

17. Bernoulli and Normal GLMs: connect the earlier guides without duplicating them

The Bernoulli Log-Likelihood for one observed binary value \(y\) and predicted probability \(p\) is \(\ell=y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)\). Suppose two customers have observed Labels \((1,0)\) and the fitted probabilities are \((0.8,0.3)\). Their joint Likelihood, assuming conditional independence, is \(0.8\times(1-0.3)=0.56\). The joint negative Log-Likelihood is \(-\log(0.56)\approx0.579818\), or a mean Binary Cross-Entropy of about 0.289909 across two observations. This is exactly the probabilistic logic behind Logistic Regression in Guide 10.

For a Normal GLM with identity link and fixed common variance \(\sigma^2\), the Log-Likelihood includes \(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\mu_i)^2\) plus terms not depending on coefficients. Maximizing it is therefore equivalent to minimizing SSE. That is why Ordinary Least Squares (Guide 7) is also a likelihood-based estimation method under suitable Gaussian assumptions. The equivalence concerns coefficient estimation; inferences and prediction intervals require additional conditions.

Three training objectives are therefore related, but not interchangeable: Gaussian likelihood corresponds to squared-error fitting (with common variance); Bernoulli likelihood leads to Binary Cross-Entropy; and Poisson likelihood leads to the count-specific negative Log-Likelihood. One is not universally better than another: the Target type, conditional distribution and intended prediction task determine which is appropriate.

18. Evaluate Validation counts on exactly the same windows

19. Poisson Deviance: evaluate the likelihood-based fit step by step

For an observed nonnegative count \(y_i\) and a strictly positive predicted mean \(\mu_i\), the individual Poisson Deviance contribution is

\[d_i=2\left[y_i\log\left(\frac{y_i}{\mu_i}\right)-(y_i-\mu_i)\right].\]

For \(y_i=0\), define \(y_i\log(y_i/\mu_i)=0\) by its mathematical limit. Deviance compares the fitted model's Log-Likelihood with a saturated model that fits each observation perfectly; its value is nonnegative and smaller means a better likelihood-based fit on the same data. It is not the probability that the model is wrong, nor should different response sets be compared naively by raw total Deviance.

Work through V1: it has \(y=1\) and \(\mu=4/3\). Therefore

\[d_{V1}=2\left[1\log(1/(4/3))-(1-4/3)\right] =2\left[-0.287682+0.333333\right]\approx0.091303.\]

The other model contributions are approximately V2 0.040032, V3 0.434883, and V4 0.058849; sum them to obtain Model Validation Deviance ≈ 0.625065. The same calculation with baseline predicted means gives Baseline Validation Deviance ≈ 5.553995. This agrees with the MAE comparison for this particular dataset, but different metrics need not always prefer the same model.

A residual based on Deviance is distinct from the raw residual \(y-\mu\) and the Pearson residual \((y-\mu)/\sqrt\mu\). The latter rescales by the Poisson standard deviation and is useful for diagnostics, though its interpretation is limited for very small counts or samples.

21. Assumptions, mean–variance relationships, and diagnostics

The usual Poisson GLM assumes: (a) the conditional count distribution is approximately Poisson given the included Features and exposure; (b) the log mean is correctly related to the Features; (c) the relevant events/observations are conditionally independent for the likelihood calculation; and (d) the exposure measurement is appropriate. Missing Features, event clustering, mismeasured hours and evolving reporting practices can violate these conditions. A good-looking Training curve cannot repair an incorrect data-generating assumption.

A hallmark of the Poisson distribution is \(\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid x,e)=E[Y\mid x,e]=\mu\). If real windows exhibit larger conditional variance, we call that Overdispersion; smaller variance is Underdispersion. A basic descriptive diagnostic is \(\hat\phi_P=\sum_i (y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/\hat\mu_i\,/\,(n-p)\), where \(n\) is the Training sample size and \(p\) the number of fitted coefficients, provided the denominator is positive.

For our eight Training rows and two coefficients, the Pearson sum is approximately 0.852273, so \(\hat\phi_P=0.852273/(8-2)\approx0.1420\). The small value reflects how deliberately regular our toy counts are. Do not diagnose the population as genuinely underdispersed from this tiny constructed sample. With real data, inspect Pearson and Deviance residual plots, clusters by time/station, excess zeros, high-leverage observations, and uncertainty estimates.

Poisson regression's coefficient point estimates may remain useful for a conditional mean relationship under some departures from equidispersion, but standard Poisson standard errors and distributional predictions can then be misleading. Assess robust or clustered standard errors, Quasi-Poisson, or Negative Binomial alternatives as dictated by the evidence and question—not merely because they sound more advanced.

22. When Poisson is not enough: useful alternatives and trade-offs

Negative Binomial Regression is often useful when variance exceeds mean even after accounting for covariates; one common parameterization has \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\mu+\alpha\mu^2\) with \(\alpha>0\). This allows conditional extra-Poisson variation but introduces additional parameters and assumptions. It is not automatically preferable when sample sizes are tiny or the excess variance arises from a correctable data-definition problem.

Zero-inflated and hurdle models address different mechanisms that can create many zero counts. A zero-inflated model mixes a structural-zero process with a count distribution; a hurdle model separately models zero versus positive counts and then positive count magnitudes. Simply seeing zeros does not justify either: a Poisson with small \(\mu\) predicts many zeros naturally. Our normal one-hour fitted Poisson already assigns approximately 26.36% probability to zero alerts.

Binomial GLMs are appropriate when each observation counts successes out of a known number of trials—for example, 3 late inspections out of 20 scheduled inspections—whereas Poisson is often used for events over a measured exposure. Gamma GLMs may model positive, continuous, right-skewed quantities such as repair duration; Gamma is not for unrestricted negative values or discrete counts. These are distributional choices tied to the Target, not interchangeable labels.

A decision tree or other flexible model may outperform a GLM, but a transparent GLM can still be valuable when interpretation and uncertainty matter. Model complexity should be justified by representative held-out performance and the quality of assumptions.

23. Data Leakage, preprocessing, and reproducibility in a GLM workflow

Suppose the Peak indicator is defined from a timetable published before the monitoring window. It is available when forecasting and is a legitimate Feature. But an “incident severity” flag recorded after an alert is generated would leak information from the future if included in a forecast made at the start of the period. The same is true of using the final alert count to decide retrospectively whether a period was busy. Define the prediction timestamp, Features and Target before extracting any dataset.

The exposure offset must remain a correctly measured, strictly positive number. If monitoring duration is missing, its omission or imputation needs a documented policy; replacing zero exposure with a tiny positive number to avoid a logarithm error does not repair a meaningless observation. If additional Features require Standardization, encoding or missing-value imputation, learn their parameters on Training alone and reuse them unchanged on Validation, Test and future production windows. The offset itself is not estimated by StandardScaler.

For genuine time-indexed data, protect chronology and label maturity. If the station has repeated windows, consider grouping station IDs and handling temporal dependence rather than randomly distributing nearly identical adjacent periods among splits. Store the unit definitions, dataset version, coefficients, evaluation period, software versions and intended deployment population. A fully specified scientific procedure is much more reproducible than a screenshot of a fitted summary table.

)

Reproduce the new case with NumPy

This standalone program verifies exposure-aware fitting, naive OLS and expected Poisson frequencies. The assert statements fail if results disagree with our hand calculations.

import math
import numpy as np

# Eight synthetic Training shifts and four untouched Validation shifts.
train_peak = np.array([0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1], dtype=int)
train_hours = np.array([1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2], dtype=float)
train_tickets = np.array([1, 2, 2, 3, 2, 5, 3, 6], dtype=int)
val_peak = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1], dtype=int)
val_hours = np.array([1, 2, 1, 2], dtype=float)
val_tickets = np.array([1, 4, 3, 4], dtype=int)

lambda_normal = train_tickets[train_peak == 0].sum() / train_hours[train_peak == 0].sum()
lambda_peak = train_tickets[train_peak == 1].sum() / train_hours[train_peak == 1].sum()
beta0 = math.log(lambda_normal)
beta1 = math.log(lambda_peak / lambda_normal)
mu = val_hours * np.exp(beta0 + beta1 * val_peak)

# Naive OLS with an intercept and Peak as the only regressor, no exposure.
X = np.column_stack((np.ones(len(train_peak)), train_peak))
ols_coef = np.linalg.lstsq(X, train_tickets.astype(float), rcond=None)[0]
naive = ols_coef[0] + ols_coef[1] * val_peak
mae_glm = np.mean(np.abs(val_tickets - mu))
mae_naive = np.mean(np.abs(val_tickets - naive))

# Expected number of Validation shifts in each possible count bin.
# This sums four Poisson probabilities, not four rounded fitted counts.
expected_bins = np.array([
    sum(math.exp(-m) * m**k / math.factorial(k) for m in mu)
    for k in range(6)
] + [sum(1 - sum(math.exp(-m) * m**k / math.factorial(k)
                    for k in range(6)) for m in mu)])
observed_bins = np.array([(val_tickets == k).sum() for k in range(6)]
                         + [(val_tickets >= 6).sum()])

np.testing.assert_allclose([lambda_normal, lambda_peak], [4/3, 8/3])
np.testing.assert_allclose([beta0, beta1], [math.log(4/3), math.log(2)])
np.testing.assert_allclose(mu, [4/3, 8/3, 8/3, 16/3])
np.testing.assert_allclose(ols_coef, [2, 2])
np.testing.assert_allclose([mae_glm, mae_naive], [5/6, 1.0])
np.testing.assert_allclose(expected_bins.sum(), len(val_tickets))
print('Training rates:', lambda_normal, lambda_peak, 'tickets/hour')
print('Poisson Validation expected counts:', np.round(mu, 6))
print('MAE Poisson and naive OLS:', round(mae_glm, 6), round(mae_naive, 6))
print('Observed bins:', observed_bins)
print('Poisson expected frequencies:', np.round(expected_bins, 6))

25B. Fit a Poisson Regression by Maximum Likelihood, step by step

Normal Training shifts total \(1+2+2+3=8\) tickets in \(1+2+1+2=6\) hours; Peak shifts total \(2+5+3+6=16\) tickets in \(6\) hours. By the estimator derived in Section 12:

\[\hat\lambda_0=\frac{8}{6}=\frac43=1.3333,\qquad\hat\lambda_1=\frac{16}{6}=\frac83=2.6667\quad\text{tickets/hour}.\]

Using \(\log\mu_i=\log e_i+\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\), we get \(\hat\beta_0=\log(4/3)=0.287682\) and \(\hat\beta_1=\log((8/3)/(4/3))=\log2=0.693147\). Therefore the Rate Ratio is \(e^{\hat\beta_1}=2\): at equal exposure the model expects twice the tickets during Peak. This is a synthetic conditional association, not causal evidence that changing a shift's status doubles requests.

25C. Predict untouched shifts and compare a naive alternative

Without refitting, the four Poisson forecasts are \((1)(4/3)=1.3333\), \((2)(4/3)=2.6667\), \((1)(8/3)=2.6667\), and \((2)(8/3)=5.3333\), while observed counts are \([1,4,3,4]\). Absolute errors are \(1/3,4/3,1/3,4/3\), so \(\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{Poisson}}=(1+4+1+4)/(3\cdot4)=10/12=0.8333\) tickets/shift.

For a deliberately limited comparison, fit Ordinary Least Squares using an Intercept and Peak without Exposure. Training means are \(8/4=2\) normal tickets/shift and \(16/4=4\) Peak tickets/shift, giving OLS \(\hat y=2+2x\). The Validation forecasts \([2,2,4,4]\) have absolute errors \([1,2,1,0]\), hence \(\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{OLS}}=4/4=1\). Poisson has lower MAE on these four shifts, not proof of universal superiority. This comparison changes both the response family and the Exposure treatment: it does not isolate which decision explains the difference. A general linear-count model also does not constrain fitted counts to be nonnegative when extrapolating.

25D. From a predicted mean to a distribution of possible counts

Do not round 1.3333 or 5.3333 and pretend they are the only possible counts. Each shift has its own distribution \(Y_i\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\hat\mu_i)\). For V3, \(\hat\mu=8/3\), hence \(P(Y=0)=e^{-8/3}=0.06948\) and \(P(Y\ge1)=1-0.06948=0.93052\). To plot expected numbers of shifts with count \(k\), sum four probabilities: \(E[N_k]=\sum_{i=1}^{4}P(Y_i=k\mid\hat\mu_i)\). For example, \(E[N_0]=e^{-4/3}+e^{-8/3}+e^{-8/3}+e^{-16/3}\approx0.40739\). The \(6+\) bin uses \(P(Y_i\ge6)\), rather than assigning the tail to exactly six.

Expected bin frequencies for \(0,1,2,3,4,5,6+\) are approximately \(0.4074,\ 0.7478,\ 0.7971,\ 0.6654,\ 0.4903,\ 0.3390,\ 0.5530\), summing to \(4.0000\). Observed bin frequencies are \(0,1,0,1,2,0,0\). Different frequencies are entirely plausible in tiny datasets: four shifts cannot establish either miscalibration or good calibration. We would need many more representative windows plus checks of independence, Overdispersion and changes in demand.

Decision caveat: an expectation of 5.333 tickets does not determine how many agents to hire. Resolution times, service capacity, costs and operational risk still matter. We must also exclude tickets opened after a shift begins from the Features used at prediction time. The goal is to justify a distribution, link, estimation and honest evaluation—not to claim an operational policy has been validated.

A complete reasoning chain is now available: determine the Target type → choose a reasonable conditional distribution → specify a linear score and link → handle exposure → maximize Training Likelihood → interpret coefficients on the correct scale → evaluate held-out predictions → audit assumptions and uncertainty. The order is important. If the response is binary, a Poisson count model is generally a different question from the Bernoulli model; if monitoring durations differ, ignoring exposure changes what is being predicted.

Our station example predicts expected numbers of alerts, not a guaranteed count and not the effect of adding a technician. A forecast of 7.33 alerts during a peak two-hour window can inform planning, but choosing additional staffing requires a separate decision analysis: workforce capacity, costs, risk of severe events and intervention efficacy. If outcomes are consequential, predictions must be examined alongside uncertainty, appropriate human oversight and evidence from actual deployments.

Three conceptual contrasts deserve a final check. Mean versus observation: \(\mu=3.67\) is valid although an observed count is integer. Rate Ratio versus Odds Ratio: the exponentiated coefficient is interpreted relative to the chosen link and response family. Fit versus evaluate: maximizing likelihood on Training never justifies describing Training accuracy as proof of generalization. These distinctions will recur throughout our 33-guide program.

26. Common mistakes: identify the misconception and repair it

1. Treating a count as continuous without justification. A straight line can predict negative counts. Repair: check response support and consider a count likelihood when appropriate. A Gaussian approximation can be useful in some high-count settings, but it needs justification rather than being a default.

2. Confusing expected count with event probability. \(\mu=3\) means three expected events, not a 300% chance of at least one. Repair: for Poisson, compute \(P(Y\ge1)=1-e^{-\mu}\); at \(\mu=3\) this is about 95.0%.

3. Ignoring exposure. Comparing eight events in two hours with four events in one hour as if the first rate were higher is incorrect. Repair: model the rate and include \(\log e\) as an offset when the proportional-exposure assumption fits.

4. Learning the coefficient of the offset. An offset is a known log multiplier with fixed coefficient 1, unlike a Feature whose effect is estimated. Repair: distinguish an offset argument from adding exposure as an ordinary predictor.

5. Reading a log coefficient as an additive count change. \(\beta_1=1.0116\) does not add 1.0116 alerts. Repair: exponentiate and interpret \(e^{\beta_1}=2.75\) as a multiplicative Rate Ratio, conditional on other Features and exposure.

6. Confusing a Rate Ratio with an Odds Ratio. Both may be written \(e^\beta\), but refer to different underlying outcomes and links. Repair: state the modeled quantity explicitly before interpreting the exponent.

7. Using Validation or Test to fit coefficients. Changing group rates after seeing V3 or E4 contaminates evaluation. Repair: estimate on Training, select on Validation, and test a frozen procedure.

8. Taking \(\log y\) and claiming to fit Poisson Regression. That fits a transformed response with different errors and fails at zero counts. Repair: use the Poisson likelihood for the original counts with a log link on the conditional mean.

9. Assuming every dataset satisfies \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=E[Y]\). Real alerts may cluster around outages. Repair: examine overdispersion, dependence and alternative count models; do not infer assumptions solely from a tiny synthetic sample.

10. Interpreting association as an intervention effect. A peak coefficient is predictive, not proof that changing station schedules prevents alerts. Repair: distinguish risk modeling from causal intervention studies.

11. Comparing Deviance across different held-out datasets without context. Total Deviance depends on observations and sizes. Repair: compare candidates on the same rows, report denominators and consider mean scores and uncertainty.

12. Calling a fitted mean a guaranteed outcome. An expected count of 7.33 does not imply exactly seven or eight alerts. Repair: distinguish fitted mean, predictive count distribution and uncertainty.

13. Failing to define time or the Target. A final alert count is unavailable at the beginning of the monitored period. Repair: write an as-of definition and audit each Feature's availability.

14. Treating correct code as valid evidence. A successful optimizer can fit biased, dependent or unrepresentative observations. Repair: audit definitions, data quality, assumptions, sampling and held-out generalization.

27. Test your understanding: 21 problems with explained answers

1. What is the difference between an observed count and its expected count?

An observed count is a nonnegative integer recorded in one window, such as 4 alerts. An expected count \(\mu=3.6667\) is the mean under the model for hypothetical comparable windows; it may be fractional. Treating it as a guaranteed observation would erase the uncertainty represented by the Poisson distribution.

2. From the Training table, calculate the normal-activity event rate.

Normal windows T1–T4 contain \(1+3+2+2=8\) alerts over \(1+2+1+2=6\) hours, so \(\hat\lambda=8/6=4/3\approx1.3333\) alerts per hour. Dividing by four *windows* would yield 2 alerts per window, not the rate per hour, because exposures differ.

3. Compute the peak rate and Rate Ratio.

Peak windows contain \(4+7+3+8=22\) alerts during 6 hours. The rate is \(22/6=11/3\approx3.6667\) alerts/hour. Divide by the normal rate \(4/3\): \((11/3)/(4/3)=11/4=2.75\). The modeled peak rate is 2.75 times the normal rate, or 175% higher.

4. Derive the two coefficients from those rates.

The baseline log rate is \(\hat\beta_0=\log(4/3)=0.287682\). Since the peak log rate equals \(\beta_0+\beta_1\), solve \(\hat\beta_1=\log(11/3)-\log(4/3)=\log(11/4)=1.011601\). Checking \(e^{\beta_0+\beta_1}\) returns \(11/3\), as it must.

5. Predict the count for a three-hour peak window not present in the table.

First compute the peak rate \(\lambda=11/3\) alerts/hour, then multiply by exposure 3 hours: \(\mu=3(11/3)=11\) expected alerts. The result is conditional on the model and assumes the same hourly rate extends over the entire three-hour period; it is not a certain outcome.

6. If a period lasts twice as long, what should happen to its predicted rate and count?

With an exposure offset, \(\mu=e\lambda\). Doubling \(e\) doubles \(\mu\) if Features and the assumed rate remain fixed, while \(\lambda\) itself stays unchanged. If intensity varies with time, that assumption needs to be revised rather than assumed true.

7. Why must exposure be positive for a log offset?

The offset is \(\log e\), which is undefined at \(e=0\) and not a real number for negative exposure. A zero-hour record cannot supply a meaningful positive-time event rate in this formulation; inspect the record rather than substituting a tiny arbitrary positive number.

8. For a one-hour normal window, compute the probability of zero alerts.

The model mean is \(\mu=4/3\). The Poisson PMF with \(y=0\) is \(e^{-\mu}\mu^0/0!=e^{-4/3}\approx0.263597\). The complementary event probability is \(P(Y\ge1)=1-0.263597=0.736403\). Neither value is the expected count itself.

9. Why does the MLE for a constant rate equal total counts divided by total exposure?

After removing terms constant in \(\lambda\), \(\ell(\lambda)=(\sum y)\log\lambda-\lambda\sum e\). Differentiate: \(\ell'(\lambda)=(\sum y)/\lambda-\sum e\). Set to zero and solve \(\hat\lambda=(\sum y)/(\sum e)\). With positive counts the second derivative is negative, so it is a maximum.

10. Carry out the first intercept-only Newton update from 2 alerts/hour.

Start \(\beta=\log2\). Total predicted events are \(12e^\beta=24\); the score is \(30-24=6\) and Hessian \(-24\). Newton's step is \(-6/(-24)=+0.25\), giving \(\beta^{(1)}=0.943147\), with total expected alerts \(12e^{0.943147}\approx30.816610\). This demonstrates a complete update, not yet convergence.

11. Compute the Validation MAE of the peak-aware model.

Predictions for V1–V4 are \(4/3,8/3,11/3,22/3\) and actual counts are \(1,3,5,8\). Absolute errors are \(1/3,1/3,4/3,2/3\), summing to \(8/3\). Divide by four windows to get \(\mathrm{MAE}=2/3\approx0.6667\) alerts per window.

12. Calculate the Poisson Deviance contribution for Validation window V1.

Use \(y=1\) and \(\mu=4/3\): \(d=2[1\log(1/(4/3))-(1-4/3)]\). Because \(\log(3/4)\approx-0.287682\) and \(1-4/3=-1/3\), we get \(d=2[-0.287682+0.333333]\approx0.091303\). The contribution is nonnegative and is not a classification error probability.

13. Why is \(\exp(\beta_1)\) a Rate Ratio here, but an Odds Ratio in Logistic Regression?

The link defines the quantity being modeled. A Poisson log link makes \(\beta_1\) an additive log-rate effect, so exponentiating yields a ratio of rates. A Bernoulli logit link makes it an additive log-odds effect, so exponentiating yields a ratio of odds. The same mathematical exponential does not make rates and odds identical.

14. What is wrong with fitting a straight line to the log of the observed counts?

Some counts are zero, so their log is undefined. More fundamentally, ordinary least squares on log counts assumes a different noise structure from a Poisson likelihood for the original integers. A Poisson GLM instead models \(\log(E[Y\mid x,e])\) and estimates coefficients using the count likelihood.

15. Suppose Validation contains a zero-count window. How does its Deviance term simplify?

When \(y=0\), the conventional limit sets \(y\log(y/\mu)=0\). The contribution is \(d=2[0-(0-\mu)]=2\mu\). Therefore predicting many events in a window where none occur is penalized, without ever evaluating \(\log0\) numerically.

16. A real dataset has much larger variance than mean. What should the analyst do?

First examine the data definition, repeated windows, missing drivers, temporal clustering and the relationship between exposure and events. Check residual diagnostics and representative periods. Depending on evidence, consider Negative Binomial, robust or clustered uncertainty estimates, or a revised mean structure. Do not simply declare Poisson correct because an optimizer converged.

17. Is the peak coefficient evidence that changing a shift to peak activity causes extra alerts?

No. The coefficient summarizes a fitted association conditional on Features under the model. Peak may proxy passenger volume, reporting practices, weather or maintenance schedules. To identify a causal effect of a particular policy, additional design and assumptions (possibly an experiment or credible quasi-experiment) are needed.

18. Calculate the customer-support rates and Rate Ratio.

Four normal shifts total 8 tickets/6 hours, so \(\hat\lambda_0=8/6=4/3\). Peak has \(2+5+3+6=16\) tickets/6 hours, so \(\hat\lambda_1=16/6=8/3\). The Rate Ratio is \((8/3)/(4/3)=2\), and its log coefficient is \(\log2=0.693147\). The model predicts twice the rate, not a fixed addition of two tickets.

19. Reproduce the Validation MAE for both competing count models.

Observed counts are \([1,4,3,4]\). With its Offset, Poisson predicts \([4/3,8/3,8/3,16/3]\), giving absolute errors \([1/3,4/3,1/3,4/3]\). These sum to \(10/3\), and \(\mathrm{MAE}=(10/3)/4=5/6=0.83333\). Naive OLS predicts \([2,2,4,4]\), with errors \([1,2,1,0]\): \(\mathrm{MAE}=4/4=1\). Four observations cannot establish universal superiority, and two modeling choices changed simultaneously.

20. Calculate the probability of zero tickets in V3 and interpret it.

V3 has Peak=1 and one hour of Exposure, so \(\mu=(1)(8/3)=8/3\). Under Poisson, \(P(Y=0)=e^{-8/3}(8/3)^0/0!=e^{-8/3}=0.06948\). The probability of at least one ticket is \(1-0.06948=0.93052\). Neither is the expected count; the expected count remains \(8/3\).

21. Why does the plot show 0.4074 expected shifts with zero tickets?

Each shift contributes the probability of a zero count under its own \(\mu_i\): \(e^{-4/3},e^{-8/3},e^{-8/3},e^{-16/3}\). Summing gives \(0.407392\), the expected number of zero-ticket shifts. An observed count of shifts must be an integer, but an expected frequency need not be. All seven bins sum to four, including the \(6+\) tail; rounding fitted means before using the PMF would be incorrect.

28. Glossary, synthesis and next learning steps

Generalized Linear Model (GLM): a model combining an exponential-family conditional distribution, a linear predictor and a link function. Linear predictor: \(\eta=X\beta\), a score linear in the coefficients. Conditional mean: \(\mu=E[Y\mid X]\), the average response predicted under the model, not necessarily an observed value. Link: \(g(\mu)=\eta\); Inverse Link: \(\mu=g^{-1}(\eta)\). Canonical link: link that equates the natural parameter to the linear predictor in a standard exponential-family formulation.

Poisson Distribution: a distribution over nonnegative integer counts with mean equal to variance under its assumptions. Exposure: amount of time, distance or opportunity during which events can occur. Offset: known additive term on the linear-predictor scale with coefficient fixed at 1, such as \(\log\) exposure. Rate: expected events per exposure unit. Rate Ratio: multiplicative comparison of rates, \(e^{\beta_j}\) for a one-unit Feature difference under a Poisson log link.

Likelihood: probability/density expression interpreted as a function of parameters after the observations are known. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE): choosing parameters that maximize the likelihood (or log-likelihood) of Training outcomes. Score: derivative or gradient of log-likelihood with respect to parameters. Hessian: matrix of second derivatives; used by Newton–Raphson/IRLS optimization. Deviance: likelihood-based discrepancy relative to a saturated model. Overdispersion: observed conditional variance larger than specified by the chosen model. Data Leakage: use of information unavailable at prediction time or improper influence of evaluation data on model development.

You should now be able to explain, without relying on a software library, why an hourly event count suggests a Poisson candidate, why \(\log e\) is an offset, why \(\beta_1\) becomes a Rate Ratio, how Training counts determine the coefficients, and why Validation and Test serve different roles. The next guide can extend these probabilistic ideas to model evaluation and statistical interpretation, but the essential skill here is to trace a distributional assumption all the way to a testable prediction—and to recognize the limitations of that prediction.
