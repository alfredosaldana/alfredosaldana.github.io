1. A model has an error. How can it improve its own parameters?

A marketing team wants to estimate qualified leads per week from its weekly advertising expenditure. It has a small historical table and decides to begin with a straight-line prediction model. In Guide 7, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) provided a direct formula for fitting a line. Gradient Descent answers a different question: starting from imperfect coefficients, how can we systematically improve them without directly solving the entire system of equations?

Imagine turning two adjustment knobs: the intercept and the slope. After every adjustment, the model makes predictions and its Loss Function measures their errors. The next adjustment should improve the model, rather than relying on guessing. A gradient tells us which direction makes the Loss increase most rapidly; moving against it tends to reduce the Loss when the step is chosen appropriately.

Learning goal. By the end, you should be able to compute a derivative and a gradient, perform at least three consecutive parameter updates by hand, distinguish Batch, Stochastic and Mini-Batch Gradient Descent, diagnose an unsuitable Learning Rate, and explain why low Training Loss is not enough to guarantee a useful model.

A small, invented dataset will let us see every number. Later we will discuss why real marketing data require richer models, controls for time and other influences, and a separate Validation set. Nothing in our numerical example establishes that increasing advertising spending causes an increase in sales or leads.

2. Read the complete dataset and define a model

Each row represents a different illustrative week. Advertising spend is measured in units of $100, so \(x=3\) means $300 in that week. The Target \(y\) is the observed number of qualified leads. These five values were chosen for teaching; they are not business observations.

Week Spend \(x\) (hundreds of USD) Observed leads \(y\)
W1 0 1
W2 1 3
W3 2 4
W4 3 7
W5 4 9

Our starting model has two parameters:

\[\widehat y_i=b+wx_i\]

Here \(\widehat y_i\) is the prediction for week \(i\); \(b\) is the intercept (predicted leads when spend is $0); \(w\) is the slope (change in predicted leads for an additional $100 of spend); and \(x_i\) is the known spend. Predicted leads can be fractional even though observed leads are counts. A real count-modeling application might prefer Poisson or Negative Binomial Regression, but a line keeps optimization transparent.

We will initialize the parameters at \(b_0=0\) and \(w_0=0\). Initialization means selecting the values before any training updates. The initial model predicts zero for every week and is plainly inadequate. That is deliberate: the student should observe learning instead of being handed the solution.

Sanity check: if someone interprets \(x=3\) as $3 rather than $300, the estimated slope will be given the wrong unit. Feature units belong to the model definition and must be documented.

3. Define exactly what the algorithm is minimizing

A Residual is the signed difference \(r_i=\widehat y_i-y_i\). If a model predicts 5 leads when the actual number is 7, then \(r=-2\): the model underestimated the observed outcome by two leads. Residuals can be positive or negative, so simply adding them could cause cancellations between errors. We square them to obtain nonnegative penalties.

We will use the Half Mean Squared Error:

\[J(b,w)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(b+wx_i-y_i)^2,\qquad m=5.\]

Here \(J\) is the objective or Loss Function across all Training observations; \(m\) is the number of Training rows. The factor \(1/2\) is convenient: it cancels the factor of 2 when differentiating a square. This objective has the same minimizer as ordinary MSE because we multiply MSE by a positive constant of \(1/2\). To recover MSE, compute \(\mathrm{MSE}=2J\).

At \((b,w)=(0,0)\), all predictions are zero. The initial Residuals are \(-1,-3,-4,-7,-9\). Their squares are \(1,9,16,49,81\), summing to 156. Therefore:

\[J(0,0)=\frac{1+9+16+49+81}{2(5)}=\frac{156}{10}=15.6\ \text{leads}^2.\]

Loss has squared-leads units; the parameters themselves have units of leads (intercept) and leads per $100 (slope). We will distinguish this Training Objective from Validation Loss, which is calculated on separate rows but must not be used to calculate the Training gradient.

4. Start with one parameter: why a derivative tells us which way to move

Before updating two parameters simultaneously, imagine a simpler objective with one adjustable parameter:

\[J(\theta)=(\theta-3)^2.\]

This curve has its minimum at \(\theta=3\): any deviation increases the squared distance. A derivative measures how quickly the function changes when its input changes by a small amount. Here,

\[\frac{dJ}{d\theta}=2(\theta-3).\]

At \(\theta=0\), the derivative is \(-6\). The negative sign says that increasing \(\theta\) slightly will lower the Loss. At \(\theta=5\), the derivative is \(+4\), so reducing \(\theta\) will lower the Loss. At \(\theta=3\), the derivative is zero: this point is stationary.

What a derivative does NOT say. The derivative does not directly tell us the location of the minimum from an arbitrary starting point, nor does a zero derivative always mean a minimum. In nonconvex functions, stationary points may be maxima or saddle points. The bowl shown here is a special, well-behaved convex example.

5. Derive the update rule and perform four complete one-parameter steps

Gradient Descent makes a parameter update of the form:

\[\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\alpha\,\frac{dJ}{d\theta}(\theta_t).\]

Here \(t\) is the iteration number and \(\alpha>0\) is the Learning Rate, controlling the length of the step. The minus sign matters: we move in the direction opposite the derivative. With our quadratic objective and \(\alpha=0.25\), the first update is:

\[\theta_1=0-0.25(-6)=1.5,\quad J(\theta_1)=(1.5-3)^2=2.25.\]

Compare the initial Loss \(J(0)=9\) with \(J(1.5)=2.25\). The decrease is \(9-2.25=6.75\). Continue, recalculating the derivative at the new parameter after each step:

Iteration \(t\) Current \(\theta_t\) Derivative \(2(\theta_t-3)\) Updated \(\theta_{t+1}\) New Loss
0 0 −6 1.5 2.25
1 1.5 −3 2.25 0.5625
2 2.25 −1.5 2.625 0.140625
3 2.625 −0.75 2.8125 0.03515625

On row 2, for example, \(\theta_3=2.25-0.25(-1.5)=2.625\); its Loss is \((-0.375)^2=0.140625\). The algorithm approaches 3, while each successive step becomes smaller because the derivative magnitude shrinks.

What have we actually learned? A mathematical rule computes a slope of the Loss surface; a Learning Rate converts the slope into a change in the parameter; new Loss verifies whether that change helped. There is no trial-and-error guessing of an answer after every row.

6. Learning Rate: small, suitable and too large are not the same

For the same simple quadratic \(J(\theta)=(\theta-3)^2\), substituting its derivative into the update gives:

\[\theta_{t+1}-3=(1-2\alpha)(\theta_t-3).\]

This identity reveals why Learning Rate matters. Every update multiplies the distance from the optimum by \(1-2\alpha\). For this specific objective, the iterates converge when \(|1-2\alpha|<1\), equivalently \(0<\alpha<1\). This interval is not a universal Learning Rate rule for other datasets or models.

  • With \(\alpha=0.05\), the distance is multiplied by \(0.90\): stable, but relatively slow.
  • With \(\alpha=0.25\), the distance is multiplied by \(0.50\): each step halves the error in \(\theta\).
  • With \(\alpha=0.50\), the factor is 0: the optimum is reached in a single step for this exact quadratic.
  • With \(\alpha=1.10\), the factor is \(-1.20\): the parameter jumps across the minimum and the distance grows.

For \(\alpha=1.10\), starting from zero: \(\theta_1=6.6\), then \(\theta_2=6.6-1.10(7.2)=-1.32\). Loss increases from 9 to 12.96 to 18.6624. This is divergence, not evidence that the true optimum disappeared. Check both Learning Rate and Feature Scaling before assuming the algorithm or data are inherently bad.

7. Two parameters require two partial derivatives

Our real line \(\widehat y=b+wx\) uses two parameters, so it has a gradient, not a single derivative. A partial derivative asks how the Loss changes when one parameter moves while the other is temporarily treated as fixed.

\[\nabla J(b,w)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}\\[3pt]\frac{\partial J}{\partial w}\end{bmatrix}.\]

The symbol \(\nabla\) is read nabla or del. The two entries of this vector tell us how the Loss responds locally to moving the intercept or the slope. Both parameters must be updated from the same current iteration; updating the intercept first and then recalculating the slope-gradient using the new intercept would be a different algorithm.

For \(r_i=b+wx_i-y_i\), we will derive:

\[\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m r_i,\qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial w}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m x_i r_i.\]

Notice what changes: the intercept-gradient averages all Residuals, while the slope-gradient averages each Residual weighted by its corresponding Feature value. This is why the slope-gradient cannot be found by simply copying the intercept-gradient.

8. Where do those gradient formulas come from? The Chain Rule

This derivation is worth doing once carefully rather than memorizing the result. Start with the contribution from one observation:

\[J_i(b,w)=\frac12(b+wx_i-y_i)^2=\frac12 r_i^2.\]

The Chain Rule differentiates a nested function: first differentiate the outer square, then multiply by the derivative of its inner expression. With respect to \(b\), the inner derivative is \(\partial r_i/\partial b=1\):

\[\frac{\partial J_i}{\partial b}=\frac12(2r_i)(1)=r_i.\]

With respect to \(w\), the inner derivative is \(\partial r_i/\partial w=x_i\):

\[\frac{\partial J_i}{\partial w}=\frac12(2r_i)(x_i)=x_i r_i.\]

Our complete objective is the mean of these five half-squared errors, so we divide the sum of their derivatives by \(m=5\). This produces the formulas of Section 7. The data \(x_i,y_i\) are fixed during the derivative; the adjustable quantities are \(b,w\).

Common conceptual obstacle. The term \(x_i\) appears in the slope-gradient not because we arbitrarily multiply error by a Feature, but because the prediction \(b+wx_i\) changes by \(x_i\) units for a one-unit increase in \(w\). This is the Chain Rule connecting parameter changes to prediction changes to Loss changes.

Divide each sum by the five Training rows:

\[\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}=\frac{-24}{5}=-4.8,\qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial w}=\frac{-68}{5}=-13.6.\]

Using \(\alpha=0.10\) and updating both coordinates simultaneously:

\[b_1=0-0.10(-4.8)=0.48,\qquad w_1=0-0.10(-13.6)=1.36.\]

The new prediction rule is \(\widehat y=0.48+1.36x\). It is no longer predicting zero for every week. But before congratulating ourselves, we must recompute its Loss on all five observations.

11. The second and third parameter updates: no hidden arithmetic

For the second update, start from the newly calculated Residuals \(-0.52,-1.16,-0.80,-2.44,-3.08\). Their sum is \(-8.00\). Their Feature-weighted sum is:

\[0(-0.52)+1(-1.16)+2(-0.80)+3(-2.44)+4(-3.08)=-22.40.\]

Therefore \(\partial J/\partial b=-8/5=-1.60\) and \(\partial J/\partial w=-22.4/5=-4.48\). Apply \(\alpha=0.10\):

\[b_2=0.48-0.10(-1.60)=0.64,\quad w_2=1.36-0.10(-4.48)=1.808.\]

The model becomes \(\widehat y=0.64+1.808x\). Its predictions are \(0.64,2.448,4.256,6.064,7.872\); the Residuals are \(-0.360,-0.552,+0.256,-0.936,-1.128\). Square and sum:

\[0.129600+0.304704+0.065536+0.876096+1.272384=2.648320.\]

So \(J_2=2.648320/10=\mathbf{0.264832}\).

For the third update, do not reuse the old gradient. Compute \(\sum r_i=-2.720\) and

\[\sum x_ir_i=0(-0.360)+1(-0.552)+2(0.256)+3(-0.936)+4(-1.128)=-7.360.\]

The new gradient is \((-0.544,-1.472)\). Update:

\[b_3=0.64-0.10(-0.544)=0.6944,\quad w_3=1.808-0.10(-1.472)=1.9552.\]

This gives predictions \(0.6944,2.6496,4.6048,6.5600,8.5152\). Their squared Residuals sum to \(1.0105856\), giving \(J_3=0.10105856\).

12. Compare the iterative result with the OLS solution

Our numerical example has measurement variation, so Gradient Descent should not drive the Training Loss to zero. To know the optimum independently, solve the OLS Normal Equations from Guide 7. For \(x=0,1,2,3,4\), the relevant sums are:

\[m=5,\quad \sum x=10,\quad \sum x^2=30,\quad \sum y=24,\quad \sum xy=68.\]

For a line with intercept, these equations are:

\[\begin{bmatrix}5&10\\10&30\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}b\\w\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}24\\68\end{bmatrix}.\]

The first equation is \(5b+10w=24\), or \(b+2w=4.8\). Twice the first equation gives \(10b+20w=48\). Subtract it from the second equation \(10b+30w=68\), obtaining \(10w=20\Rightarrow w=2\). Substitute into \(b+2w=4.8\): \(b=0.8\).

\[\boxed{\widehat y_{\mathrm{OLS}}=0.8+2x}.\]

Its predictions \(0.8,2.8,4.8,6.8,8.8\) yield Residuals \(-0.2,-0.2,+0.8,-0.2,-0.2\). SSE is \(0.04+0.04+0.64+0.04+0.04=0.80\), so \(J_{\min}=0.80/10=0.08\).

After only three Gradient Descent updates, \(J_3\approx0.1011\), still above 0.08. More appropriate updates can approach that solution. OLS solves this small linear least-squares problem algebraically; Gradient Descent iteratively improves the coefficients and becomes useful when direct solutions are inconvenient or costly for larger models.

13. The gradient is a direction in parameter space—not a picture of the data

The line plot shows points \((x,y)\). A Loss Surface shows a different set of coordinates: parameter pairs \((b,w)\) versus their Loss. Every location on that surface corresponds to an entire prediction model. Gradient Descent does not move observations; it moves the parameter values that define the model.

The contour lines connect parameter combinations with equal Loss. The arrows show successive steps from \((b,w)=(0,0)\) toward approximately \((0.8,2)\). In a contour plot, the gradient is locally perpendicular to a level curve and points uphill; its negative points downhill. The trajectory need not travel straight to the optimum because the surface has different curvature in different directions.

The plot uses exactly the same Training dataset and objective as our calculations, not a decorative picture. Its minimum corresponds to the OLS solution. In larger problems we cannot display hundreds or millions of parameter dimensions, but the vector interpretation remains valid.

14. Why the initial point and the shape of the objective matter

For ordinary linear regression with a squared-error objective, \(J(b,w)\) is a convex quadratic in the parameters. Any local minimum is a global minimum, provided the objective is properly constructed; if the design matrix has full column rank, the coefficient minimizer is unique. This is why the OLS comparison makes sense and why a suitable fixed Learning Rate can converge.

If the columns of the Design Matrix are perfectly linearly dependent, different coefficient combinations may give identical predictions, so the solution need not be unique. Gradient Descent can still minimize Loss, but coefficients may not be separately identifiable.

For Neural Networks the objective is generally nonconvex. Different initializations may lead to different trajectories and stationary points, and gradient-based optimizers do not generally guarantee a global optimum. This distinction prevents a convenient introductory bowl from becoming a misleading claim about every Machine Learning problem.

Why the safe Learning Rate depends on the shape of this loss

The one-parameter example gave a safe range of Learning Rates for one particular parabola. In our five-week model, the slope and intercept interact. The resulting surface is a tilted quadratic bowl: some directions curve much more sharply than others. We can measure this curvature instead of guessing whether a Learning Rate such as 0.35 will work.

Start with the formulas already derived and replace the sums by their actual five-week values. Since \(\overline{x}=10/5=2\), \(\overline{x^2}=30/5=6\), \(\overline y=24/5=4.8\) and \(\overline{xy}=68/5=13.6\), the gradient for any coefficients \((b,w)\) is

\[\nabla J(b,w)=\begin{bmatrix}b+2w-4.8\\[3pt]2b+6w-13.6\end{bmatrix}.\]

Differentiate each gradient entry once more. The Hessian, denoted \(H\), is the matrix of second derivatives, which describes local curvature. Here it is constant because the objective is quadratic:

\[H=\begin{bmatrix}\partial^2J/\partial b^2&\partial^2J/\partial b\partial w\\[2pt]\partial^2J/\partial w\partial b&\partial^2J/\partial w^2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&6\end{bmatrix}.\]

The diagonal entries measure curvature when one coefficient changes on its own; the two off-diagonal 2s measure how the intercept and slope interact. The determinant is \(1(6)-2(2)=2>0\), and the top-left entry is positive, so this Hessian is positive definite: the loss has a unique minimum for these data. To identify the steepest principal curvature, solve the eigenvalue equation \(\det(H-\lambda I)=0\). An eigenvalue is the curvature factor in a direction that the matrix maps onto itself. Here \(I\) is the 2×2 identity matrix:

\[\det\!\begin{bmatrix}1-\lambda&2\\2&6-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(1-\lambda)(6-\lambda)-4=\lambda^2-7\lambda+2=0.\]

Using the quadratic formula, \(\lambda_{\pm}=(7\pm\sqrt{41})/2\). Therefore \(\lambda_{\max}=(7+\sqrt{41})/2\approx6.70156\) and \(\lambda_{\min}\approx0.29844\). For a positive-definite quadratic, the parameter error along each principal direction is multiplied per update by \(1-\alpha\lambda_j\). Requiring its absolute value to be below 1 yields the constant-step stability condition:

\[0<\alpha<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}=\frac{4}{7+\sqrt{41}}\approx\boxed{0.298438}.\]

This is a result for these five Training rows, this feature unit, this Half MSE definition and simultaneous full-batch updates—not a universal rule. Our \(\alpha=0.10\) is inside the interval. \(\alpha=0.25\) is also stable but initially oscillates along a steep direction, while \(\alpha=0.35\) is outside and diverges. The first step at 0.35 moves from \((0,0)\) to \((1.68,4.76)\) and increases loss from 15.6 to 28.1776, even though the gradient is calculated correctly.

Connect curvature to Feature Scaling. The numbers 6.70156 and 0.298438 change when we express advertising spending in dollars instead of hundreds of dollars, or standardize it using Training statistics. Scaling changes parameter-space geometry and therefore the usable numerical step size. This is why learning rates must be tuned for the actual model and preprocessing, using Training/Validation—not chosen from a universal memorized number.

15. Feature Scaling changes the geometry of learning

Suppose we represent the same marketing spend in actual dollars instead of hundreds of dollars: \(x_{\mathrm{USD}}=100x\). A slope of 2 leads per $100 corresponds to 0.02 leads per $1. Predictions can remain identical if we change the coefficient consistently, but the numerical gradient scales with the numeric representation of the Feature.

From the gradient formula \(\partial J/\partial w=(1/m)\sum x_ir_i\), multiplying every \(x_i\) by 100 multiplies that gradient component by 100 at the same predictions, while a suitable physical conversion of the slope goes in the opposite direction. The Learning Rate \(0.10\) that worked on \(x=0,1,2,3,4\) need not work when \(x=0,100,200,300,400\).

Standardization changes a Feature using parameters fitted on Training, such as \(z=(x-\mu_{\rm train})/\sigma_{\rm train}\). In our five weeks, \(\mu_x=(0+1+2+3+4)/5=2\), and the StandardScaler-style population standard deviation is \(\sigma_x=\sqrt{((0-2)^2+\cdots+(4-2)^2)/5}=\sqrt{2}\approx1.4142\). Thus \(x=4\) becomes \((4-2)/1.4142\approx1.4142\).

Fit the scaler on Training once. Apply its stored mean and scale to Validation, Test and new weeks. Recalculating them using held-out observations is a different and potentially leaky evaluation procedure (as shown in Guide 5). Scaling often improves numerical conditioning and makes Learning Rates easier to tune; it does not prove the underlying causal relationship or necessarily improve every model.

16. Batch, Stochastic and Mini-Batch Gradient Descent

The examples above compute a gradient from all five Training rows before each update. That is Batch Gradient Descent (also called Full-Batch Gradient Descent). Modern training often uses fewer rows per update.

Method Data used for one update Practical consequence
Batch GD All \(m\) Training rows Accurate full-data gradient; potentially costly for huge datasets
Stochastic GD (SGD) One Training row Cheap but noisy update; order and randomness matter
Mini-Batch GD A subset, e.g., 2 or 128 rows Balance of noisy progress and efficient vectorized computation

The same initial parameters produce different first gradients depending on the selected rows. For the five-week dataset:

  • Full batch: \(\nabla J=(-4.8,-13.6)\), the average of all five per-row gradients.
  • Single row W2 (\(x=1,y=3\)): \(\nabla J_{\mathrm{W2}}=(-3,-3)\). A step of 0.1 yields \((b,w)=(0.3,0.3)\), which is not the same as the full-batch update.
  • Mini-batch W1–W2: the two per-row gradients are \((-1,0)\) and \((-3,-3)\); their average is \((-2,-1.5)\). A step of 0.1 yields \((b,w)=(0.2,0.15)\).

The SGD or mini-batch gradient is an estimate based on selected observations, not a claim that those observations define the complete objective. If sampled appropriately, its expected gradient can match the full Training gradient; particular updates can temporarily increase the full Training Loss.

17. Iterations, epochs, shuffling and batch size

An iteration is one parameter update. An epoch usually means processing the Training dataset once, though the exact convention depends on the training procedure. With five observations and mini-batches of size two, one ordinary epoch contains batches of sizes 2, 2 and 1, hence three updates. With SGD, a full pass contains five updates; full-batch training produces one update per epoch.

Shuffling changes row order to reduce systematic order effects in SGD and mini-batch training. Reproducible experiments record the random seed and data ordering. But shuffling must respect the data structure: for chronological prediction or grouped observations, randomly mixing otherwise restricted future information into Training is not acceptable just because SGD benefits from shuffled rows.

The average Loss computed from the same fixed entire Training set is comparable between epochs; the Loss of an individual mini-batch is noisier and may vary simply because the observations differ. When comparing learning curves, label which quantity was plotted.

18. When should training stop, and how can we diagnose failure?

The phrase convergence means updates approach a stable or satisfactory objective value or parameter region; it does not imply that the model is correct in the real world. Different stopping strategies answer different questions:

  • Small gradient norm: \(\lVert\nabla J\rVert\) is near zero. Useful for smooth optimization, though saddle points also have small gradients.
  • Small improvement: successive objective values change by less than a declared tolerance. A tiny Learning Rate can produce tiny changes far from the optimum, so this alone is insufficient.
  • Maximum iterations or compute budget: a pragmatic safeguard, not a proof of convergence.
  • Early stopping on Validation: stop when independent Validation performance stops improving; this is a model-selection choice, not a step in the Training gradient.

If Training Loss becomes NaN or grows rapidly, inspect Learning Rate, Feature Scaling, exploding gradients, invalid input values and numerical overflow. If Training Loss decreases but Validation Loss increases, investigate overfitting, distribution differences and the need for regularization or early stopping. A low Training Loss is evidence of fit to Training—not automatic evidence of generalization.

19. Numerical gradient checking: catch a derivative error before trusting code

We can approximate a derivative using nearby objective values:

\[\frac{\partial J}{\partial w}\approx\frac{J(b,w+h)-J(b,w-h)}{2h}.\]

This is the central finite-difference approximation, where \(h\) is a small positive perturbation. It computes Loss twice while holding the other parameter fixed. At our starting point, the analytic slope-gradient is \(-13.6\); using a suitably small \(h\), the finite difference should return approximately \(-13.6\). Similarly, perturbing \(b\) gives approximately \(-4.8\).

Calculate it, rather than merely naming the method. Holding \(b=0\), our five-row objective along the slope direction is \(J(0,w)=15.6-13.6w+3w^2\). Choose \(h=0.01\): \(J(0,+0.01)=15.4643\) and \(J(0,-0.01)=15.7363\). The central difference is \((15.4643-15.7363)/(0.02)=-13.6\), exactly matching the analytic slope-gradient for this quadratic. Along the intercept direction, \(J(b,0)=15.6-4.8b+0.5b^2\); values \(J(+0.01,0)=15.55205\) and \(J(-0.01,0)=15.64805\) give \((15.55205-15.64805)/0.02=-4.8\). The numerical procedure checks both coordinates independently.

Finite differences are a useful debugging test, not normally the efficient way to train models with many parameters. Very large \(h\) makes the approximation coarse; extremely tiny \(h\) can increase floating-point cancellation errors. Automatic Differentiation systems implement the Chain Rule efficiently, but still benefit from sanity checks on small examples.

20. From Linear Regression to Logistic Regression and Neural Networks

Gradient Descent is an optimization procedure, not a synonym for Linear Regression. Different models create different objective functions and different gradients. For Binary Classification, Logistic Regression uses a probability:

\[p_i=\sigma(b+wx_i)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(b+wx_i)}}.\]

With Binary Cross-Entropy, the per-row derivative with respect to the linear score \(z_i=b+wx_i\) simplifies to \(p_i-y_i\). Consequently, the full-batch gradients take the familiar form \(\partial J/\partial b=(1/m)\sum(p_i-y_i)\) and \(\partial J/\partial w=(1/m)\sum x_i(p_i-y_i)\). The error term changed from \(\widehat y-y\) in squared-error regression to \(p-y\) under sigmoid-plus-cross-entropy; the update principle remains the same.

For intuition, if one Classification observation has \(p=0.80\), \(y=1\) and \(x=2\), then \(p-y=-0.20\); its contribution to the slope-gradient is \(2(-0.20)=-0.40\). At \(\alpha=0.10\), a single-row update would increase \(b\) by \(0.02\) and \(w\) by \(0.04\). This shows how an underestimated positive outcome can push its score upward; Guide 10 will derive the full probabilistic model.

Neural Networks contain many layers and parameters. Backpropagation is an efficient application of the Chain Rule to compute gradients through those layers; an optimizer such as SGD or Adam then uses the gradients to update parameters. Computing gradients and deciding the update are related but distinct operations.

21. Reproduce the result in Python—and understand each block

The following example starts from the same five rows, uses NumPy but no ready-made optimizer, and prints the first three updates. It then independently computes the OLS solution so students can compare the iterative and algebraic approaches. Values are deliberately small enough to audit manually.

import numpy as np

spend = np.array([0., 1., 2., 3., 4.])
leads = np.array([1., 3., 4., 7., 9.])

intercept = 0.0
slope = 0.0
rate = 0.10

for step in range(4):
    predictions = intercept + slope * spend
    residuals = predictions - leads
    loss = np.mean(residuals ** 2) / 2
    print(step, round(intercept, 6), round(slope, 6), round(loss, 8))

    grad_intercept = np.mean(residuals)
    grad_slope = np.mean(spend * residuals)
    intercept -= rate * grad_intercept
    slope -= rate * grad_slope

matrix = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(spend)), spend])
ols = np.linalg.lstsq(matrix, leads, rcond=None)[0]
print("OLS coefficients:", ols)

Read the code in groups. First, the two arrays contain exactly the same table as Section 2. Second, predictions - leads gives one signed Residual per observation. Third, the two means calculate the gradient components derived through the Chain Rule. Fourth, both coefficients are updated after the gradient has been computed from the same parameter values. Finally, np.linalg.lstsq independently estimates the least-squares coefficients without altering the Gradient Descent history.

Expected printed Loss values before each next update are approximately 15.6, 1.7696, 0.264832, 0.10105856. The OLS result is [0.8, 2.0]. A reproducible lab can extend this code to 100 iterations, compare Learning Rates, plot Loss and test a standardized Feature.

Step 1 — Derive the first gradient from all rows

At initialization predictions are \((0,0,0)\), and residuals \(r=\widehat y-y=(-2,-4,-6)\) minutes. Their sum is \(-2-4-6=-12\), so the intercept-gradient is \(-12/3=\boxed{-4}\). Weight the residuals by the Features: \(0(-2)+1(-4)+2(-6)=-16\), giving the slope-gradient \(-16/3\approx\boxed{-5.333333}\). Update both from the old parameter state:

\[b_1=0-0.10(-4)=0.4,\qquad w_1=0-0.10(-16/3)=0.533333\ldots\]

New predictions are \((0.4000,0.9333,1.4667)\). The residuals are \((-1.6000,-3.0667,-4.5333)\). Using the unrounded fractions, the new sum of squared residuals is \(32.5155556\), so \(J_1=32.5155556/6=\boxed{5.4192593}\). This is below \(J_0=(4+16+36)/6=56/6=\boxed{9.3333333}\).

Step 2 — Recompute, do not recycle, the gradient

Now \(\sum r_i=-1.6-3.0666667-4.5333333=-9.2\), giving \(g_b=-9.2/3=-3.0666667\). The weighted sum is \(0(-1.6)+1(-3.0666667)+2(-4.5333333)=-12.1333333\), giving \(g_w=-12.1333333/3=-4.0444444\). Thus:

\[b_2=0.4-0.1(-3.0666667)=\boxed{0.7066667},\quad w_2=0.5333333-0.1(-4.0444444)=\boxed{0.9377778}.\]

The second predictions are \((0.7066667,1.6444444,2.5822222)\); corresponding residuals are \((-1.2933333,-2.3555556,-3.4177778)\). Square each, add and divide by six: \(J_2=18.9025580/6=\boxed{3.1504263}\).

From those three residuals, \(\sum r_i=-7.0666667\) and \(\sum x_ir_i=0(-1.2933333)+1(-2.3555556)+2(-3.4177778)=-9.1911112\). Dividing by three gives \(g_b=-2.3555556\) and \(g_w=-3.0637037\). Hence:

\[b_3=0.7066667-0.1(-2.3555556)=\boxed{0.9422222},\quad w_3=0.9377778-0.1(-3.0637037)=\boxed{1.2441481}.\]

The third predictions \((0.9422222,2.1863704,3.4305185)\) lead to residuals \((-1.0577778,-1.8136296,-2.5694815)\), squared-residual sum \(11.0103813\), and \(J_3=11.0103813/6=\boxed{1.8350636}\). The table records every state so you can independently reproduce each result.

Independent answer and a held-out check

This tiny Training table was deliberately constructed from \(y=2+2x\). Verify the algebra instead of trusting that statement: with three observations, \(\sum x=3\), \(\sum x^2=5\), \(\sum y=12\) and \(\sum xy=16\), the normal equations are \(3b+3w=12\) and \(3b+5w=16\). Subtracting gives \(2w=4\Rightarrow w_{OLS}=2\); substituting gives \(b_{OLS}=2\). The OLS Training loss is zero on this synthetically perfect line. After just three Gradient Descent updates the loss is positive: optimization is still in progress, not mathematically incorrect.

A separate illustrative run, never used for coefficient updates, has \(x_{new}=3\) (300 devices) and observed \(y_{new}=8.5\) minutes. Our three-step model predicts \(0.9422222+1.2441481(3)=4.6746665\) minutes, so signed error \(4.6746665-8.5=-3.8253335\) minutes. The OLS Training fit predicts \(2+2(3)=8\), with error \(8-8.5=-0.5\) minutes. One held-out observation cannot establish comparative generalization. In a real project, parameters are learned with Training, Learning Rate and stopping choices are tuned with Validation, and Test is reserved for the final unbiased-as-possible assessment.

Reproduce every step with NumPy

The following independent block constructs the new dataset without hidden variables, prints every update, checks the OLS solution and asserts the stated results. Run it in one Jupyter cell or as a standalone Python script.

import numpy as np

production = np.array([0., 1., 2.])  # hundreds of devices
inspection = np.array([2., 4., 6.])  # minutes
b, w, learning_rate = 0., 0., 0.10
history = []

for iteration in range(4):
    residuals = b + w * production - inspection
    loss = np.mean(residuals ** 2) / 2
    history.append((b, w, loss))
    print(iteration, round(b, 7), round(w, 7), round(loss, 7))
    if iteration < 3:
        gradient_b = np.mean(residuals)
        gradient_w = np.mean(production * residuals)
        b_next = b - learning_rate * gradient_b
        w_next = w - learning_rate * gradient_w
        b, w = b_next, w_next

assert np.allclose(history[3], [0.9422222222222222,
                                 1.2441481481481481,
                                 1.8350635573845444])
training_matrix = np.column_stack([np.ones(3), production])
ols_b, ols_w = np.linalg.lstsq(training_matrix, inspection, rcond=None)[0]
assert np.allclose([ols_b, ols_w], [2., 2.])
new_production, new_observed_minutes = 3., 8.5
print('GD held-out prediction:', b + w * new_production)
print('OLS held-out prediction:', ols_b + ols_w * new_production)
print('OLS:', ols_b, ols_w)

marketing_x = np.array([0., 1., 2., 3., 4.])
hessian = np.array([[1., marketing_x.mean()],
                    [marketing_x.mean(), np.mean(marketing_x ** 2)]])
eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(hessian)
print('Hessian max eigenvalue:', eigenvalues[-1])
print('Critical learning rate:', 2 / eigenvalues[-1])
assert np.isclose(2 / eigenvalues[-1], 0.2984378812835757)
Three checks before claiming success. (1) Did you update the slope and intercept from the same old state? (2) Did you recompute the Training Loss after each update instead of using old residuals? (3) Did you keep the held-out observation out of all gradients? Reproducing the calculations and explaining these choices is stronger evidence of mastery than copying an optimizer call.

23. Common mistakes: the reason, the consequence and the correction

Mistake 1 — Confusing Gradient and Learning Rate

The gradient is computed from the objective and current data; the Learning Rate is a chosen multiplier. A large gradient does not automatically mean the Learning Rate must be large. Correction: report both quantities and evaluate resulting Loss and stability.

Mistake 2 — Moving with the gradient instead of against it

For minimization, \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\alpha\nabla J\). Adding the gradient typically moves uphill. Correction: confirm the minus sign with a simple quadratic whose optimum is known.

Mistake 3 — Updating the slope with the intercept formula

The slope-gradient includes \(x_ir_i\), not merely \(r_i\). Omitting the Feature factor changes the optimization problem. Correction: derive each partial derivative using the Chain Rule and check numeric gradients.

Mistake 4 — Mixing parameter states within one Batch update

If the intercept is changed before the slope-gradient is computed, the two components no longer represent one simultaneous Batch gradient. Correction: compute and store both components first, then update both coefficients.

Mistake 5 — Treating every falling Loss as evidence of generalization

Training Loss can fall while Validation Loss worsens. Correction: use a legitimate Training/Validation separation and monitor both with consistent definitions.

Mistake 6 — Equating one iteration with one epoch

Their relationship depends on batch size. Correction: explicitly state dataset size, batch size and update count.

Mistake 7 — Interpreting a noisy SGD step as a broken algorithm

One observation can point differently from the average gradient. Correction: inspect trends across batches and epochs rather than insisting every mini-batch update reduce full-data Loss.

Mistake 8 — Ignoring Feature units and scaling

Changing $100 units to $1 units alters gradients and the behavior of a fixed Learning Rate. Correction: specify units, fit any scaler on Training, and retune the Learning Rate when needed.

Mistake 9 — Stopping when updates are tiny without asking why

An extremely small Learning Rate also yields tiny steps far from the minimum. Correction: consider gradient norm, objective value, Validation behavior and practical tolerances together.

Mistake 10 — Assuming Gradient Descent always finds a global optimum

Convex least squares is not representative of every Neural Network objective. Correction: distinguish convex and nonconvex objectives, and report initialization and optimization conditions.

Mistake 11 — Using Validation or Test examples in the Training gradient

This contaminates independent evaluation. Correction: fit model and preprocessing on Training only; use Validation for selection and Test for final assessment.

Mistake 12 — Confusing optimizer success with causal evidence

A slope that predicts leads from advertising spend does not prove increasing spend causes that change. Correction: examine confounding, experimental design and the actual decision before making causal claims.

24. Knowledge checks: reason, calculate, verify

1. What does the derivative \(dJ/d\theta=-6\) at \(\theta=0\) tell you?

For a sufficiently small positive change in \(\theta\), Loss tends to decrease: the local slope is negative. With our particular \(J=(\theta-3)^2\), increasing \(\theta\) moves toward the minimum at 3. The derivative alone does not specify a universally safe step size.

2. Complete one update of \(J=(\theta-3)^2\) from \(\theta=0\) at \(\alpha=0.25\).

The derivative is \(2(0-3)=-6\). Update: \(\theta_1=0-0.25(-6)=1.5\). Initial Loss is 9; new Loss is \((1.5-3)^2=2.25\). The update reduced Loss by 6.75.

3. Why does the Half MSE divide by \(2m\), and does that change the minimum?

The 2 cancels the derivative of the square. Half MSE equals \(\mathrm{MSE}/2\), so it has exactly the same minimizers as MSE for a fixed dataset. The numerical Loss and gradient are scaled; a Learning Rate may need adjustment if the objective's scale changes.

4. Recalculate the starting intercept-gradient for the five weeks.

At \(b=w=0\), predictions are 0, so Residuals are −1, −3, −4, −7, −9. Sum = −24; divide by \(m=5\): \(\partial J/\partial b=-24/5=-4.8\). It is an average Residual, not an average squared Residual.

5. Why is the starting slope-gradient −13.6 rather than −4.8?

Each Residual contributes its Feature-weighted value \(x_ir_i\). The sum is 0 − 3 − 8 − 21 − 36 = −68, so the mean is −68/5 = −13.6. This arises from \(\partial(b+wx_i-y_i)/\partial w=x_i\).

6. Compute both coefficients after the first Batch update with \(\alpha=0.10\).

The starting gradient is (−4.8, −13.6). Thus \(b_1=0−0.1(−4.8)=0.48\) and \(w_1=0−0.1(−13.6)=1.36\). Both values come from the same initial parameter state.

7. Confirm the first updated Loss from all five predictions.

Predictions are 0.48, 1.84, 3.20, 4.56 and 5.92. Squared Residuals total 17.696. Divide by \(2m=10\), obtaining \(J=1.7696\), lower than the starting 15.6.

8. Carry out the second update without reusing the first gradient.

The new Residuals sum to −8.0 and weighted Residuals sum to −22.4. Hence the gradient is (−1.6,−4.48), giving \(b_2=0.48+0.16=0.64\) and \(w_2=1.36+0.448=1.808\). The new Loss is 0.264832.

9. Show how to obtain the third-step coefficients.

At \((0.64,1.808)\), the Residuals sum to −2.720 and their Feature-weighted sum is −7.360. Divide each by 5: gradient (−0.544,−1.472). Update at 0.1: \(b_3=0.6944\), \(w_3=1.9552\); \(J_3=0.10105856\).

10. What does the OLS benchmark tell us about convergence?

The exact least-squares minimizer for these Training rows is \((b,w)=(0.8,2)\), where \(J=0.08\). The third Gradient Descent iterate has larger Loss, 0.10105856. Optimization has improved the model substantially but has not yet reached the minimum.

11. Count updates per epoch for five rows at mini-batch size two.

Three batches are needed: two rows, two rows and one row. Therefore there are three updates per ordinary pass (epoch). SGD with batch size one gives five updates per epoch; full-batch gives one.

12. Calculate the first SGD update using only W2.

At initialization, W2 has \(x=1,y=3\) and Residual −3, so its per-row gradient is (−3,−3). With \(\alpha=0.1\), the update gives (0.3,0.3). This differs legitimately from the full-batch result (0.48,1.36).

13. Why can \(\alpha=1.1\) diverge on the simple quadratic?

The error from the optimum is multiplied by \(1-2\alpha=-1.2\); its magnitude increases by 20% on every step. From \(\theta_0=0\), the first two updates are 6.6 and −1.32, with Loss rising to 12.96 and 18.6624.

14. Explain why feature scaling can require a new Learning Rate.

Changing the spend Feature from hundreds of dollars to dollars multiplies numeric x by 100 and changes the slope-gradient at the same predictions by a factor of 100. A fixed step multiplier therefore behaves differently. Consistent parameter units and Training-fitted scaling are crucial.

15. Does a decreasing Training Loss prove marketing spending caused the observed leads?

No. Gradient Descent only optimizes a predictive objective on provided observations. Time trends, seasonality, other campaigns, promotion quality or selection effects may influence both variables. Causal effects require suitable identification or experimentation.

16. Sensitivity: calculate the first marketing update with Learning Rate 0.35 and decide whether it is stable.

From the known initial gradient \((-4.8,-13.6)\), \(b_1=0-0.35(-4.8)=1.68\) and \(w_1=0-0.35(-13.6)=4.76\). Using all five Training rows gives \(J(1.68,4.76)=28.1776\), larger than \(15.6\). Independently the Hessian has \(\lambda_{max}\approx6.70156\), so the stable fixed-step range is \(0<\alpha<2/6.70156\approx0.298438\). Because 0.35 exceeds it, the full-batch quadratic iteration diverges from generic non-optimal starts. The number 0.298438 is specific to the problem and parameterization.

17. Transfer: perform the first quality-control update if the Learning Rate were 0.20 instead of 0.10.

For \(x=(0,1,2)\) and \(y=(2,4,6)\) with initial \((b,w)=(0,0)\), the gradients are \(g_b=-4\) and \(g_w=-16/3\). At 0.20, \(b_1=0.8\) and \(w_1=16/15\approx1.0666667\). Predictions are \((0.8,1.8666667,2.9333333)\); residuals \((-1.2,-2.1333333,-3.0666667)\). The squared residuals sum to \(1.44+4.5511111+9.4044444=15.3955556\), so \(J_1=15.3955556/6=\boxed{2.5659259}\). This step improves the Training objective more quickly than rate 0.10 here, but one update does not determine the best rate for new data.

18. Why does three-step Training Loss in the new manufacturing example not prove deployment accuracy?

The three updates used only three synthetic Training observations and still did not reach the exact OLS minimum. On the independent illustrative run at 300 devices with observed 8.5 minutes, the three-step prediction is about 4.6747 minutes (error −3.8253) versus the Training-fitted OLS prediction of 8 minutes (error −0.5). Neither comparison establishes future deployment accuracy from a single held-out record. Select the optimization procedure using Validation; reserve Test for final evaluation.

Term Meaning in this guide
Parameter A model coefficient updated during Training, such as \(b\) or \(w\).
Initialization Choosing starting values before optimization, here \((0,0)\).
Residual Signed difference \(\widehat y-y\) between prediction and observed value.
Objective / Loss A scalar function the optimizer seeks to minimize.
Derivative Local change rate of a one-variable function.
Partial derivative Local change rate when one parameter varies while others are fixed.
Gradient Vector of partial derivatives of the objective.
Learning Rate Positive multiplier controlling step size.
Iteration One parameter update.
Epoch Usually one pass through the Training rows.
Batch GD Full-Training gradient for each update.
SGD Gradient update from one sampled Training row.
Mini-Batch GD Gradient update from a subset of Training rows.
Convex objective Objective for which every local minimum is global.
Conditioning How differently curvature or scales affect the numerical optimization.

Early stopping Selecting when to halt Training using a held-out Validation criterion.
