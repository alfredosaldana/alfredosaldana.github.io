1. Why learn with a forest instead of a single tree?

A Decision Tree can explain a prediction as a sequence of questions, but that strength comes with a weakness: a small change in Training may change which question becomes the root. Imagine evaluating an industrial machine for failure during the next seven days. If several predictors offer similar reductions in Gini Impurity, one additional inspection might change the fitted tree substantially. This is instability, not proof that decision trees are useless.

Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) combats instability by fitting multiple trees on differently resampled versions of Training, then combining their predictions. A Random Forest adds a second source of randomness: each split considers only a random subset of available Features. The individual trees can disagree, but the aggregated prediction may be less sensitive to which observations happened to be included in Training.

We will not claim that a forest always outperforms a tree. Our small worked example actually contains two errors made by the forest itself. That is useful evidence: aggregation is a mechanism for managing variability, not a mathematical guarantee of perfect predictions. Later, a larger reproducible experiment will show how to tune and evaluate a forest without looking at Test.

2. Learning objectives, prerequisites, and the information boundary

By the end, you should be able to construct a Bootstrap sample, compute the Gini reduction of each toy tree, combine classification votes and regression predictions, calculate an Out-of-Bag (OOB) score, derive the ensemble variance formula, compare Bagging and Random Forest, and evaluate a complete model using Training, Validation, and Test.

You need only basic proportions, averages, elementary Probability, and the idea of a Decision Tree from Guide 19. We will restate the formulas when they are needed. Here the Target is a binary event: \(Y=1\) means a confirmed machine failure within the next seven days; \(Y=0\) means no confirmed failure in that period. Features must have been available at the prediction time. Including measurements obtained after a failure would create Data Leakage.

Notation. \(n\) is the number of Training records; \(B\) is the number of trees; \(T_b(x)\) is the prediction from tree \(b\) for input \(x\); \(m\) (sometimes max_features) is the number of Features considered at a split. Subscript \(b\) identifies a tree, not a class. All numerical datasets in this guide are synthetic instructional examples, not evidence of industrial reliability.

4. Gini Impurity: measuring the quality of a question

At a node with a proportion \(p\) of failures and \(1-p\) of nonfailures, Gini Impurity is \(G=1-p^2-(1-p)^2=2p(1-p)\). It is zero for a pure node, because one class has probability one, and reaches 0.5 when both classes are equally represented. It is not the probability of making an error; it is a criterion for comparing class mixtures.

To assess a split, calculate Gini in each child and weight it by the number of Training draws arriving there, including repeated Bootstrap draws:

\[G_{\mathrm{after}}=\frac{n_L}{n}G_L+\frac{n_R}{n}G_R,\qquad \Delta G=G_{\mathrm{before}}-G_{\mathrm{after}}.\]

For example, a node with three failures and one nonfailure has \(p=3/4\), so \(G=1-(3/4)^2-(1/4)^2=1-9/16-1/16=6/16=0.375\). In a weighted split, that number must be multiplied by the child's share of all draws. A larger \(\Delta G\) describes a better local split according to Gini; it does not prove a model will generalize better.

5. Bootstrap: sample with replacement, not a normal partition

A Bootstrap draw picks one of the \(n\) Training records uniformly, replaces it, and picks again. With \(n=8\) we draw eight times for each tree. The same ID can appear twice or more, and some IDs can disappear. This differs from partitioning records into eight disjoint groups: resampling changes the empirical composition of a tree's Training data.

We will use these three fixed sequences of draws (provided so the exercise is fully reproducible):

6. Why are some observations Out-of-Bag?

Pick one particular Training record, say ID 5. The probability that a single draw does not select that ID is \(7/8\). The eight draws are independent, so the probability that this record never appears in one tree's Bootstrap sample is

\[P(\mathrm{OOB})=\left(1-\frac{1}{8}\right)^8=\left(\frac78\right)^8\approx0.3436.\]

For a general sample of size \(n\), the probability is \((1-1/n)^n\), which approaches \(e^{-1}\approx0.3679\) as \(n\) grows. Therefore roughly 36.8% of the unique Training observations are absent from a typical Bootstrap sample in a large dataset. This is an expectation, not a guarantee: our three particular trees have 2, 3, and 1 OOB records.

The expected OOB count for \(n=8\) is \(8(7/8)^8\approx2.7489\). Notice why this calculation does not say "every observation is OOB in exactly 2.75 trees": it predicts an average count per tree over random repetitions. A Training record left out of one tree may still be used to train other trees.

How many OOB votes should we expect per Training record?

A record is excluded from one tree with probability \(q=(7/8)^8\approx0.343609\). If three trees draw their Bootstrap samples independently, the probability that a particular record is absent from at least one of them is the complement of being included in all three:

\[P(\text{at least one OOB tree})=1-(1-q)^3=1-(1-0.343609)^3\approx0.71719.\]

The expected number of eligible OOB trees per record is \(3q\approx1.03083\). Our fixed three-tree example actually covers six of eight records, or \(6/8=0.75\); IDs 2 and 4 have none. Expected coverage is not observed accuracy, and one OOB vote is a very noisy basis for checking an individual observation.

7. Tree 1: compute its root, children, and selected rule

Tree 1 uses the draw sequence \([1,1,2,3,4,6,7,7]\) and is offered only HighTemp at its root (\(m=1\)). Its labels are \([0,0,0,0,1,1,1,1]\), so the root has four failures and four nonfailures:

\[G_{\mathrm{root},1}=1-(4/8)^2-(4/8)^2=0.5.\]

The HighTemp = 0 child receives IDs \([1,1,2,3,4]\): four zeros and one one. Its impurity is \(1-(4/5)^2-(1/5)^2=8/25=0.32\). The HighTemp = 1 child receives \([6,7,7]\): three ones and no zeros, giving Gini zero. Therefore:

\[G_{\mathrm{after},1}=(5/8)(0.32)+(3/8)(0)=0.20.\]
\[\Delta G_1=0.50-0.20=0.30.\]

The resulting stump predicts 0 for low temperature and 1 for high temperature. These are majority-class rules learned from the resampled rows. Record 4 is misclassified despite low temperature, and record 5 would be misclassified despite high temperature. A split can be optimal for a node and still be wrong on individual cases.

8. Tree 2: a different Bootstrap sample and Feature

Tree 2 draws \([2,3,3,4,5,5,8,8]\) and is offered only HighVibration. Its root contains five zeros and three ones:

\[G_{\mathrm{root},2}=1-(5/8)^2-(3/8)^2=30/64=0.46875.\]

The HighVibration = 0 child is \([2,5,5]\), all zero, hence \(G_0=0\). The HighVibration = 1 child is \([3,3,4,8,8]\), containing two zeros and three ones, hence \(G_1=1-(2/5)^2-(3/5)^2=12/25=0.48\). Weight the two leaves:

\[G_{\mathrm{after},2}=(3/8)(0)+(5/8)(0.48)=0.30.\]
\[\Delta G_2=0.46875-0.30=0.16875.\]

This tree predicts 0 when vibration is normal and 1 when vibration is high. It disagrees with Tree 1 on records 3, 4, 5, and 6. That disagreement is the diversity aggregation needs; diversity alone, however, does not ensure its members are accurate.

9. Tree 3: a different split quality and imperfect leaves

Tree 3 draws \([1,2,4,5,6,6,7,8]\) and receives HighTemp as its candidate Feature. Its root contains three zeros and five ones:

\[G_{\mathrm{root},3}=1-(3/8)^2-(5/8)^2=30/64=0.46875.\]

Low temperature contains \([1,2,4]\) with two zeros, one one: \(G_L=1-(2/3)^2-(1/3)^2=4/9\). High temperature contains \([5,6,6,7,8]\) with one zero and four ones: \(G_R=1-(1/5)^2-(4/5)^2=8/25\). Consequently:

\[G_{\mathrm{after},3}=(3/8)(4/9)+(5/8)(8/25)=1/6+1/5=11/30\approx0.366667.\]
\[\Delta G_3=0.46875-0.366667\approx0.102083.\]

Although Tree 3 selects the same Feature as Tree 1, it has different leaf proportions because it saw different Bootstrap data. This matters for estimated probabilities, even where majority-class predictions coincide. Its majority predictions are still 0 for low temperature and 1 for high temperature.

11. Votes and estimated probability are not the same number

The fraction of hard votes for failure is a useful descriptive score: for ID 5, two of three trees vote 1, so it is \(2/3\approx0.6667\). But standard RandomForestClassifier.predict_proba averages the class proportions stored in the leaves, not necessarily one-hot hard votes. In our hand model, Tree 1's high-temperature leaf has 3 failures out of 3 (probability 1), Tree 2's normal-vibration leaf has 0 out of 3 (probability 0), and Tree 3's high-temperature leaf has 4 failures out of 5 (probability 0.8).

\[\widehat p(Y=1\mid x_5)=\frac{1+0+0.8}{3}=0.60.\]

Thus 60% is the mean leaf probability, while 66.67% is the hard-vote fraction; both yield class 1 at a threshold of 0.5. Neither number is automatically a calibrated probability for future machines. Leaf fractions can be unstable, and Bootstrap resampling means duplicates affect the denominator. We must evaluate probabilistic quality separately (for example, with Brier Score and Calibration Curves).

13. Bagging versus Random Forest: locate the extra randomness

Both methods train many trees on Bootstrap samples and aggregate predictions. The critical distinction is the candidate Features offered at each split. Standard Bagging ordinarily offers all \(p\) Features when fitting each tree. Random Forest independently samples a subset of size \(m\le p\) at every split before selecting the locally best rule. When strong Features repeatedly dominate the root, random Feature selection prevents every tree from relying on exactly the same one.

In our miniature demonstration \(p=2\) and \(m=1\), so each tree's sole split considered either Temperature or Vibration. The full algorithm redraws that subset for each internal node, not just once per tree. A setting such as max_features=2 would offer both Features at each node, but could still produce different trees because the Bootstrap data differ.

This distinction matters because Bootstrap reduces dependence on particular rows, whereas Feature subsampling can reduce correlation between trees. Neither operation creates new independent information. If Training itself is biased or leaks future data, the forest inherits that problem.

Do the two three-stump ensembles predict differently?

Yes. With the same Bootstrap samples, the restricted forest's three split rules are (Temperature, Vibration, Temperature); the Bagging comparison's are (Temperature, Vibration, Vibration). Majority voting in the restricted forest follows Temperature and errs on Training IDs 4 and 5. Majority voting for these Bagging stumps follows Vibration and errs on Training IDs 3 and 6. Both achieve 6/8 = 75% Training Accuracy, but their errors differ.

Keep the same OOB membership matrix: records 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 8 are scoreable. The restricted forest gets four of six OOB predictions correct; Bagging gets three of six (its OOB mistakes are IDs 3, 5, and 6). These tiny, partly single-vote OOB scores are demonstrations, not evidence that Random Forest generally beats Bagging. The comparison changes only candidate Feature availability; a standard fitted full forest would resample Features independently at every nonterminal node.

14. Derive why averaging imperfect, correlated trees can help

For a Regression Forest, consider the average \(\overline T=(1/B)\sum_{b=1}^B T_b\). Suppose each tree prediction has variance \(\sigma^2\) and each distinct pair has correlation \(\rho\), so pairwise covariance equals \(\rho\sigma^2\). The variance of a sum is the sum of individual variances plus all pairwise covariances:

\[\operatorname{Var}(\overline T)=\frac{1}{B^2}\left[B\sigma^2+B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2\right].\]

Factor \(B\sigma^2\) and simplify:

\[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\overline T)=\sigma^2\left[\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\right]}.\]

For \(\sigma^2=0.04\), \(B=5\), and \(\rho=0.25\), the resulting variance is \(0.04[0.25+0.75/5]=0.04(0.40)=0.016\); its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.016}\approx0.1265\), versus 0.20 for one tree. If correlation were 0.90, the variance would instead be \(0.04[0.90+0.10/5]=0.0368\). The trees can be numerous but still highly redundant.

As \(B\to\infty\), this idealized variance tends to \(\rho\sigma^2\), not necessarily zero. The formula requires the stated equal-variance/equal-correlation assumptions and applies directly to numeric averages; it is not an exact formula for Classification Accuracy or majority-vote error.

15. Regression Forests: average leaf predictions, not labels

Random Forest is not limited to binary Classification. In Regression, each Decision Tree predicts a numeric quantity—usually a Training mean in its terminal leaf when fitted under Squared Error—and the forest averages those numeric predictions. Suppose three fitted trees predict 5, 8, and 6 hours until an industrial component requires inspection.

\[\widehat y_{\mathrm{forest}}=\frac{5+8+6}{3}=\frac{19}{3}\approx6.333\text{ hours}.\]

If the observed time were 7 hours, the signed residual would be \(7-6.333\approx0.667\) hours and Squared Error about \(0.444\) hours². The arithmetic is simple, but its interpretation is important: the average blends model predictions, not the raw targets of the three nearest observations. Regression Forest does not extrapolate reliably beyond values represented in its Training leaves; averaging observed leaf means generally keeps predictions within the range of Training target values.

16. From a tiny proof to a reproducible development experiment

The hand example made each Bootstrap draw visible, but eight records cannot establish industrial performance. We therefore construct a separate synthetic dataset of 150 independent inspection cycles with three Features: standardized heat, standardized vibration, and a binary seal indicator. A random seed fixes the simulation. The failure probability used to generate these synthetic labels increases with heat and vibration and includes an interaction; the learner does not receive this generating equation.

Because the simulation draws independent cycles, the groups do not represent repeated measurements from the same machine. 17. Select Hyperparameters using Validation, not Test

A Parameter is learned by the model (for example, a split threshold). A Hyperparameter is a configuration chosen through a development procedure (for example, max_depth). We train four candidate forests, each with 200 trees, min_samples_leaf=3, Bootstrap sampling, and a fixed random seed. The candidate differences are the number of Features per split (\(m\)) and maximum tree depth (\(d\)). None means no explicit depth limit, not that trees grow infinitely.

The selection metric is Balanced Accuracy, defined as the average of Recall for the failure class and Specificity for the nonfailure class. It gives both classes equal weight even if their counts differ:

\[\mathrm{Balanced\ Accuracy}=\tfrac12\left[\frac{TP}{TP+FN}+\frac{TN}{TN+FP}\right].\]

19. Out-of-Bag is a diagnostic, not a magic substitute for Test

For each Training record, a full Random Forest can combine the votes or mean leaf probabilities of only the trees whose Bootstrap samples excluded that record. The OOB score is then an internally held-out diagnostic. For the selected model here, oob_score_ gives 0.76667 Training OOB Accuracy. Crucially, that number measures Accuracy, whereas our Validation figure 0.57656 measures Balanced Accuracy. They must not be compared as though they were the same metric.

Even after selecting the same metric, OOB and external Validation can differ because they use different observations and because tuning based repeatedly on OOB can overfit that diagnostic. Furthermore, if several rows belong to the same machine, OOB sampling at the row level can leave one row out while keeping highly correlated rows from that machine in a tree. A group-held-out evaluation is more appropriate when generalizing to new machines.

A sound workflow uses OOB as supplementary evidence, Validation (or Cross-Validation) for model selection, and Test for the final predeployment estimate. None alone guarantees performance under later Distribution Shift.

21. Impurity-based Feature Importance: what is actually counted?

Decision-tree splits are chosen to reduce impurity. A common Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI) score accumulates weighted impurity reductions by Feature across a fitted forest, then normalizes scores. For our three one-split toy trees, each tree uses only one Feature. If we pool the unnormalized root gains, Temperature gets \(0.30+0.102083=0.402083\) and Vibration gets \(0.16875\), for a total of \(0.570833\).

The illustrative pooled-gain shares are \(0.402083/0.570833\approx70.44\%\) for Temperature and \(0.16875/0.570833\approx29.56\%\) for Vibration. These are not exactly scikit-learn's forest feature_importances_: scikit-learn normalizes importance within each tree, then averages tree scores. For these three single-split trees, that convention would instead give \(2/3\) and \(1/3\).

In the separate 150-cycle fitted experiment, the actual scikit-learn impurity importances are approximately 0.374 for heat, 0.518 for vibration, 0.108 for the seal. Importance shows what this fitted model used; it does not demonstrate that changing the Feature causes failures. Correlated Features can share or mask importance, and impurity importance can favor variables that offer many candidate thresholds.

22. Permutation Importance: perturb a Feature and re-evaluate

Permutation Importance asks a different question: how much does the fitted model's score fall if a particular Feature's values are shuffled across Validation rows? Shuffling destroys the association of that Feature with its original labels while leaving the model fixed. We do not refit the forest during the shuffle.

With ten seeded shuffles of each Validation Feature and Balanced Accuracy as the scoring metric, the average score reductions in our synthetic experiment are heat 0.01435, vibration 0.05574, and seal −0.00502. For vibration, this is an average drop of about 5.57 percentage points, not a claim that eliminating vibration would reduce failures by that amount. The negative seal estimate simply means these particular permutations slightly improved the score on average; sampling noise is one possible reason.

Use Validation for this diagnosis if you are still developing the model. Repeatedly using Test to rank Features and refit would turn Test into another Validation set. If Features are strongly correlated, shuffling only one can underestimate their shared predictive contribution; group or conditional permutation approaches may be more informative. Neither permutation nor impurity importance automatically measures causality.

23. Imbalanced classes, probability calibration, and threshold choice

In failure detection, false negatives may be much more expensive than false positives. A forest's default threshold of 0.5 is a decision rule, not a value the trees must learn. A team could compare thresholds using Validation and a stated operational cost matrix, then freeze the threshold before Test. Threshold selection should consider capacity limits (for example, inspection technicians available per day) and the consequences of each error.

If probabilities are required for cost calculations, assess Calibration rather than assuming that an average of leaf class proportions is a reliable probability. A forest can discriminate useful cases while still systematically overestimating or underestimating event frequency. Calibration methods themselves need appropriate held-out data or Cross-Validation and must not be fit to Test.

For class imbalance, report Recall, Precision, Specificity, Balanced Accuracy or PR-AUC as warranted; do not celebrate a high Accuracy obtained by always predicting the majority class. Class weights or resampling are options, but their effects must be evaluated within the Training process. Resampling Validation or Test to improve their class proportions would corrupt the evaluation target.

24. Missing values, categorical variables, and leakage in Pipelines

Trees split on Features rather than using Euclidean distances, so scaling usually matters far less than in k-NN or SVM. This is not permission to ignore preprocessing. Missing-value behavior depends on the algorithm and library version, and categorical encodings must represent meaningful information without inserting future outcomes. For a reproducible general-purpose example, one can impute missing numerical Features and encode categorical Features inside a Pipeline.

If an Imputer learns a median from Training, fit() determines that median from Training only; transform() applies the same frozen median to Validation, Test, and future records. Computing another median using Test would permit evaluation data to influence the model workflow. Similarly, encoding categories using Target rates must be performed within each Training fold to avoid target leakage.

For a machine-failure problem, a subtler leakage source is timing: an inspection code entered after a failure cannot be a valid predictor for a seven-day-ahead warning. The crucial question is not merely whether the column exists in the database, but whether its value would have been available at the moment the prediction was made.

25. Generalization, computational cost, and limits of forests

Forests are powerful on tabular data because they capture nonlinear effects and interactions without hand-specifying a polynomial equation. They can also handle heterogeneous Features and reduce some instability of individual trees. But a forest can still overfit noisy Training labels, inherit selection bias, fail under Distribution Shift, and struggle to extrapolate numeric Targets beyond Training experience.

Training \(B\) trees costs more computation and memory than training one; prediction also evaluates many trees. Bagging trees independently makes parallel training attractive. Increasing n_estimators usually stabilizes ensemble estimates, but cannot fix missing information or systematic bias. max_depth, min_samples_leaf, max_features, and sample size influence the bias–variance trade-off in different ways; none should be tuned on Test.

Do not confuse Random Forest with Boosting: a Random Forest fits randomized trees largely independently and aggregates them, whereas Boosting adds trees sequentially to improve an evolving objective. Nor should the word "random" be mistaken for meaningless predictions: randomization is applied to Training samples and split candidates, while the chosen split inside the allowed subset is still based on a defined criterion.

26. Reproduce the complete experiment in Python

The following code reproduces the separate 150-cycle experiment, not the hand-built three-stump forest. It fixes both simulation and forest seeds, uses only Training to fit each candidate, uses Validation to select Hyperparameters, prints OOB as a secondary diagnostic, and evaluates Test once. The three Features are already numerical, so no data-learned preprocessing is required here.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import balanced_accuracy_score, accuracy_score, confusion_matrix
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 150
X = np.column_stack([
    rng.normal(size=n),              # heat
    rng.normal(size=n),              # vibration
    rng.binomial(1, 0.25, size=n)   # seal indicator
])
z = -0.9 + 1.1 * X[:, 0] + 1.2 * X[:, 1] + 0.7 * X[:, 2]
z += 0.7 * X[:, 0] * X[:, 1]
p_failure = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
y = rng.binomial(1, p_failure)

X_train, y_train = X[:90], y[:90]
X_val, y_val = X[90:120], y[90:120]
X_test, y_test = X[120:], y[120:]

choices = [(2, 3), (2, None), (1, 3), (1, None)]
results = []
for m, depth in choices:
    forest = RandomForestClassifier(
        n_estimators=200, max_features=m,
        max_depth=depth, min_samples_leaf=3,
        bootstrap=True, oob_score=True,
        random_state=23, n_jobs=1
    )
    forest.fit(X_train, y_train)
    score = balanced_accuracy_score(y_val, forest.predict(X_val))
    results.append((score, m, depth, forest))
    print(m, depth, round(score, 5), round(forest.oob_score_, 5))

best_score, best_m, best_depth, chosen = max(
    results, key=lambda item: item[0]
)
print('Selected:', best_m, best_depth)
for name, yy, xx in [('Validation', y_val, X_val),
                     ('Test', y_test, X_test)]:
    predicted = chosen.predict(xx)
    print(name, confusion_matrix(yy, predicted).tolist())
    print('Accuracy:', round(accuracy_score(yy, predicted), 5))
    print('Balanced:', round(balanced_accuracy_score(yy, predicted), 5))

print('Impurity importance:', chosen.feature_importances_.round(5))
permutation = permutation_importance(
    chosen, X_val, y_val, n_repeats=10,
    random_state=24, scoring='balanced_accuracy'
)
print('Permutation:', permutation.importances_mean.round(5))

How to read the code, block by block. First, default_rng(42) creates a reproducible stream of random draws. The three columns of X contain 150 simulated heat measurements, vibration measurements, and seal indicators. The numerical score z combines those Features with an interaction; 1/(1+exp(-z)) converts it into a probability between zero and one. rng.binomial(1, p_failure) then draws a binary outcome for each cycle. A probability of 0.7 does not mean the outcome must be 1: the random Bernoulli draw may still be zero.

Second, array slices create three disjoint groups. For each Hyperparameter combination, forest.fit(X_train, y_train) learns 200 trees from Training only. The same fitted trees predict Validation; balanced_accuracy_score produces a number we can compare. We save the score, settings, and fitted object together so we can identify exactly which model was selected. max(..., key=lambda item: item[0]) chooses the largest Validation score; it never reads Test.

Third, the loop prints two Confusion Matrices for the already selected forest. Validation was used earlier for selection, while Test is shown only afterward for final evaluation. Finally, feature_importances_ summarizes fitted impurity reductions, whereas permutation_importance holds the model fixed and shuffles the columns of Validation ten times. They answer different questions and should not be interpreted as causal effects.

Try altering max_features or min_samples_leaf, but keep Test outside the tuning loop. Then generate a fresh independent synthetic Test sample to see how unstable a single 30-row evaluation can be. For an industrial dataset, replace the independent-cycle assumption with realistic grouping by machine and a time-aware evaluation when the deployment setting calls for it.

27. Checkpoints: can you explain each transition?

Checkpoint A — Bootstrap. A record occurs twice in Tree 1's sample. Does that mean it counts once or twice when computing a node's Gini? Twice: the resampled empirical distribution contains both draws. The number of unique records matters for OOB membership, not for ignoring duplicates in impurity calculations.

Checkpoint B — Averaging. Tree votes for failure are \((1,0,1)\). Does this guarantee a calibrated 66.67% chance of failure? No: vote fractions are not calibrated probabilities. A library may average nonbinary leaf proportions, producing a different numeric score.

Checkpoint C — OOB. May record 4 be counted as an OOB error in our three-tree example? No: every tree trained on record 4, so there is no eligible OOB prediction. Scoring it anyway would change the denominator incorrectly.

Checkpoint D — Evaluation. Can we choose a new threshold after seeing the Test cost? No: that uses Test for model selection. Record the result and perform any revised policy development with new held-out data in a subsequent experiment.

28. Common mistakes: diagnosis, correction, and consequence

1. Treating Bootstrap as sampling without replacement

A repeated record has repeated weight in its tree. Removing duplicates changes the intended Training distribution and its Gini counts.

2. Computing OOB from every tree

Use only trees whose samples excluded that record. Otherwise the apparent held-out score includes in-sample predictions.

3. Dividing OOB correct predictions by all Training rows

Rows without any OOB votes cannot be scored. Our toy denominator is six, not eight.

4. Assuming more trees guarantee perfect Accuracy

More trees stabilize aggregation under appropriate assumptions; they cannot correct shared bias, poor Features or faulty Labels.

5. Using the same random Feature subset only once per full tree

The standard Random Forest samples candidate Features at every split; our Stumps have only one split by construction.

6. Equating the fraction of hard votes with predict_proba

Libraries may average class proportions in leaves. Our example gives 2/3 hard votes but 0.60 mean leaf probability.

7. Reading OOB Accuracy as Validation Balanced Accuracy

They are different metrics on different predictions; our 0.76667 OOB Accuracy and 0.57656 Validation Balanced Accuracy are not directly comparable.

8. Selecting Hyperparameters on Test

Every change based on Test contaminates the final evaluation. Tune only on Validation or proper Cross-Validation.

9. Using row-level OOB as proof of new-machine generalization

Repeated records from the same machine can leak group-specific information between in-bag and OOB rows.

10. Calling Feature Importance causal

MDI and Permutation Importance quantify use or dependence of a fitted model, not what would happen if an engineer intervened.

11. Assuming tree probabilities are calibrated

Check Brier Score and calibration on data not used to fit the probabilities; class scores need not be reliable frequencies.

12. Ignoring false-negative costs

A majority-class Baseline can match Accuracy while detecting no failures. Choose metrics and thresholds from the operational objective.

13. Scaling all numeric columns without explanation

Trees often need no scaling, but time validity, missingness, encoding, and leakage precautions remain important.

14. Mixing two datasets silently

The hand-built three-stump forest and the 150-cycle scikit-learn forest are separate experiments; do not reuse one model’s numbers as the other’s.

15. Forgetting dependence between trees in the variance formula

The reduction for an average depends on both tree count and correlation. The equal-correlation derivation is not a guarantee for Classification Accuracy.

16. Reporting synthetic cost differences as realized savings

Our dollar amounts are hypothetical. Real savings require estimating the effect and cost of interventions, not just predictive classification.

29. Exercises with fully worked answers

1. What does "with replacement" mean for Tree 1?

Its eight draws include record 1 twice and record 7 twice. Their duplicate occurrences count twice in the Gini frequencies: the labels are 0,0,0,0,1,1,1,1. The number of distinct records is six.

2. Recompute Tree 1’s high-temperature leaf Gini.

Records 6,7,7 all have label 1, so p=3/3=1 and G=1−1²−0²=0. The split’s weighted impurity is (5/8)(0.32)+(3/8)(0)=0.20.

3. Verify the Gini improvement for Tree 2.

The root Gini is 30/64=0.46875. The weighted children yield (3/8)(0)+(5/8)(12/25)=0.30. Hence the gain is 0.16875.

4. Why can Tree 1 and Tree 3 disagree on probabilities yet agree on classes?

Their low-temperature leaves contain 1/5 and 1/3 failures, respectively: different estimated probabilities, both below 0.5. Their majority class is therefore 0 in both cases.

5. Count the three hard votes for record 4.

Its Features are (0,1); Tree 1 votes 0, Tree 2 votes 1, Tree 3 votes 0. The majority vote is 0, although the observed outcome is 1: a False Negative.

6. Calculate the mean leaf probability for record 5.

The three leaf proportions are 1, 0, and 4/5. Their mean is (1+0+0.8)/3=0.60. Two of three hard votes are 1, producing a distinct 66.67% vote fraction.

7. Identify the eligible OOB trees for record 6.

Tree 1 includes record 6, Tree 2 excludes it, and Tree 3 includes it twice. Only Tree 2 predicts OOB: vibration is 0 so it predicts 0, while Y=1. This is one OOB error.

8. Derive the full toy OOB Accuracy.

IDs 1,3,5,6,7,8 have OOB predictions; IDs 2 and 4 do not. Correct OOB IDs are 1,3,7,8, so the score is 4/6≈0.6667. Never divide by eight.

9. Compute the probability that a record is absent from one eight-draw Bootstrap.

The probability of avoiding a particular record in one draw is 7/8. With eight independent draws: (7/8)^8≈0.343609. Multiply by eight for the expected OOB count ≈2.74887.

10. Compute the averaged Regression prediction from three trees.

If the trees output 5,8,6 hours, their average is (5+8+6)/3=19/3≈6.333 hours. With observed Y=7, signed residual is approximately 0.667 hours.

11. Use the variance formula with B=5, σ²=0.04 and ρ=0.25.

Var(mean)=0.04[0.25+0.75/5]=0.016. The corresponding SD is sqrt(0.016)≈0.1265; this calculation concerns numerical averages, not classification vote Accuracy.

12. What if the correlation of those trees were 0.90?

Var(mean)=0.04[0.90+0.10/5]=0.0368, close to 0.04 for one tree. More trees offer little reduction when predictions are very strongly correlated.

13. Rebuild Balanced Accuracy from the Validation Confusion Matrix.

Recall=TP/(TP+FN)=4/11≈0.36364; specificity=TN/(TN+FP)=15/19≈0.78947. Their mean is ≈0.57656, despite ordinary Accuracy of 19/30≈0.63333.

14. Why is the always-negative Baseline inadequate despite equal Accuracy?

It gets all 19 nonfailures right but misses all 11 failures. Thus Accuracy is 19/30, Recall is 0/11=0, and Balanced Accuracy=(0+1)/2=0.5. The forest finds four failures.

15. Reproduce the Test Balanced Accuracy.

TP=6, FN=8, TN=15, FP=1. Recall=6/14; specificity=15/16; Balanced Accuracy=(6/14+15/16)/2≈0.68304. The 30-row Test is independent of the selection step.

16. Calculate the illustrative Validation costs.

Forest: 7 missed failures×$800 + 4 false alerts×$120 = $6,080. Always-negative Baseline: 11 missed failures×$800 = $8,800. These are hypothetical losses, not causal savings.

17. Explain why two importance formulas differ in the three-stump example.

Pooling unnormalized gains gives Temperature (0.30+0.102083)/0.570833≈0.7044. Normalizing each one-split tree first and averaging yields 2/3 because two of three trees use Temperature. They answer related but not identical accounting questions.

18. A Feature has negative mean Permutation Importance. What can you conclude?

Only that shuffling it improved the chosen Validation score on average in that experiment. This can be noise or model behavior; it does not prove the Feature is causally protective or should automatically be deleted.

19. Controlled split comparison: why does Tree 3 change when both Features become available?

Tree 3 has five failures and three nonfailures in its eight Bootstrap draws, giving root Gini \(15/32=0.46875\). Its Temperature children produce weighted Gini \(11/30=0.366667\), so Gain is \(49/480\approx0.102083\). Vibration gives five draws with class counts 3:2 and three pure failures: weighted Gini is \((5/8)(12/25)=0.30\); Gain is \(15/32-0.30=27/160=0.16875\). With both Features, Bagging chooses Vibration; with only Temperature available, the restricted Random Forest stump cannot make that choice. The Bootstrap sample did not change.

20. Transfer: what if the three trees are replaced by ten independent Bootstraps?

For a given record among eight Training records, the chance of being absent from one eight-draw sample is still \(q=(7/8)^8\approx0.343609\). If ten Bootstrap samples are independently generated, expected eligible OOB votes equal \(10q\approx3.43609\) and the chance of at least one eligible vote is \(1-(1-q)^{10}\approx0.98515\). The additional trees increase expected OOB coverage, but do not establish a corresponding increase in predictive Accuracy. In real repeated-machine data, row-level Bootstrap may remain invalid for generalizing to entirely unseen machines.

30. Glossary, synthesis, and future laboratory

Bootstrap sample
Sample of n draws from n Training records, independently with replacement.
Bagging
Bootstrap Aggregating: train multiple models on resamples, then aggregate their predictions.
Random Forest
Tree ensemble using Bootstrap samples and random Feature subsets at each split.
Decision Stump
Decision Tree with only one split and at most two terminal leaves.
Gini Impurity
Class mixture criterion equal to one minus the sum of squared class proportions.
Out-of-Bag (OOB)
Training observation excluded from a particular tree’s Bootstrap sample.
Majority Vote
Aggregate class by choosing the class predicted by most trees.
Leaf Probability
Fraction of a class among fitted Training draws in a terminal node.
`max_features`
Maximum count of candidate Features offered for one split.
`max_depth`
Upper bound on the number of levels of splits within an individual tree.
`min_samples_leaf`
Minimum observations required in each terminal leaf.
`n_estimators`
Number of trees in the fitted ensemble.
Balanced Accuracy
Average of positive-class Recall and negative-class Specificity.
OOB Score
Diagnostic calculated from per-record predictions of trees that excluded the record.
MDI Importance
Impurity reduction attributed to Features within fitted trees.
Permutation Importance
Decrease in chosen validation metric after shuffling one Feature’s values.
Calibration
Agreement between estimated probabilities and observed event frequencies.
Data Leakage
Invalid information flow from future or evaluation data into model development.
Tree Correlation
Statistical dependence among tree predictions; limits variance reduction from averaging.
Generalization
Performance on observations drawn from the intended unseen population.

A Random Forest is built from Bootstrap samples, random Feature subsets, individually fitted trees, and an aggregation rule. You can now verify that process on eight records, calculate a genuinely OOB diagnostic, derive how correlation limits variance reduction, and evaluate a larger model without consulting Test prematurely. The forest's strength comes from combining sufficiently useful, diverse trees—not from a guarantee that every tree, every dataset, or every deployment will improve.

The guide teaches all prerequisite ideas; the lab can then investigate their behavior on richer data.
