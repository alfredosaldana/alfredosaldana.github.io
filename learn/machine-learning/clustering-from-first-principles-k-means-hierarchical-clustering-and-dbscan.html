1. Why clusters if we have no labels?

A retailer knows how often each customer visits and the average size of a purchase, but has no trusted “customer segment” label. Unlike Classification, Clustering does not learn a supplied Target such as churn or fraud. It searches for structure in the Features themselves. That difference changes the questions we can ask and the evidence needed to trust an answer.

A cluster is an operational grouping defined by an algorithm and its representation of similarity; it is not necessarily a natural, immutable type of person. We will construct groups from nine synthetic customers, interpret their business meaning, and challenge those interpretations with three different algorithms. Notice that an impressive geometric separation does not prove what caused a customer to behave that way.

Learning promise: you will not be asked merely to recognize the names k-Means, Hierarchical Clustering and DBSCAN. You will calculate what each method does, explain where methods disagree, and defend when a cluster should—or should not—be used.

2. What you will be able to do—and what we assume

By the end, you should be able to: calculate Euclidean distances and centroid updates; derive the k-Means objective; perform its assignments and SSE audit; explain how linkage builds a dendrogram; identify DBSCAN core, border and noise points; reconstruct one Silhouette Score; and decide how to evaluate unsupervised groupings without inventing ground truth.

You only need arithmetic, the meaning of a mean, square roots and the idea of a Feature introduced earlier in this series. A vector such as (2,3) is simply a pair of measured quantities. We will define each new symbol before using it. The worked dataset stays the same in all principal demonstrations; smaller auxiliary examples are marked explicitly.

Route: data → distances → k-Means → choosing k → hierarchical methods → DBSCAN → evaluation and limits → reproducible Python → practice.

4. Distance: turn “similar” into a reproducible number

For two customers a=(x_a,y_a) and b=(x_b,y_b), Euclidean Distance is the straight-line separation between two points in the Feature Space. Squaring each coordinate difference prevents positive and negative differences from canceling; summing combines both dimensions; the square root brings the result back to coordinate units.

\[d(a,b)=\sqrt{(x_a-x_b)^2+(y_a-y_b)^2}\]

For C1=(1,1) and C2=(1,3), the distance is \(\sqrt{(1-1)^2+(1-3)^2}=\sqrt{0+4}=2\). For C1 and C4=(3,3), \(\sqrt{(1-3)^2+(1-3)^2}=\sqrt8\approx2.828\). C2 is nearer to C1 than C4 under this definition.

The familiar name Manhattan Distance instead adds absolute coordinate differences: \(d_1(a,b)=|x_a-x_b|+|y_a-y_b|\). For C1–C4 it equals 4, not 2.828. An algorithm cannot decide what “similarity” means for your business; that choice is part of the research design.

5. Units, scaling and a dangerous invisible choice

Euclidean Distance adds squared differences from both Features. Our visits/month and basket/10 dollars are conceptually different units; their numerical sizes affect the result. Merely expressing basket in dollars makes its coordinate ten times larger, so differences in purchasing amount dominate the squared sum. This can change nearest points and clusters even though customer behavior has not changed.

A common remedy is Standardization. Training mean and population-form standard deviation for visits are 6.3333 and 4.1633; for basket/10 they are 5.1111 and 3.6038. The fitted transformation is:

\[z_x=\frac{x-6.3333}{4.1633},\qquad z_y=\frac{y-5.1111}{3.6038}\]

For C1, standardized coordinates are approximately (−1.281,−1.141). These quantities count Training standard deviations rather than visits and dollars. They should not be interpreted as quantities of merchandise.

Critical Data Leakage rule: determine all means, standard deviations and imputation values from Training only. New observations are transformed with frozen parameters. For a new population whose distribution shifts, investigate rather than silently refitting using Test.

6. Three families, three definitions of “group”

k-Means describes clusters by centroids and attempts to minimize Within-Cluster Sum of Squared Errors. Hierarchical Clustering builds a sequence of merges (or splits) and represents nested similarity relationships. DBSCAN searches for locally dense connected regions and can mark isolated observations as Noise. None is universally best.

We will compare them on the original two-dimensional values to make the arithmetic transparent. Later we will explain when a deployment should scale Features, remove unsupported values or use a more appropriate domain distance. Remember: Clustering does not magically infer customer psychology, causality or future purchasing behavior.

An unsupervised result is not automatically “correct” because a graphic looks tidy. Validation may include cohesion, stability, domain expertise and downstream usefulness; without ground truth, Accuracy is not directly defined.

7. k-Means: what exactly is optimized?

A Centroid is the coordinate-wise mean of the points assigned to one cluster; unlike a Medoid, it need not coincide with any observed customer. With k=2, the algorithm seeks two centroids and assignments that minimize the sum of squared Euclidean distances to the assigned centroids. The usual objective is called Inertia or Within-Cluster SSE.

\[J=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|x_i-\mu_{c_i}\right\|_2^2\]

Here n=9; \(x_i=(x_{i1},x_{i2})\) is customer i; \(c_i\in\{1,2\}\) names its assigned cluster; \(\mu_{c_i}\) is that cluster’s centroid; and \(\|\cdot\|_2^2\) means “square each coordinate difference and add them.” J has mixed squared coordinate units unless Features were scaled to a common dimensionless system.

Why the mean? For one coordinate, minimizing \(\sum_i(x_i-m)^2\) gives derivative \(-2\sum_i(x_i-m)=0\), so \(m=\sum_i x_i/n\). Applying this independently to the two coordinates gives the centroid. This explains mathematically why the Update Step uses an average rather than an arbitrary representative.

10. Second assignment, stopping and why this is only a local solution

The next Assignment Step compares every customer with (2,2) and (9.8,7.6). C9 is still nearer to the second:

\[d(C9,\mu_1)=11\]
\[d(C9,\mu_2)=\sqrt{3.2^2+(-5.6)^2}\]
\[=\sqrt{41.6}\approx6.450\]

C1–C4 remain with centroid 1 and C5–C9 remain with centroid 2. Since no assignment changes, the next update produces the same means and the algorithm stops.

Why does the alternating procedure reduce J? With fixed centroids, choosing the nearest gives the smallest contribution for each customer. With fixed assignments, choosing the mean minimizes the sum of squared distances, as proven above. Therefore neither step can increase J (apart from floating-point or empty-cluster implementation details). But the method only guarantees reaching a stationary local configuration, not discovering the globally smallest SSE across all possible assignments.

Different starts can produce different solutions. Common implementations use k-means++ initialization and several starts (`n_init`) to mitigate the risk. An empty cluster needs an explicit implementation policy, often reseeding, rather than an undefined mean.

11. Choosing k: derive the Elbow numbers instead of guessing

Unlike DBSCAN, k-Means requires the number of clusters in advance. Setting k too small merges different patterns; setting it too large can create tiny clusters with little operational meaning. 12. Silhouette Score: full arithmetic for one customer

Silhouette compares cohesion inside an assigned cluster with separation from the closest other cluster. For customer i, a(i) is the mean Euclidean distance to other customers in its own cluster; b(i) is the smallest mean distance to another cluster. The score is between −1 and 1 whenever defined: values near 1 indicate a good local match, near 0 ambiguity, and below 0 possible misassignment.

\[s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\{a(i),b(i)\}}\]

For C1=(1,1) in the k=2 partition, its three peers C2,C3,C4 are at distances 2,2 and \(\sqrt8=2.8284\). Thus:

\[a(C1)=\frac{2+2+\sqrt8}{3}=2.2761\]

Its five distances to C5–C9 are \(\sqrt{98},\sqrt{130},\sqrt{130},\sqrt{162},\sqrt{145}\). Their mean is:

\[b(C1)=\frac{\sqrt{98}+2\sqrt{130}+\sqrt{162}+\sqrt{145}}{5}=11.4945\]
\[s(C1)=\frac{11.4945-2.2761}{11.4945}\approx0.8020\]

The mean Silhouette across all nine customers for k=2 is 0.6508. For k=3 it is 0.6602, slightly higher—but scikit-learn assigns a Silhouette of 0 to the isolated singleton. That tiny difference is not decisive business evidence.

13. Silhouette interpretation, singleton traps and metric limits

A large Silhouette is useful for compact, well-separated clusters under the specified distance, but it can favor roughly convex geometry and can undervalue legitimate non-convex density groups. Moreover, changing units changes distances; two Silhouette numbers calculated under different feature preprocessing pipelines are not necessarily comparable as if they measured the same geometry.

When a cluster contains just one customer, a(i) has no “other member” to average. scikit-learn sets that sample’s Silhouette to zero by convention; it does not establish that the isolated customer is well described by a meaningful segment. Scores are also undefined as a global measure when all records are in one group or every record is a separate group.

Use Silhouette to diagnose, not to replace domain reasoning. The best operational decision could be to treat an isolated customer as an outlier even if a k=3 score is narrowly higher than the score for k=2. In a real retailer, cluster stability, segment sizes and ethically appropriate uses must also be examined.

14. Hierarchical Clustering: merge relationships, not centroids

Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering begins with each observation as its own cluster. At each step it merges the closest pair of clusters under a chosen Linkage definition. Unlike k-Means, it does not generally alternate assignments with mean updates; instead it records a hierarchy of nested partitions. A Dendrogram draws these successive merges, with height showing the merge distance.

Start with four existing observations C1=(1,1), C2=(1,3), C3=(3,1), and C5=(8,8). All six Euclidean distances are reproducible:

15. Single, Complete and Average Linkage: compute the next merge

Let A={C1,C2} and B={C3}. Single Linkage uses the minimum cross-cluster distance; Complete Linkage uses the maximum; Average Linkage averages every cross-cluster pair. All three use exactly the same original distances; only the definition of between-cluster proximity changes.

\[d_{\mathrm{single}}(A,B)=\min(2,\sqrt8)=2\]
\[d_{\mathrm{complete}}(A,B)=\max(2,\sqrt8)=2.8284\]
\[d_{\mathrm{average}}(A,B)=\frac{2+\sqrt8}{2}=2.4142\]

For comparison, distance from A to C5 under Single Linkage is the smaller of \(\sqrt{98}=9.899\) and \(\sqrt{74}=8.602\), or 8.602. Thus C3 merges with A before the remote C5 under every method. But the merge height is 2, 2.828 or 2.414 depending on Linkage. That is why a Dendrogram cannot be interpreted without knowing its metric and Linkage rule.

Complete Linkage emphasizes maximum diameter and tends to favor compact groups. Single Linkage can connect extended chains via one nearby pair, sometimes joining geometrically separated regions. Average Linkage is intermediate, not universally superior.

16. Dendrograms: how to read a height and choose a cut

On the full nine-customer dataset, the Dendrogram places each initial customer at the bottom; vertical branches record which groups merge, and at what Linkage distance. To obtain a flat segmentation, draw a horizontal cut: any branches joined below the cut belong to the same cluster. A different cut can produce a different number of groups without rerunning the hierarchy.

Do not mistake a tall branch for a guaranteed statistical discovery. It only means the chosen distance and Linkage require a relatively large merge. A high merge can be created by an outlier, a scaling issue or a true structural gap. For this dataset, examine whether C9 remains isolated under a meaningful cut, and compare that with the forced k-Means assignments.

Memory/computation matters. Straightforward hierarchical algorithms may need a full pairwise distance representation, with approximately n(n−1)/2 distances, which is expensive for millions of customers. Interpretability of a Dendrogram is usually strongest for manageable samples.

17. DBSCAN: clusters as density-connected neighborhoods

DBSCAN stands for Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise. Its two central parameters are eps, the radius defining a neighborhood, and min_samples, the minimum number of observations in that neighborhood for a point to be a Core Point. In scikit-learn, the point itself counts toward that minimum.

A Core Point has enough neighbors including itself; a Border Point is not core but lies within eps of at least one core point; a Noise Point is neither. Starting from a core point, the cluster expands through density-reachable core neighborhoods; border points may be attached without expanding further. The labels are arbitrary identifiers, not rankings of customer quality.

Unlike k-Means, DBSCAN does not ask us to specify k. However, it asks us to define what counts as “near” and “dense”—decisions strongly affected by units, scale, location and varying densities. It can discover non-convex connected shapes, but also merge groups through density bridges.

18. DBSCAN manually: core, border and noise with actual distances

Use our original nine customers with eps=2.1 and min_samples=3. For C1=(1,1), the neighbors within radius are C1 itself (distance 0), C2 (distance 2), C3 (distance 2). C4 is at 2.828, outside the radius. Therefore C1 has exactly 3 observations in its eps-neighborhood and is Core. C2,C3,C4 are Core by analogous counts. So are C5–C8.

For C9=(13,2), its closest other customer is C7=(10,8). We calculate:

\[d(C9,C7)=\sqrt{(13-10)^2+(2-8)^2}\]
\[=\sqrt{45}\approx6.708>2.1\]

Its neighborhood contains only itself: it is Noise. The algorithm returns two clusters of four points and one Noise observation.

19. Sensitivity: eps and min_samples can change the answer

Keep min_samples=3 and vary eps, always on the original nine customers. If eps=1.9, even the nearest other corner is distance 2, so no point has two additional neighbors; all nine are Noise. If eps=2.1 or 2.9, the compact corners become Core and C9 remains Noise. If eps=7.2, the density neighborhoods bridge the previously separate regions (and reach C9), yielding one connected group.

21. Evaluation without ground truth: stability, diagnostics, and honest claims

If there are no authoritative segment labels, ordinary Classification Accuracy cannot tell us whether a cluster assignment is correct. Internal diagnostics such as Inertia and Silhouette describe geometry under chosen assumptions. External indices such as Adjusted Rand Index or Normalized Mutual Information become meaningful only when appropriate reference labels exist; arbitrary business labels should not be invented after viewing clusters and then treated as independent truth.

A practical evaluation plan might repeat clustering under bootstrap or time windows, match resulting groups carefully (numeric labels can permute), examine cluster-size stability, and check whether summaries such as average basket and frequency remain consistent. Compare methods using the same preprocessing when possible, and conduct sensitivity analysis. Assess whether the segmentation changes practical choices, costs or service quality, rather than only maximizing a numeric score.

For DBSCAN, Silhouette calculated only on non-Noise customers omits difficult customers; reporting it alongside a k-Means score on all customers would be misleading without that denominator difference. Report the Noise share, the chosen radius and other context.

Keep data roles explicit: fit feature preprocessing on development/Training only. If you assess future stability on a held-out period, do not use that period to tune the clustering solution and then describe the same result as an untouched external test.

Controlled experiment: unit conversion can change the clusters

Section 5 warns that Scaling matters; we can prove it without introducing new records. Keep precisely the same nine customers, k=2 and Euclidean distance. Only change how the second Feature is expressed: multiply baskets in $10 units by 10 to obtain dollars. This is a controlled experiment on the same Training observations, not an evaluation on future customers.

First, the original partition. As calculated earlier, C1–C4 belong to the cluster centered at (2,2), while C5–C9 are assigned to (9.8,7.6), with SSE=68 in the mixed coordinates (visits/month, tens of dollars). C9 is in the second group despite its small basket.

Next, the same information expressed in dollars. For C9=(13,$20), converting the previous centers to (2,$20) and (9.8,$76) yields these squared distances:

\[d_A^2=(13-2)^2+(20-20)^2=121\]\[d_B^2=(13-9.8)^2+(20-76)^2=10.24+3136=3146.24\]

C9 is now closer to A. Re-fitting k-Means yields A={C1,C2,C3,C4,C9} and B={C5,C6,C7,C8}. Their fitted centers are A=(4.2 visits,$20) and B=(9 visits,$90). Here is the complete coordinate-by-coordinate SSE calculation:

\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{SSE}_{A,x}&=(1-4.2)^2+(1-4.2)^2\\&\quad +(3-4.2)^2+(3-4.2)^2+(13-4.2)^2\\&=10.24+10.24+1.44+1.44+77.44=100.8,\\\mathrm{SSE}_{A,\$}&=(10-20)^2+(30-20)^2\\&\quad +(10-20)^2+(30-20)^2+(20-20)^2\\&=100+100+100+100+0=400.\end{aligned}\]\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{SSE}_{B,x}&=(8-9)^2+(8-9)^2+(10-9)^2+(10-9)^2=4,\\\mathrm{SSE}_{B,\$}&=(80-90)^2+(100-90)^2\\&\quad +(80-90)^2+(100-90)^2=400,\\\mathrm{SSE}_{\mathrm{total}}&=100.8+400+4+400=\boxed{904.8}.\end{aligned}\]

Do not compare this SSE with 68 as though both were measured in identical units: the squared-distance objectives use different geometries. For this tiny dataset we can independently enumerate the 255 nonempty two-way partitions (keeping C1 in A). Their respective global minima are exactly 68 and 904.8. This establishes a fact about these nine points, not a global-optimum guarantee for k-Means on general datasets.

Measure stability without ground truth: Adjusted Rand Index

We can quantify agreement between two partitions without claiming either provides true customer labels. Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) compares which pairs of individuals share a cluster and corrects for expected chance agreement. It equals 1 for identical partitions (even if labels are renamed), is about 0 for chance-like agreement under its null model, and may be negative.

The two-by-two overlap table has entries: original A ∩ dollar A = 4; original A ∩ dollar B = 0; original B ∩ dollar A = 1; original B ∩ dollar B = 4. Row totals are (4,5), column totals (5,4), and n=9. Define \(\binom m2=m(m-1)/2\), the number of unordered pairs from m observations. Consequently:

\[\begin{aligned}T&=\tbinom42+\tbinom02+\tbinom12+\tbinom42=6+0+0+6=12,\\R&=\tbinom42+\tbinom52=6+10=16,\\C&=\tbinom52+\tbinom42=10+6=16,\\N&=\tbinom92=36,\\E&=RC/N=16\cdot16/36=64/9.\end{aligned}\]\[\mathrm{ARI}=\frac{T-E}{(R+C)/2-E}=\frac{12-64/9}{16-64/9}=\frac{44}{80}=\boxed{0.55}\]

This is measurable sensitivity to representation: the individual records are unchanged, yet the two solutions have ARI=0.55 instead of 1. This is not evidence that either partition is “true,” nor is it a population-level confidence interval. A real analysis should replicate the audit across appropriate resamples or time periods and report variability.

DBSCAN is also scale-sensitive. With eps=2.1 and min_samples=3 in the original coordinates, there are two clusters of four customers and C9 is Noise. Keeping the numerical eps=2.1 after changing basket units to dollars, C1-to-C2 distance rises from 2 to 20 while C1-to-C3 remains 2. Its neighborhood shrinks from {C1,C2,C3} to {C1,C3}, from three points to two: C1 ceases to be Core. Repeating this count for every observation leaves all nine as Noise. Business behavior did not change; the radius must be defined and calibrated in the new feature space using development data, not retrospectively using Test.

22. Practical limits: missingness, categorical data, drift and ethics

Euclidean distance requires numeric vectors. Missing purchase history should not silently become zero: a missing measurement and a real zero purchase mean different things. Imputation parameters should be fitted on development data; if “missing” itself is informative, an indicator may be useful. Combining categorical and continuous Features requires an appropriate encoding and distance, not an arbitrary assignment of numbers to categories.

Highly correlated Features can double-count a behavioral dimension. Strongly skewed spending can dominate distances even after naive transforms. DBSCAN often struggles when parts of the population have sharply different densities; k-Means struggles with elongated or non-convex regions; hierarchical methods may be resource intensive. The clustering task can also become unstable if customer habits change seasonally or after an intervention.

These labels describe observed purchasing patterns in a particular dataset. They do not establish a psychological identity, creditworthiness, medical status or causal explanation. Ensure segmentation use respects privacy, avoids unjustified discrimination and can be challenged with updated evidence.

The subsequent library comparisons expose the role of multiple initializations, Hierarchical Linkage, DBSCAN neighborhood rules and fitted Scaling.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN, AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, silhouette_samples
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage

customers = np.array([
    [1, 1], [1, 3], [3, 1], [3, 3],
    [8, 8], [8, 10], [10, 8], [10, 10], [13, 2]
], dtype=float)

# Manually chosen initial centers: reproduce the worked solution
centers = np.array([[1., 1.], [8., 8.]])
for iteration in range(1, 6):
    sqdist = ((customers[:, None, :] - centers[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)
    labels = sqdist.argmin(axis=1)
    updated = np.array([customers[labels == j].mean(axis=0) for j in range(2)])
    converged = np.allclose(centers, updated)
    centers = updated
    sse = ((customers - centers[labels]) ** 2).sum()
    print('Iteration', iteration, 'centers', centers.tolist(), 'SSE', round(sse, 6))
    if converged:
        break

# Multiple initializations help avoid poor local configurations
for k in range(1, 6):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=30, random_state=2).fit(customers)
    s = silhouette_score(customers, km.labels_) if k > 1 else None
    print('k', k, 'inertia', round(km.inertia_, 6), 'silhouette', s)

# Verify the single C1 silhouette calculation
fixed_labels = np.array([0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1])
print('C1 silhouette', silhouette_samples(customers, fixed_labels)[0])

# Hierarchical relationships; numeric cluster labels may differ
hier = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=3, linkage='complete').fit(customers)
print('Hierarchical labels', hier.labels_)
print('First four single-linkage merges', linkage(customers, method='single')[:4])

# DBSCAN on the original nine customers
for eps in (1.9, 2.1, 2.9, 7.2):
    db = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=3).fit(customers)
    print('DBSCAN eps', eps, 'labels', db.labels_.tolist())

# Separate ten-customer example: C10 is a border observation
extended = np.vstack([customers, [5., 3.]])
model = DBSCAN(eps=2.1, min_samples=3).fit(extended)
print('C10 label', model.labels_[-1], 'core?', 9 in model.core_sample_indices_)

# Standardization learns parameters from Training only
scaler = StandardScaler().fit(customers)
print('Training mean', scaler.mean_, 'Training scale', scaler.scale_)
print('New observation standardized', scaler.transform([[2., 2.]]))


# The same nine Training records, with baskets expressed in dollars
# Only the coordinate representation changes; no new labels are supplied.
from itertools import product
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

dollar_customers = customers * np.array([1.0, 10.0])
raw_k2 = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=30, random_state=2).fit(customers)
dollar_k2 = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=30, random_state=2).fit(dollar_customers)

# ARI ignores arbitrary cluster-ID permutations.
ari = adjusted_rand_score(raw_k2.labels_, dollar_k2.labels_)
print('Scaling experiment: raw SSE', raw_k2.inertia_,
      'dollar SSE', dollar_k2.inertia_, 'ARI', ari)
print('C9 raw/dollar cluster IDs', raw_k2.labels_[8], dollar_k2.labels_[8])

# Inspect relative memberships rather than numeric cluster IDs.
raw_c9_peers = [i+1 for i in range(8)
                if raw_k2.labels_[i] == raw_k2.labels_[8]]
dollar_c9_peers = [i+1 for i in range(8)
                   if dollar_k2.labels_[i] == dollar_k2.labels_[8]]
print('C9 shares a group with, raw/dollar:', raw_c9_peers, dollar_c9_peers)

# Extra independent check available for n=9 only: all 255 unlabelled
# nonempty 2-cluster partitions, fixing the first point in cluster 0.
def exact_best_sse(data):
    best = float('inf')
    for flags in product([0, 1], repeat=8):
        assignment = np.array((0,) + flags)
        if assignment.sum() == 0:
            continue
        sse = sum(((data[assignment == j] -
                    data[assignment == j].mean(axis=0))**2).sum()
                  for j in (0, 1))
        best = min(best, sse)
    return best

assert np.isclose(exact_best_sse(customers), 68.0)
assert np.isclose(exact_best_sse(dollar_customers), 904.8)
assert np.isclose(raw_k2.inertia_, 68.0)
assert np.isclose(dollar_k2.inertia_, 904.8)
assert np.isclose(ari, 0.55)
assert raw_c9_peers == [5, 6, 7, 8]
assert dollar_c9_peers == [1, 2, 3, 4]

# DBSCAN's eps=2.1 is not portable across a change in units.
db_dollars = DBSCAN(eps=2.1, min_samples=3).fit(dollar_customers)

assert (db_dollars.labels_ == -1).all()

The manual loop should report centroids (2,2) and (9.8,7.6) with SSE=68, followed by an unchanged iteration. The k-Means inertia values for k=1..5 should be 272.8889, 68, 16, 12 and 8. DBSCAN with eps=2.1 should return two four-customer groups and C9 as Noise; check structural group membership rather than trusting arbitrary numeric group IDs.

Important implementation distinction: scikit-learn `KMeans.predict()` can assign new points to learned centroids. `DBSCAN` does not provide a general `predict()` method for unseen data. Assigning a fresh customer to an existing density cluster requires a separately defined rule or an appropriate extension; silently refitting the whole dataset is not the same operation.

24. From exploration to use: a new customer and a defensible workflow

Suppose a new customer arrives with (2,2): two visits/month and a $20 average basket. Using the frozen two-centroid k-Means fit, its distances to (2,2) and (9.8,7.6) are 0 and \(\sqrt{7.8^2+5.6^2}=\sqrt{92.2}\approx9.602\). It belongs to the first cluster. A customer at (11,9) would instead be 1.8439 from (9.8,7.6) and far from (2,2), joining the second. Assigning via `predict` is different from refitting and moving both centroids.

A responsible retail workflow would: define an intended use, verify the time when Features are known, fit preprocessing and clustering on development data, evaluate stability/sensitivity with suitable held-out periods, interpret groups with domain staff, test a modest decision intervention ethically, and monitor changes. If you select k or eps using a held-out period repeatedly, it is no longer a clean independent check.

A future Jupyter/R lab can extend this guide by comparing Euclidean to Manhattan distances, fitting scaled and unscaled features, resampling customers, and visualizing whether business segment descriptions survive these perturbations. Keep that investigation separate from claiming the synthetic toy groups represent actual retail customers.

25. Common mistakes: diagnose the reasoning, not only the vocabulary

Each mistake below identifies the tempting but incorrect conclusion and the specific remedy. These are not small editorial details: several would lead a retailer to act on unstable or unfair segments.

1. Using Accuracy without labels

Accuracy needs trusted targets; report cohesion, stability and domain use instead.

2. Treating a cluster ID as an ordinal rating

Cluster 0 is not intrinsically inferior to cluster 1; labels are arbitrary and can permute.

3. Changing dollars to tens of dollars without reconsidering distances

Recompute distances and justify units or fit an appropriate Scaler on Training.

4. Claiming the smallest inertia proves the best k

Inertia usually decreases as k increases; inspect Elbow, Silhouette, segment sizes and purpose.

5. Using a singleton as proof of a useful segment

A singleton can be an outlier. Silhouette convention assigns zero; assess business meaning.

6. Calling k-Means centers observed customers

Centroids are arithmetic means; a centroid may be no actual customer.

7. Claiming alternating k-Means always finds the global optimum

It monotonically improves a local objective; multiple starts mitigate local traps.

8. Reading a Dendrogram without a linkage definition

Heights depend on metric and Single/Complete/Average definitions.

9. Calling every DBSCAN point a Core point

Core requires min_samples inside eps, Border does not, Noise is neither.

10. Forgetting that DBSCAN counts the point itself

min_samples=3 can mean self plus two other neighbors.

11. Selecting eps without testing sensitivity

Small changes can turn clusters into Noise or merge distinct regions.

12. Comparing scores computed on different sample subsets

DBSCAN excluding Noise and k-Means including all customers have different denominators.

13. Refitting a Scaler using future/Test records

Compute fitted statistics on Training and reuse them unchanged for new records.

14. Mistaking clusters for causal human identities

Groups describe similarity under given data and assumptions, not immutable traits.

15. Pretending DBSCAN has standard out-of-sample predict

Define a new assignment rule or method; do not invent a built-in `predict`.

16. Failing to audit an apparently beautiful plot

Check complete datasets, mathematics, business assumptions and operational stability.

26. Practice and solved exercises: calculate, interpret, transfer

Try to solve each task before revealing the answer. These questions test three learning outcomes: conceptual explanation, mathematical reconstruction and transfer to a new situation. The arithmetic relies only on the displayed datasets.

1. Find the Euclidean distance between C2=(1,3) and C4=(3,3).

The x difference is −2 and the y difference is 0. Thus sqrt[4+0]=2. The neighbors are two visit units apart in our numeric Feature Space.

2. Why does a centroid not need to equal an actual customer?

The centroid minimizes summed squared deviations coordinate-by-coordinate; a mean such as (9.8,7.6) is a valid center even if no one has that exact basket.

3. Compute the updated x-coordinate of the second k-Means centroid.

The five x values are 8+8+10+10+13=49; divide by 5 to obtain 9.8.

4. Reconstruct C9’s SSE contribution.

(13−9.8)²+(2−7.6)²=3.2²+(−5.6)²=10.24+31.36=41.60. This is more than half the total SSE=68.

5. Why does k=3 have lower inertia than k=2?

With three centers at (2,2),(9,9),(13,2), SSE=8+8+0=16 rather than 68. More centers allow a smaller within-cluster squared distance but do not guarantee useful segmentation.

6. Explain why k=1 inertia is 272.8889.

The single center is (57/9,46/9). Sum every customer’s two squared coordinate deviations from this center; the result is 272.8889.

7. Compute a(C1) for the k=2 Silhouette.

Distances from C1 to its three companions are 2,2,√8. Their mean is (2+2+2.8284)/3=2.2761. The point itself is excluded.

8. Interpret a negative Silhouette for some future customer.

It would mean the mean distance to the nearest other cluster is lower than to its own cluster; inspect possible misassignment, scaling and cluster shape, not immediately “bad customer”.

9. Given A={C1,C2} and B={C3}, compute Complete Linkage.

Distances across groups are 2 and √8≈2.828. Complete takes the maximum: 2.828, not the average or minimum.

10. What does a tall branch of a Dendrogram prove?

It proves only that two parts merge at a large distance under the chosen metric and linkage. It does not establish causal or statistical significance.

11. Count C1’s DBSCAN neighborhood for eps=2.1, min_samples=3.

C1,C2,C3 qualify at distances 0,2,2. C4 at √8>2.1 does not. Count 3, so C1 is Core.

12. Why is C10=(5,3) Border rather than Core?

It is within eps=2.1 of Core C4 at distance 2, but its own neighborhood is {C10,C4}: only 2

13. What happens if eps=1.9?

Nearest corners are distance 2, outside the radius; each observation sees only itself. No Core exists, so all nine are Noise.

14. Explain why DBSCAN cannot simply `predict()` a new point.

Ordinary scikit-learn DBSCAN exposes labels for fitted samples, not a general rule for unseen points. A new assignment rule, incremental method or justified refitting must be chosen explicitly.

15. What is Data Leakage in a clustering preprocessing workflow?

Computing StandardScaler means and variances from held-out future data before evaluation leaks distribution information. Fit on Training only and transform held-out points with frozen values.

16. Explain why the best numeric Silhouette alone need not define a useful retail segment.

Here k=3 reaches 0.6602 vs 0.6508 for k=2 but creates a singleton. Segment size, stability, customer purpose and consequences must be evaluated.

17. New customer at (2,2): assign using the saved k-Means centers.

Its distances are 0 to (2,2) and sqrt[(−7.8)²+(−5.6)²]=sqrt(92.2)≈9.602 to (9.8,7.6). It belongs to Cluster 1 without refitting.

18. Explain an ethical limit of this segmentation.

The cluster code is not a statement about someone’s identity or worth. Uses require privacy protection, business relevance and ongoing checks for harmful or unstable decisions.

19. Verify C9’s changed assignment and its new individual SSE contribution.

In dollar coordinates, the new centers are A=(4.2,$20) and B=(9,$90). C9=(13,$20) belongs to A. Its squared-distance contribution is (13−4.2)²+(20−20)²=8.8²+0=77.44. In the original representation, it belonged to B and contributed 41.60. Comparing 77.44 to 41.60 does not compare model quality: units of the second coordinate changed, so the objective changed.

20. Compute ARI for the original and dollar-coordinate partitions. What does it mean?

The contingency table is [[4,0],[1,4]]. Matching combinations within its cells yield T=6+0+0+6=12. Row and column totals give R=C=16; the total number of pairs is N=36. Expected agreement is E=16·16/36=64/9. Thus ARI=(12−64/9)/(16−64/9)=44/80=0.55. This exposes sensitivity to feature representation; it does not prove the discovery of true or natural customer categories.

27. Glossary, connected learning and the final student test

Clustering: discovering groups without supplied category labels. Centroid: coordinate-wise cluster mean. Inertia/SSE: sum of squared distances to assigned centroids. k-Means: alternating nearest-centroid assignment and mean update. k-means++: initialization designed to spread starting centers. Linkage: rule for distance between groups during hierarchical merges. Dendrogram: tree depicting nested merges and their heights. Silhouette: relative within- versus between-cluster distance. Core/Border/Noise: roles in density clustering. eps: DBSCAN neighborhood radius. min_samples: minimum neighborhood population including the point itself. Data Leakage: unwanted influence of unavailable/evaluation data on fitting or selection.

After Dimensionality Reduction (Guide 25), Gaussian Mixture Models and Expectation-Maximization (Guide 26) will introduce probabilistic soft membership. A customer may receive several membership probabilities instead of exactly one deterministic k-Means label. This guide prepares that comparison without claiming k-Means and DBSCAN produce calibrated probabilities.
