Anomaly Detection and Association Rule Mining from First Principles

Detect unusual readings without claiming faults, and discover recurring baskets without confusing association with causation: eight sensor readings, twelve receipts and fully worked arithmetic.

1. Why unusual observations and recurring combinations are different questions

A cold-storage retailer collects machinery sensor measurements and customer receipts. Two legitimate Unsupervised Learning questions emerge: Is this vibration reading unusual relative to comparable operating conditions? and Which products tend to occur together within a receipt? The first produces an anomaly score or an alert; the second produces frequent Itemsets and Association Rules. Neither produces a true fault label or proves that buying one product causes another purchase.

Learning outcomes. By the end you should be able to (1) calculate classical and robust outlier scores by hand, (2) explain and check Local Outlier Factor (LOF) and Isolation Forest, (3) calculate Support, Confidence and Lift from every original receipt, (4) trace Apriori candidates and pruning, (5) design defensible validation and thresholds without future-data Leakage, and (6) execute two standalone reproducible programs.

This chapter uses two deliberately small, entirely synthetic datasets: eight vibration measurements in millimeters per second (mm/s) and twelve transaction baskets. Neither provides real equipment failures or observed commercial benefits. The methods belong together because they uncover structure without labels, but we will not pretend that association mining is an anomaly detector.

2. Anomaly Detection: the eight original sensor readings

A refrigeration compressor reports root-mean-square vibration amplitude in mm/s at eight evenly spaced checkpoints, under supposedly comparable conditions. Its ordered readings are fully disclosed below. These numbers are invented for mathematics; a real vibration limit would require equipment specifications and operating context.

ID S1 S2 S3 S4 S5 S6 S7 S8
Vibration (mm/s) 2.0 2.2 2.4 2.6 2.8 3.0 3.2 8.0

There are point anomalies (a reading unusual on its own), contextual anomalies (unusual only at a given load, season, shift or temperature), and collective anomalies (a sequence whose overall pattern is unusual even when each individual value appears plausible). Eight isolated scalar values can illustrate only the first type. We cannot conclude whether S8 indicates a mechanical defect, unit conversion, changed speed, measurement glitch or harmless operating regime.

The fundamental question is unusual compared with what reference? If we include future readings or contaminated historical periods indiscriminately, even a correct mathematical formula may generate poor alerts.

3. Mean, Sample Standard Deviation and the classical Z-score

The mean balances all values but moves when one value is extreme. Summing explicitly gives \(2.0+2.2+2.4+2.6+2.8+3.0+3.2+8.0=26.2\) mm/s, so

\[\bar x=\frac{26.2}{8}=3.275\ \mathrm{mm/s}. \]

The Sample Standard Deviation uses denominator \(n-1=7\), reflecting the conventional sample-variance definition. The eight squared deviations, in \((\mathrm{mm/s})^2\), are:

\[s=\sqrt{\frac{26.635}{7}}=1.950641\ \mathrm{mm/s},\qquad z_{S8}=\frac{8-3.275}{1.950641}=2.4223.\]

The Z-score is dimensionless: mm/s divided by mm/s. Under an illustrative \(|z|>3\) cutoff, S8 would not be flagged. This does not prove S8 is normal: the extreme reading increased both the mean and the standard deviation. A Gaussian tail interpretation also requires stronger distributional assumptions than these eight readings justify.

4. Median, Median Absolute Deviation and a robust Z-score

The Median is the midpoint of sorted readings. With eight values it is the mean of the fourth and fifth: \((2.6+2.8)/2=2.7\) mm/s. The Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) is the median of the eight distances to 2.7, not their mean:

Reading 2.0 2.2 2.4 2.6 2.8 3.0 3.2 8.0
\(|x_i-2.7|\) 0.7 0.5 0.3 0.1 0.1 0.3 0.5 5.3

Sorted absolute deviations are 0.1, 0.1, 0.3, 0.3, 0.5, 0.5, 0.7, 5.3, hence \(\mathrm{MAD}=(0.3+0.5)/2=0.4\) mm/s. The factor 1.4826 rescales MAD to estimate standard deviation under an approximately Gaussian reference distribution; it does not make any dataset Gaussian.

\[z_i^{\rm robust}=\frac{x_i-\mathrm{median}(x)}{1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}},\qquad z_{S8}^{\rm robust}=\frac{8-2.7}{1.4826(0.4)}=8.9370.\]

With the separately chosen illustrative cutoff \(|z^{\rm robust}|>3.5\), S8 is flagged. Compare the earlier \(2.4223\) with \(8.9370\): the denominator matters. This demonstrates resistance to a single extreme value; it does not establish that 3.5 is an appropriate operational threshold. If MAD equals zero (many identical measurements), the formula is undefined; a different scale estimate, tie policy or domain-specific method is needed.

5. Turn a score into an alert—and examine sensitivity

A score measures unusualness relative to a reference; a decision threshold translates it into an action. With \(|z^{\rm robust}|>3.5\), readings S1–S7 are below the cutoff and S8 exceeds it. For instance, S1 has \((2.0-2.7)/0.59304=-1.1804\), not a fault indicator by itself. S8 has absolute score 8.9370.

Sensitivity analysis. If the cutoff rises from 3.5 to 9.0, no reading is flagged; if it falls to 1.0, only S1 and S8 are flagged: S1 has absolute score 1.1804, S7 has only \((3.2-2.7)/0.59304=0.8431\), and S8 has 8.9370. Thus the correct count is two alerts at 1.0, one at 3.5, and zero at 9.0. This illustrates why alert totals must be recomputed rather than guessed.

An operator should decide whether the score triggers remeasurement, a technician review or shutdown. Without confirmed outcomes we cannot compute meaningful Precision or Recall. If verified faults eventually exist, assess false alerts, missed faults, alert volume, lead time and asymmetric costs on a later time period.

6. Distance, neighborhoods and Feature Scaling

In more than one dimension, measuring "far away" requires a distance rule. Euclidean Distance between \(x=(x_1,x_2)\) and \(y=(y_1,y_2)\) is \(d(x,y)=\sqrt{(x_1-y_1)^2+(x_2-y_2)^2}\). For vibration 2.0 mm/s and temperature 4 °C versus vibration 3.0 mm/s and 5 °C, naïvely mixing raw units gives \(\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt 2\)—a number with no coherent physical unit. Distances become dominated by whichever Feature has the larger numeric scale.

For many Euclidean or density-based algorithms, we therefore transform using Training-only parameters, for example \(z_j=(x_j-\mu_j^{\rm train})/s_j^{\rm train}\) for Feature \(j\). At inference time, reuse exactly those saved numbers: do not refit on a suspicious reading or future week. Beware that standardization may conceal a physically meaningful unit difference; domain-based scaling can be preferable. Zero standard deviation requires an explicit feature policy.

Distance methods can miss contextual anomalies: a vibration of 4 mm/s might be normal during startup but dangerous at idle. Add operating regime or compare within relevant strata. More features can also degrade neighborhood geometry through high-dimensional distance concentration.

7. Local Outlier Factor: complete five-point density calculation

Local Outlier Factor (LOF) asks whether a point occupies a less dense neighborhood than its neighbors. Unlike a simple global center, LOF can respect regions with different local densities. Consider these five additional synthetic points, with dimensionless coordinates: \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(0,1)\), \(C=(1,0)\), \(D=(1,1)\) and \(E=(5,5)\). Set the neighbor count \(k=2\), excluding each point itself.

  1. k-distance. Each of A, B, C, D has second-neighbor distance 1. E's closest neighbor is D at \(\sqrt{(5-1)^2+(5-1)^2}=\sqrt{32}=5.656854\); its second-neighbor distance is \(\sqrt{41}=6.403124\) to B or C. Resolve the tie consistently (choose B here).
  2. Reachability distance. For any neighbor \(o\), \(\operatorname{reach}_k(E,o)=\max\{k\mathrm{dist}(o),d(E,o)\}\). Thus the values for neighbors D and B are \(\max(1,5.656854)=5.656854\) and \(\max(1,6.403124)=6.403124\).
  3. Local reachability density. \(\operatorname{lrd}_k(E)=1/[(5.656854+6.403124)/2]=0.165838\) per coordinate unit. For D and B, every reachability distance to their two nearest dense neighbors is 1, so both have \(\operatorname{lrd}=1\).
  4. LOF ratio. \(\operatorname{LOF}_k(E)=[(1+1)/2]/0.165838=6.029989\). For A, the analogous ratio is \([(1+1)/2]/1=1\).

A LOF near 1 means comparable neighborhood density; a large value means locally sparse relative to neighbors. LOF is neither a failure probability nor automatically comparable across different \(k\) and datasets. Note that scikit-learn uses negative_outlier_factor_ with the opposite sign (approximately −6.03 for E in this example). If coordinates are duplicated or distances tie extensively, implementation tie conventions matter.

8. Isolation Forest: the idea of shorter isolation paths

Isolation Forest repeatedly separates observations with randomized split Features and split values. A point in an exposed region often takes fewer splits to become isolated. Unlike LOF, it does not start by estimating a neighborhood density. This is especially useful for multidimensional datasets, but results depend on sampling, randomness, Features and configuration.

Using the original eight readings, illustrate a single deterministic split at \(5.6\) mm/s: the left child contains S1–S7, while the right child contains only S8. S8 is isolated at path length \(h=1\). In a full Isolation Forest, many independently randomized trees are averaged; this particular split is illustrative, not a guarantee that every tree isolates S8 immediately.

For a tree built from \(n\) distinct training points, the standard normalization is \(c(n)=2H_{n-1}-2(n-1)/n\), where \(H_m=\sum_{j=1}^{m}1/j\). At \(n=8\):

\[H_7=1+\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\tfrac14+\tfrac15+\tfrac16+\tfrac17=2.592857.\]
\[c(8)=2(2.592857)-\frac{14}{8}=3.435714.\]

The published Isolation Forest anomaly-score form is \(s(x,n)=2^{-E[h(x)]/c(n)}\), where \(E[h(x)]\) is a forest-averaged, adjusted path length. Only as a single-tree illustration, plugging \(h=1\) gives \(2^{-1/3.435714}=0.8173\). This is not a measured Isolation Forest score; to estimate one we need all trees, their subsamples and leaf-size adjustments. Different libraries can expose a differently signed or shifted score, so verify the API before interpreting thresholds.

9. Isolation Forest versus LOF, novelty versus outlier detection

A point can be unusual globally but not locally. LOF compares neighboring density; Isolation Forest looks for easy-to-isolate configurations of Feature values. Neither detects all anomalies, and neither is supervised classification merely because its library returns labels +1 and −1.

Outlier Detection often fits on data that might already include unusual points. Novelty Detection builds a reference model using mostly normal historical behavior, then scores future observations; these are different deployment assumptions. In scikit-learn, LocalOutlierFactor(novelty=False) provides fit_predict for the training sample but does not support the same predict workflow for future samples; novelty=True enables a separate prediction path on unseen observations. Do not use training-set predict scores under novelty mode as if they were equivalent to negative_outlier_factor_.

IsolationForest exposes score_samples, decision_function and predict. Its decision_function has positive = inlier, negative = outlier relative to the chosen offset; a smaller score_samples value is more anomalous. The contamination setting controls the threshold/offset estimation rather than proving the true fault prevalence. Use an operational budget or labeled Validation when selecting actionable thresholds.

10. A multivariate alternative: Mahalanobis distance and assumptions

If features correlate, Euclidean distance may count the same change twice. Mahalanobis Distance accounts for covariance: \(D_M(x)=\sqrt{(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}\), where \(\mu\in\mathbb R^p\) is the reference center, \(\Sigma\in\mathbb R^{p\times p}\) is an invertible reference covariance matrix, and \(x\in\mathbb R^p\) is the candidate. The result is dimensionless when all covariance units are consistent.

For a transparent two-dimensional example, let \(\mu=(0,0)^\top\), \(\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(x=(2,1)^\top\). Since \(\Sigma^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1/4&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\):

\[D_M^2=(2,1)\begin{bmatrix}1/4&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=2^2/4+1^2/1=2,\]
\[D_M=\sqrt 2\approx1.4142.\]

By contrast Euclidean distance would be \(\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt5\). Mahalanobis recognizes that a change of two units along the first dimension corresponds to one reference standard deviation. With a singular covariance matrix the ordinary inverse does not exist; regularization or a justified pseudoinverse is required. A Chi-square cutoff for squared distance needs distributional and estimation assumptions; it is not guaranteed for arbitrary sensor data.

11. Threshold calibration, costs, and honest evaluation

A system rarely has unlimited capacity to investigate every alarm. Suppose reviewing an alert costs US$40, missing a genuine equipment fault costs US$2,000, and 100 new readings arrive per day. This is a hypothetical decision problem, not a measured cost study. One alert per day costs US$40/day to review; ten cost US$400/day. Yet lowering alert count without regard to missed faults could be disastrous.

Where confirmed historical labels exist, tune the threshold on Validation, preferably from later dates and with comparable operating regimes. Then freeze the model and threshold before checking an untouched Test period. Precision = confirmed true alerts / all reviewed alerts; Recall = detected true faults / all real faults. If no verified labels exist, report only alert rates and case reviews, not supervised accuracy. A carefully audited sample of alerts and non-alerts can help identify blind spots.

If an Isolation Forest is fitted to Training, store the preprocessing, model parameters and threshold. Apply all three unchanged to future data. Do not choose a score cutoff after looking at Test labels. Time-to-failure labels may mature late; using them prematurely creates Leakage. Operators need human review, safety procedures and escalation when the algorithm's assumptions do not hold.

This is a capacity illustration, not a valid estimate of Precision, Recall, False Positive Rate or failure probability. Decide tie handling (\(>\) versus \(\ge\)) in advance. Retuning using the later block would destroy its role as an independent check.

12. Drift, rare events, and consequences of a false positive

The distribution that defined "normal" may move after equipment maintenance, sensor replacement, seasonal load changes or a new store opening. Data Drift means input distributions shift; Concept Drift means the relationship between inputs and actual fault status changes. An alerting system must track sensor availability, missingness, input distributions, score distribution, confirmed outcomes and alert workload.

A false positive can cause costly refrigeration shutdowns; a false negative can jeopardize food safety. Thus the same mathematical anomaly score need not imply the same response for every machine. Equipment criticality, regulations and human inspection protocols belong to the operational decision layer. A sensor anomaly is not a causal explanation of failure.

A valid monitoring plan specifies how readings are collected, which baseline is current, who approves retraining, how score drift is detected and which future labeled data authorize threshold changes. Retrospectively recomputing the baseline using the suspected incident itself may conceal the very anomaly being investigated.

13. From sensor readings to shopping baskets: a different data type

Association Rule Mining studies sets of items per transaction, not distances between sensor measurements. A receipt containing Bread, Milk and Coffee is a set \(\{B,M,C\}\); order and quantities are omitted in this introductory binary representation. A different business question (purchase sequence, amount, quantity or customer identity) needs a different model.

The universe of items is \(I=\{B,M,C,T,Q\}\) for Bread, Milk, Coffee, Tea and Coupon. A transaction is a subset of \(I\). An Itemset such as \(\{B,M\}\) occurs in a receipt when both items are present. An Association Rule \(X\Rightarrow Y\) requires \(X\cap Y=\varnothing\): its left side is the Antecedent, its right side the Consequent. The arrow means conditional co-occurrence, not cause-and-effect.

We deliberately included Coupon as an item because promotional campaigns can mechanically attach coupons to particular products. A perfect Tea–Coupon association will be pedagogically useful but should not be mistaken for a promising cross-selling intervention.

15. Support Counts, Support and the frequency denominator

For an Itemset \(X\), let \(N(X)\) be the number of receipts containing every item in \(X\), and let \(N=12\) be total receipts. Support is \(\operatorname{supp}(X)=N(X)/N\). It measures frequency over all baskets, not just baskets containing the Antecedent.

Scan the original table and count single items: \(N(B)=9\), \(N(M)=9\), \(N(C)=7\), \(N(T)=3\), \(N(Q)=3\). Frequent pairs include \(N(BM)=7\), \(N(BC)=5\), \(N(MC)=5\), \(N(TQ)=3\); the triple \(N(BMC)=4\). For example:

\[\operatorname{supp}(BM)=7/12=0.5833,\quad \operatorname{supp}(BMC)=4/12=0.3333.\]

We will use Minimum Support Count 3, equivalently \(3/12=0.25\). The threshold deliberately retains the Tea–Coupon pair but excludes single-occurrence pairings. Changing the transaction definition or denominator would change all Supports; a fixed minimum-support percentage does not guarantee that a pattern is statistically stable or commercially useful.

16. The Apriori downward-closure principle and its proof

Apriori reduces search by exploiting a deterministic mathematical fact: if \(A\subseteq B\) are Itemsets, every basket containing \(B\) also contains \(A\). Therefore the set of baskets containing \(B\) is a subset of those containing \(A\), so

\[N(B)\le N(A),\qquad \operatorname{supp}(B)\le\operatorname{supp}(A).\]

This is the Anti-Monotonicity / Downward Closure property of Support. It is a set-inclusion proof, not a heuristic. Consequently, if a pair such as Bread–Tea occurs only once, every superset containing Bread and Tea occurs at most once and cannot meet our required count of three. We may safely prune Bread–Tea–Coupon without counting every possible candidate triple.

Do not confuse this rule with Confidence or Lift: those measures need not decrease when adding items. Pruning based on Support is exact for the chosen transaction table and threshold; it does not imply a low-support scientific discovery is impossible, only that it fails the selected mining criterion.

19. Confidence is conditional; it does not correct for a common consequent

For disjoint Itemsets \(X\) and \(Y\), Confidence is the share of transactions containing \(X\) that also contain \(Y\): \(\operatorname{conf}(X\Rightarrow Y)=N(X\cup Y)/N(X)\). Thus Confidence estimates a sample conditional frequency, not an experimental treatment effect.

Take Bread \(\Rightarrow\) Milk. Nine baskets contain Bread; seven of those also contain Milk. Thus \(\operatorname{conf}(B\Rightarrow M)=7/9=0.7778\), or 77.78%. This looks high until we notice that 9 of all 12 baskets contain Milk anyway: \(\operatorname{supp}(M)=9/12=0.75\).

Lift compares the conditional frequency with this base rate:

\[\operatorname{lift}(B\Rightarrow M)=\frac{\operatorname{conf}(B\Rightarrow M)}{\operatorname{supp}(M)}=\frac{7/9}{9/12}=\frac{28}{27}=1.0370.\]

A Lift of 1 is independence in the observed transaction frequencies; greater than 1 is positive co-occurrence, less than 1 negative co-occurrence. 1.037 is only a small difference from independence in this tiny synthetic sample. A 77.78% Confidence does not justify a campaign when the baseline is already 75%. We have no uncertainty interval or test of whether this difference generalizes.

20. A rule with Lift 4 can still be commercially meaningless

Now consider Tea \(\Rightarrow\) Coupon. Tea occurs in three baskets (R9–R11), and Coupon appears in exactly those same three. Therefore:

\[\operatorname{supp}(TQ)=3/12=0.25,\quad \operatorname{conf}(T\Rightarrow Q)=3/3=1.\]
\[\operatorname{lift}(T\Rightarrow Q)=\frac{1}{3/12}=4.00.\]

Compare this with Bread \(\Rightarrow\) Milk visually:

In our constructed system, a coupon is automatically attached to purchases containing Tea. A perfect pattern with Lift 4 is therefore expected by the recording rule. Offering the same coupon does not necessarily cause additional tea sales. The association may be redundant, promotional, time-specific, or a logging artifact. The sample has only three Tea baskets, so generalization is especially uncertain.

If the aim is to increase tea purchases, appropriate evidence would be a credible comparison of customers offered and not offered the promotion under an ethical experimental design. Rule mining can generate hypotheses; it does not estimate the incremental effect of an action.

21. Rule direction, negative association and additional measures

The two directions of a rule usually differ. For example, \(\operatorname{conf}(B\Rightarrow C)=N(BC)/N(B)=5/9=0.5556\), while \(\operatorname{conf}(C\Rightarrow B)=5/7=0.7143\). The intersection is symmetric but the denominator is not. The Lift in either direction is symmetric because \(N(BC)N/[N(B)N(C)]=5(12)/(9\cdot7)=20/21=0.95238\).

This Lift below 1 indicates slightly less co-occurrence than predicted by the product of the marginal frequencies. Again, 12 receipts cannot establish a robust negative pattern. Leverage quantifies the absolute difference between observed joint Support and independence reference: \(\operatorname{lev}(B,C)=5/12-(9/12)(7/12)=5/12-63/144=-3/144=-0.020833\). Leverage is measured as a fraction of transactions; Lift is a ratio without units.

Another metric, Conviction, is \(\operatorname{conv}(X\Rightarrow Y)=[1-\operatorname{supp}(Y)]/[1-\operatorname{conf}(X\Rightarrow Y)]\) when the denominator is nonzero. For Bread \(\Rightarrow\) Milk it is \((1-0.75)/(1-7/9)=0.25/(2/9)=1.125\). If Confidence = 1 and the Consequent's Support is less than 1, Conviction is formally infinite; it does not demonstrate causal certainty. If an Antecedent has Support zero, Confidence is undefined and the rule must not be computed.

22. Multiple comparisons, rule selection and temporal validation

Searching thousands of Itemsets and choosing only the largest Lift risks selection bias: even unrelated items may look associated by chance. Low-support rules can show spectacular ratios because one or two receipts dominate the denominator. Apriori's exact counting does not correct for multiple testing, temporally correlated customers, seasonality or experiment design.

A disciplined workflow separates a discovery period from later Validation. In discovery, choose Itemsets, Support threshold and rule direction. In later Validation, freeze the proposed rules and calculate new support, conditional frequency, Lift and uncertainty on new receipts, using compatible transaction definitions. If you repeatedly tune on Validation, set aside a subsequent untouched Test period for a final assessment. Customer-repeat effects require group-aware treatment when independence matters.

To estimate practical benefit, compare the proposed business action against a baseline. Co-purchase alone cannot tell us whether a recommendation changes customer behavior. A/B testing or credible causal evaluation might be necessary for such a claim. Protect privacy: mining very rare combinations of sensitive purchases may expose individuals even when names are removed.

Step 2 — Later Validation. Of R9–R12, only R12 contains both items; Bread occurs in R9 and R12; Milk in R10 and R12. Therefore:

\[\operatorname{conf}_V=\frac12=0.50,\qquad\operatorname{lift}_V=\frac{1/2}{2/4}=1.\]

Step 3 — Interpretation. Confidence falls from 0.8571 to 0.50, but Milk's marginal Support also falls from 0.875 to 0.50. Hence Lift stays near one in both periods. We cannot infer that Bread predicts Milk beyond its baseline or that the change reflects a commercial trend: Validation contains only four receipts. Pooling all twelve receipts describes the combined sample but does not replicate this Validation design.

Integrity rule. In a real study, discover on an earlier block, freeze the candidate, and test on genuinely unseen later data; retain stable Transaction definitions, investigate promotions, and account for repeat customers. Do not present this retrospective exercise as external validation or causal evidence.

23. Apriori versus FP-Growth, and when not to mine rules

Apriori counts candidate Itemsets level by level and prunes supersets of infrequent sets. This makes its logic transparent but can require repeated dataset scans and large candidate lists. FP-Growth compresses transactions into a frequency-oriented prefix tree (FP-tree) and mines patterns without explicitly enumerating the same candidate lists. Both target frequent Itemsets under chosen support rules; neither solves causal inference or makes a business decision.

Consider a catalog with 50,000 products but receipts averaging only three items. The number of all possible Itemsets is \(2^{50000}-1\), so exhaustive enumeration is impossible. Efficient algorithms exploit sparse baskets and minimum Support, but resource demands still depend on basket width, frequency structure and threshold. A low threshold can flood analysts with redundant patterns, even if the algorithm runs successfully.

Do not mine rules when the question is explicitly about sequence (buy A today, B next month), quantities (three units versus one), price elasticity, or causal effect. Such questions need temporal, quantitative, econometric or experimental methods. In some workflows a simple frequency table is a better answer than thousands of Association Rules.

25. Reproducible Python lab A: robust anomaly scores, Isolation Forest and LOF

Run the following complete block in one fresh Python session with NumPy and scikit-learn installed. It reproduces Median = 2.7, MAD = 0.4 and robust S8 score ≈ 8.9370. Isolation Forest results are an illustration dependent on the stated seed and library; do not equate its signed decision score with probability of failure.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest

vibration = np.array([2.0, 2.2, 2.4, 2.6, 2.8, 3.0, 3.2, 8.0])
median = np.median(vibration)

robust_z = (vibration - median) / (1.4826 * mad)

print('robust flags:', np.where(np.abs(robust_z) > 3.5)[0] + 1)

print('IF decision scores:', np.round(forest.decision_function(vibration.reshape(-1, 1)), 4))

lof.fit_predict(xy)

# Separate hypothetical Validation scores, not the eight sensor readings.
validation_scores = np.array([.4, .6, .8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.4, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 3.6])

The LOF call demonstrates that scikit-learn reports the opposite sign through negative_outlier_factor_. These operations are educational, not an operationally validated sensor-monitoring system.

26. Reproducible Python lab B: exhaustive cross-check of Apriori patterns

The next standalone program verifies the small example using direct enumeration (all combinations), not an Apriori implementation. This is intentional: an independent computation checks the Apriori pruning logic and results. For a five-item toy basket this exhaustive reference is inexpensive; for a large catalog, use an optimized frequent-itemset implementation.

from collections import Counter
from itertools import combinations

# Synthetic transactions: one list element = one receipt.
receipts = [
    {'Bread','Milk','Coffee'}, {'Bread','Milk'},
    {'Bread','Milk','Coffee'}, {'Bread','Milk'},
    {'Bread','Coffee'}, {'Milk','Coffee'},
    {'Bread','Milk','Coffee'}, {'Bread','Milk'},
    {'Bread','Tea','Coupon'}, {'Milk','Tea','Coupon'},
    {'Coffee','Tea','Coupon'}, {'Bread','Milk','Coffee'}]
minimum_count = 3
items = sorted(set().union(*receipts))
frequent = {}
for size in range(1, len(items) + 1):
    for candidate in combinations(items, size):
        occurrences = sum(set(candidate).issubset(r) for r in receipts)
        if occurrences >= minimum_count:
            frequent[candidate] = occurrences
print('frequent size histogram:', dict(sorted(Counter(map(len, frequent)).items())))

count = lambda group: sum(set(group).issubset(r) for r in receipts)
N = len(receipts)
def measures(antecedent, consequent):
    a, c = set(antecedent), set(consequent)
    assert a and c and a.isdisjoint(c)
    both, na, nc = count(a | c), count(a), count(c)
    support = both / N
    confidence = both / na
    lift = confidence / (nc / N)
    return (round(support, 6), round(confidence, 6), round(lift, 6))
print('Bread => Milk:', measures({'Bread'}, {'Milk'}))
print('Tea => Coupon:', measures({'Tea'}, {'Coupon'}))
print('Bread => Coffee:', measures({'Bread'}, {'Coffee'}))


# Retrospective time-order illustration: reuse the same 12 receipts.
# Only the first 8 stand in for discovery; last 4 stand in for later checking.
def frozen_rule_period(block):
    n = len(block)
    n_b = sum('Bread' in receipt for receipt in block)
    n_m = sum('Milk' in receipt for receipt in block)
    n_bm = sum({'Bread', 'Milk'}.issubset(receipt) for receipt in block)
    return {'B': n_b, 'M': n_m, 'BM': n_bm,
            'support': n_bm / n,
            'confidence': n_bm / n_b,
            'lift': (n_bm / n_b) / (n_m / n)}

print('discovery B=>M:', frozen_rule_period(receipts[:8]))
print('later B=>M:', frozen_rule_period(receipts[8:]))

Expected histogram: 5 singletons, 4 pairs, 1 triple. Expected Bread→Milk \((\mathrm{Support},\mathrm{Confidence},\mathrm{Lift})=(0.583333,0.777778,1.037037)\); Tea→Coupon is \((0.25,1.0,4.0)\). Note that the rule-generation routine requires nonempty, disjoint Antecedent and Consequent. It does not pretend to evaluate statistical significance or causal actionability.

27. Frequent errors: diagnose the incorrect reasoning, not just its label

Mistake Why it fails and what to do instead
1. Anomaly = fault S8 is unusual under the eight-reading reference; check sensor context and confirm outcomes before fault claims.
2. Treating \(z\) as probability A Z-score is a standardized distance, not \(P(\text{fault})\). Distributional calibration would require labels and assumptions.
3. Assuming a mean is robust S8 shifts the mean to 3.275; Median stays 2.7. Compare sensitivity before choosing a center.
4. Confusing MAD with the mean deviation MAD is the median of \(0.7,0.5,0.3,0.1,0.1,0.3,0.5,5.3\): it equals 0.4.
5. Dividing by zero MAD When MAD = 0, robust Z is undefined; choose an explicit alternative rather than secretly adding a constant.
6. Mixing incompatible units Euclidean distance on mm/s and °C without a scaling policy is not physically coherent.
7. Misreading LOF signs scikit-learn's negative_outlier_factor_ is negative: compare \(-\mathrm{negative\_outlier\_factor\_}\) to conceptual LOF.
8. Interpreting one isolation split as a forest \(0.8173\) is a one-tree illustration, not the ensemble's observed score.
9. Using future data to set thresholds Fit references on allowed Training; tune decisions on Validation; keep Test untouched.
10. Using the wrong Support denominator Support(BM) = 7/12, not 7/9. The latter is Confidence(B→M).
11. Confusing Confidence with Lift Confidence(B→M) = 77.78%, but baseline Milk frequency is 75%; Lift is just 1.037.
12. Reading an arrow as causality Tea→Coupon's Lift 4 arises from an automatic coupon rule, not evidence of incremental sales.
13. Treating rule direction as symmetric Confidence(B→C) = 5/9 but Confidence(C→B) = 5/7. Denominators differ.
14. Applying Apriori pruning to Lift Downward closure holds for Support, not generally for Confidence or Lift.
15. Reporting rules selected on Test Mine on Discovery, check frozen rules on later records, and reserve Test for final evaluation.
16. Treating duplicate customers as independent A prolific shopper can dominate receipts; account for clustering and sampling in empirical claims.

These are diagnostic procedures rather than slogans: for each error, ask which denominator, assumption, unit, reference period or causal claim is being confused? A strong conclusion explains what the reported number supports and what it cannot establish.

28. Worked exercises: reproduce, diagnose and transfer

Before opening each solution, write the relevant numerator, denominator, input data and physical units. Exercises range from direct reproduction to a changed support threshold and a fresh evaluation protocol. The last exercises deliberately require reasoning beyond copying the original example.

1. Recompute the mean without S8. What changed?

S1–S7 sum to \(18.2\) mm/s. Their mean is \(18.2/7=2.6\) mm/s instead of \(3.275\). The change \(0.675\) mm/s demonstrates the sensitivity of the mean to an extreme reading.

2. Derive MAD again from the full list.

Median \(=(2.6+2.8)/2=2.7\); ordered absolute deviations are \(0.1,0.1,0.3,0.3,0.5,0.5,0.7,5.3\); hence MAD \(=(0.3+0.5)/2=0.4\) mm/s. Taking the mean would answer a different question.

3. What is S1’s robust Z-score?

\(z=(2.0-2.7)/(1.4826\cdot0.4)=-0.7/0.59304=-1.1804\). Its absolute value is below 3.5; this does not certify equipment health.

4. If the robust threshold becomes 1.0, which readings are flagged?

Only S1 (\(|-1.1804|>1\)) and S8 (\(8.9370>1\)). S7 has \(0.5/0.59304=0.8431\), so it is not flagged. The count changes from one alert to two.

5. Compute E-to-D distance in the LOF example.

\(d(E,D)=\sqrt{(5-1)^2+(5-1)^2}=\sqrt{16+16}=\sqrt{32}=5.656854\) coordinate units. The reachability distance uses the maximum of this and D’s k-distance 1.

6. Calculate LOF(E) without copying its answer.

Neighbors D and B have \(\mathrm{lrd}=1\). E’s \(\mathrm{lrd}=1/[(\sqrt{32}+\sqrt{41})/2]\). Therefore \(\mathrm{LOF}(E)=(\sqrt{32}+\sqrt{41})/2=6.029989\). The units cancel.

7. Does an isolation score of 0.817 mean an 81.7% failure probability?

No. The number came from the path normalization of one illustrative tree, not a calibrated posterior probability or even the fitted ensemble score. Actual failure probabilities require outcomes and validation.

8. Find the Mahalanobis squared distance for x=(0,2).

With \(\mu=(0,0)\) and \(\Sigma^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(1/4,1)\), \(D_M^2=0^2/4+2^2=4\), hence \(D_M=2\). Compare with \((2,1)\) giving \(\sqrt2\); the second coordinate has smaller reference variance.

9. Count Bread and Coffee co-occurrences and compute Support.

Both appear in R1, R3, R5, R7 and R12: five receipts. Support \(=5/12=0.4167\), not \(5/9\) (which conditions on Bread).

10. Calculate Confidence(Coffee → Bread).

Coffee occurs in seven receipts and Bread+Coffee in five; Confidence \(=5/7=0.7143\). The reversed rule is \(5/9=0.5556\); direction changes the denominator.

11. Compute Lift(Bread → Coffee) and interpret it.

Confidence \(=5/9\); Coffee Support \(=7/12\). Lift \(=(5/9)/(7/12)=20/21=0.95238\). This is slightly below 1 for this synthetic sample, not evidence of a stable negative causal relation.

12. Why is Bread–Tea–Coupon pruned by Apriori?

The Bread–Tea pair appears only in R9, so \(N(BT)=1<3\). Every superset containing BT, including BTQ, has count \(\le1\); it cannot meet minimum Support Count 3.

13. Change minimum support count from 3 to 4: what disappears?

Tea and Coupon singletons each have count 3, so both disappear; Tea–Coupon pair (3) also disappears. B (9), M (9), C (7), BM (7), BC (5), MC (5), and BMC (4) remain: 7 frequent Itemsets.

14. Compute Tea → Coupon Support, Confidence and Lift from scratch.

Support \(=N(TQ)/12=3/12=0.25\); Confidence \(=N(TQ)/N(T)=3/3=1\); baseline Coupon Support \(=3/12=0.25\); Lift \(=1/0.25=4\). Automatic promotions explain the pattern but do not establish sales uplift.

15. Why can Lift be symmetric when Confidence is not?

\(\mathrm{Lift}(X\Rightarrow Y)=\mathrm{supp}(XY)/[\mathrm{supp}(X)\mathrm{supp}(Y)]\), unchanged when swapping X and Y. Confidence divides by only the Antecedent Support, so directions differ.

16. Transfer: which validation design would support a real decision?

For anomalies: fit a reference on past normal-like Training, set thresholds using later Validation outcomes or alert capacity, and reserve Test. For rules: discover patterns on early baskets, freeze candidates and measure later co-occurrence, then evaluate any intervention causally. Neither synthetic exercise supports a real efficacy claim.

A correct numeric answer without a valid denominator, assumption or interpretation is incomplete. If you cannot reproduce LOF(E), Apriori pruning and the distinction between Confidence and Lift, revisit their derivations before proceeding.

17. Transfer: apply the frozen threshold t=1.8 to five later scores.

Scores: 1.1, 1.9, 2.4, 0.3, 3.0. Under the strict rule \(s>1.8\), 1.9, 2.4 and 3.0 are flagged: \(3/5=60\%\). Validation flagged \(2/10=20\%\). We did not change the threshold; this descriptive change proves neither Drift nor any failure rate.

18. Transfer: what if t=2.2, and how should exact ties be handled?

With \(s>2.2\), only 3.6 is above threshold in Validation: \(1/10=10\%\). Later, 2.4 and 3.0 are above: \(2/5=40\%\). A score equal to 2.2 is not included under \(>\). Switching to \(\ge\) after seeing results changes the count; fix the tie rule in advance.

19. Transfer: derive Lift(Bread→Milk) in Discovery and Validation without pooling data.

Discovery R1–R8: Bread 7, Milk 7, both 6; Confidence \(6/7\), Milk Support \(7/8\); Lift \(48/49\approx0.979592\). Validation R9–R12: Bread 2, Milk 2, both 1; Confidence \(1/2\), Milk Support \(2/4\); Lift = 1. Changed Confidence is neither proof of causal improvement nor degradation. Four tickets provide very limited descriptive evidence.

Term Precise meaning in this chapter
Anomaly Detection Ranking or identifying observations unusual relative to a reference.
Novelty Detection Scoring new points using an earlier, mostly normal reference model.
Median / MAD Robust center and median of absolute distances to that center.
LOF Ratio comparing neighbors’ reachability density with a point's density.
