1. Why learn a sequence of decisions?

Imagine a food distributor receiving refrigerated deliveries. Each shipment records hours outside its recommended temperature range and whether its packaging seal was compromised. Historical inspections indicate whether products were rejected for spoilage. A manager wants to identify shipments needing priority inspection, but the underlying decision should be understandable: Which observations led us to inspect this shipment?

A Decision Tree learns a sequence of questions such as “Was the temperature excursion longer than 3.75 hours?” Each question divides the historical observations into smaller groups. The algorithm seeks questions that make the resulting groups more homogeneous in their known outcomes. At a terminal group, called a leaf, it predicts a class or a numerical value.

Unlike Logistic Regression, the tree need not assume a linear change in Log-Odds. Unlike k-Nearest Neighbors, it does not calculate distances to every historical record when making a prediction. Instead, it learns reusable decision rules during Training. Its flexibility is attractive, but it also makes it easy to memorize tiny patterns rather than discover rules that generalize.

All shipments and measured outcomes in this chapter are synthetic and educational. A rejected shipment is an inspection outcome, not proof that a sensor caused spoilage. Real cold-chain safety decisions require domain protocols and independent validation; none of our illustrative thresholds is an operational safety limit.

2. Learning goals, prerequisites and the information boundary

By the end, you should be able to compute Gini Impurity and Entropy; enumerate legal thresholds; justify the best split; build a two-level tree; calculate leaf class proportions; derive a Regression Tree split using Squared Error; compare complexity and Validation performance; and reproduce the results in Python.

We assume only basic fractions, weighted averages and squared differences. We will explain Target (\(y\), the historical outcome), Feature (\(x\), a quantity observed before predicting), and Training, Validation, and Test. Training learns the tree; Validation compares candidate configurations such as max_depth; Test is reserved for the final unbiased-as-possible assessment of the frozen selection. None of the three datasets should be treated as a causal experiment.

A tree has parameters learned from Training (the actual splits, thresholds and leaf values) and hyperparameters specified by the analyst (for example maximum depth and minimum leaf size). Importantly, a number such as 3.75 in our tree is learned from Training: it is not a physical threshold mandated by the cold-chain industry.

4. From a question to a tree: root, branches, leaves and depth

A tree starts at a root node containing the Training observations. A split asks one question and sends each observation down one of two branches. An internal node asks another question; a leaf node stops and returns the class prediction and, often, an estimated class proportion. The depth of a node counts the number of edges from the root, so a root-only tree has depth 0.

For a numeric Feature, an example split is \(\text{Hours}\leq t\) versus \(\text{Hours}>t\). For binary Seal, a split can ask whether \(\text{Seal}\leq0.5\), equivalent to “Seal = 0?” Every historical record must follow exactly one branch at each node. If an observation lies exactly on the threshold, our convention routes it to the left (\(\leq\)).

A tree is a composition of simple rules, not an average of their predictions. A shipment with Hours = 3.0 and Seal = 1 follows the left branch at 3.75, then the compromised-seal branch. A shipment with Hours = 5.0 goes directly to the right leaf, regardless of Seal. We will derive, rather than assume, those questions below.

5. Gini Impurity: measure how mixed a node is

Before choosing a question, we need a measure of class mixing. Let \(p_k\) be the fraction of observations belonging to class \(k\) inside a node. Gini Impurity is \(G=1-\sum_k p_k^2\). For binary outcomes with positive fraction \(p\), this simplifies to \(G=1-p^2-(1-p)^2=2p(1-p)\).

At the root, \(p_1=7/12\) and \(p_0=5/12\). Substituting every number:

\[G_{\text{root}}=1-\left(\frac7{12}\right)^2-\left(\frac5{12}\right)^2=1-\frac{49+25}{144}=\frac{70}{144}=\frac{35}{72}\approx0.486111.\]

A pure node containing only one class has Gini 0; a perfectly balanced binary node has \(G=0.5\). Gini is not the probability that a particular shipment is spoiled. One interpretation is the chance that two independent class labels drawn from the node's empirical class distribution disagree. It depends on how many of each label occur, not on Features directly.

Check your understanding: What is the Gini of a leaf with three rejected and no accepted shipments? It is \(1-(3/3)^2-(0/3)^2=0\); that does not make three observations a reliable estimate of future performance.

6. Score a candidate numeric split with weighted Gini

Suppose a question divides twelve shipments into left and right groups of sizes \(n_L\) and \(n_R\). We evaluate it with weighted child impurity:

\[G_{\text{split}}=\frac{n_L}{12}G_L+\frac{n_R}{12}G_R,\qquad \Delta G=G_{\text{root}}-G_{\text{split}}.\]

We weight by group size because a perfectly pure leaf containing only one observation should not hide the mixing of the other eleven. For example, take \(t=3.75\) hours, halfway between the Training values 3.5 and 4.0. Left: T01–T07, with two rejected and five accepted; right: T08–T12, with five rejected and zero accepted. Therefore:

\[G_L=1-(2/7)^2-(5/7)^2=20/49\approx0.408163,\quad G_R=0.\]
\[G_{\text{split}}=(7/12)(20/49)+(5/12)(0)=\frac5{21}\approx0.238095.\]
\[\Delta G=\frac{35}{72}-\frac5{21}\approx0.248016.\]

The positive reduction means the question made the child groups collectively more homogeneous. We have not established that the question is best, only that it improves Gini. We must compare it with all legal candidate splits.

8. Compare the other Feature: why the root uses Hours

Hours is not the only possible split. Seal is binary, so we compare Seal = 0 versus Seal = 1. Of the six intact-seal shipments, two were rejected and four accepted; their Gini is \(1-(2/6)^2-(4/6)^2=4/9\approx0.444444\). Of the six compromised-seal shipments, five were rejected and one accepted; their Gini is \(1-(5/6)^2-(1/6)^2=5/18\approx0.277778\).

The combined score is:

\[G_{\text{Seal}}=(6/12)(4/9)+(6/12)(5/18)=\frac{13}{36}\approx0.361111.\]

Hours at 3.75 scores \(5/21\approx0.238095\), lower than \(13/36\). Since lower weighted impurity is better, Hours wins at the root. The comparison is fair because both questions are evaluated against the same original twelve observations and the same Gini definition.

A classic Decision Tree chooses greedily: it selects the best available split at the current node, then repeats within each resulting group. Greedy does not mean the entire resulting tree is guaranteed globally optimal among all trees of a chosen size. A different first question could potentially produce a better later combination on another dataset.

9. Entropy and Information Gain: an alternative split criterion

Gini is not the only measure of mixing. Shannon Entropy for class probabilities \(p_k\) is \(H=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k\), with the convention \(0\log_2 0=0\). Binary Entropy is 0 for a pure node and 1 bit when both classes are equally likely. Bits here describe uncertainty in an empirical class distribution, not file-storage size.

At the root:

\[H_{\text{root}}=-\frac7{12}\log_2\left(\frac7{12}\right)-\frac5{12}\log_2\left(\frac5{12}\right)\approx0.979869\;\text{bits}.\]

At the Hours split of 3.75, left Entropy is \(H(2/7,5/7)\approx0.863121\) bits and right Entropy is zero. Weighted Entropy is \((7/12)(0.863121)\approx0.503487\). Information Gain is thus \(0.979869-0.503487\approx0.476382\) bits. For Seal, weighted Entropy is about 0.784159 and the gain about 0.195710 bits. Both criteria therefore prefer Hours on our dataset, but Gini and Entropy are not mathematically identical and need not always rank candidate splits the same.

10. Grow the second level: solve the split, not just draw it

After the first split, the right node (Hours > 3.75) contains five rejected shipments and is already pure. The left node has seven shipments: T01–T07, with two rejected (T04, T06) and five accepted. Its current Gini is \(20/49\approx0.408163\). We now reconsider potential questions using only these seven records, not all twelve.

Split left by Seal. The four intact-seal shipments T01, T03, T05, T07 are all accepted; their Gini is 0. The three compromised-seal shipments T02, T04, T06 have labels \(0,1,1\), giving \(G=1-(1/3)^2-(2/3)^2=4/9\). The local weighted impurity is:

\[G_{L,\text{after Seal}}=(4/7)(0)+(3/7)(4/9)=\frac4{21}\approx0.190476.\]

The local Gini reduction is \(20/49-4/21\approx0.217687\). On the full Training dataset, the right pure branch contributes 0, so the three-leaf tree's total weighted impurity becomes \((7/12)(4/21)=1/9\approx0.111111\).

A second Hours threshold at 1.75 in the left node would produce a pure three-record group and a four-record group with two rejected and two accepted, giving \((3/7)(0)+(4/7)(0.5)=2/7\approx0.285714\). Seal (0.190476) is better. We can now verify it against every legal Hours threshold within the seven-record node, not just the illustrative 1.75 case:

11. The fitted tree: follow every path and check its leaves

Our depth-two tree has three leaves and can be written as a rule system:

  • If Hours > 3.75, predict Rejected (five Training examples, all rejected).
  • Otherwise, if Seal = 0, predict Accepted (four Training examples, all accepted).
  • Otherwise, predict Rejected (three Training examples, two rejected).

To verify it, walk the twelve Training rows through those three rules. T08–T12 all reach the first rejected leaf; T01, T03, T05, T07 reach the accepted leaf; T02, T04, T06 reach the final rejected leaf. Only T02 is misclassified. Thus Training Accuracy is \((12-1)/12=11/12\approx91.67\%\). The node sample counts \(5+4+3=12\) also verify that no observation was omitted or counted twice.

A leaf predicts the majority class under standard unweighted classification. This is not a magical formula: assigning the majority label minimizes the number of misclassified Training records within that leaf. If costs differ by class, or observations carry weights, a different decision rule may be more appropriate.

12. Leaf proportions versus real-world probabilities

A classification tree can report the observed fraction of class 1 in the leaf. For the three leaves above, those raw proportions are \(5/5=1\), \(0/4=0\), and \(2/3\approx0.6667\). For a new shipment with Hours = 3.0 and Seal = 1, the tree follows the left and compromised-seal branches and reports class 1 with raw leaf proportion \(2/3\).

But \(2/3\) from only three Training records is not automatically a calibrated 66.67% real-world spoilage risk. A tiny leaf can be highly uncertain; shifts in inspection practices can alter class prevalence; and the leaf partitions were deliberately chosen to fit Training labels. Calibration needs sufficiently representative held-out predictions and careful evaluation.

Consider another new shipment, Hours = 4.0 and Seal = 0. It reaches the right leaf (raw fraction 1) even though the seal is intact: the first split makes Seal irrelevant along that path. A tree does not evaluate every Feature for every prediction. This is an important distinction from a linear model, where each included Feature typically contributes to the score for every observation.

13. Training fit, tree depth and overfitting

Why not continue splitting until every Training record is classified correctly? At depth three, the mixed leaf T02, T04, T06 can be split again at Hours = 1.5: T02 (1.0 hours) is accepted, while T04 and T06 (2.0 and 3.0 hours) are rejected. Training Accuracy becomes 12/12 = 100%. Yet this additional decision is supported by only three observations. It may encode a real pattern, or it may memorize noise.

A tree is often controlled by Pre-Pruning hyperparameters: max_depth limits levels, min_samples_split sets the smallest node allowed to attempt a split, min_samples_leaf prevents children smaller than a chosen size, and max_leaf_nodes caps the number of leaves. These have distinct meanings. For example, min_samples_leaf=2 would forbid isolating T02 alone, even if the resulting Training Accuracy improved.

Depth is not itself a quality metric. A shallow tree may Underfit (miss genuine interactions); a deep tree may Overfit (capture accidental details). Compare candidate tree complexities on Validation, not on Training Accuracy. A tree with a perfect Training fit and poor future predictions is not successful merely because it memorized history.

For a leaf containing continuous Targets \(y_1,\dots,y_m\), the constant prediction \(c\) that minimizes Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) is their mean. Differentiate:

\[SSE(c)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(y_i-c)^2,\qquad \frac{dSSE}{dc}=-2\sum_{i=1}^{m}(y_i-c)=0.\]

Thus \(mc=\sum_i y_i\) and \(c=\bar y\). The second derivative \(2m>0\) confirms a minimum. Our six Targets are \(1,1,2,5,6,7\) kilograms; their root mean is \(22/6=11/3\approx3.6667\) kg. A Regression Tree asks which split most reduces the sum of squared prediction errors when each child uses its own mean.

17. Solve the Regression Tree split, from dataset to predictions

Try the midpoint at Hours = 3.5. The left Targets are \(1,1,2\), so \(\bar y_L=4/3\) kg. Each observation with value 1 has difference \(-1/3\) from the mean, contributing \(1/9\) kg²; the observation with value 2 differs by \(2/3\), contributing \(4/9\) kg². Thus:

\[SSE_L=\frac19+\frac19+\frac49=\frac23\;\text{kg}^2.\]

The right Targets are \(5,6,7\), so \(\bar y_R=6\) kg. Their deviations are \(-1,0,1\) kg; squaring produces \(1,0,1\) kg². Therefore:

\[SSE_R=1+0+1=2\;\text{kg}^2.\]

The combined \(SSE=2/3+2=8/3\approx2.6667\) kg². At the unsplit root, \(\sum y_i^2=116\) and \(\sum y_i=22\). Using the identity \(SSE=\sum y_i^2-(\sum y_i)^2/n\) gives:

\[SSE_{\text{root}}=116-\frac{22^2}{6}.\]
\[SSE_{\text{root}}=\frac{106}{3}\approx35.3333\;\text{kg}^2.\]

Now subtract the children’s error from the original error:

\[\Delta SSE=\frac{106}{3}-\frac83.\]
\[\Delta SSE=\frac{98}{3}\approx32.6667\;\text{kg}^2.\]

For MSE, divide each SSE by six observations. The root yields \(106/18\approx5.8889\) kg², whereas the split yields \(4/9\approx0.4444\) kg². Splitting has reduced Training MSE, although future performance still requires independent evaluation.

The fitted stump predicts 1.3333 kg for Hours \(\leq3.5\) and 6 kg for Hours \(>3.5\). A new shipment at Hours = 4.2 is assigned 6 kg; this is a leaf mean, not the observed discarding for that new shipment. 18. Why trees capture interactions—and where axis-aligned splits struggle

Our Classification tree uses a small logical interaction: Seal matters only if Hours \(\leq3.75\). In algebraic language, the effect of Seal is conditional on the region of the Feature Space. We did not explicitly create a product \(\text{Hours}\times\text{Seal}\); successive branches represent that dependence automatically.

But a conventional CART tree makes axis-aligned splits: one Feature and one threshold at a time. Boundaries therefore look like rectangles in two-dimensional Feature Space. A diagonal boundary may require several steps, and smooth changes may appear as abrupt jumps. A Regression Tree returns piecewise-constant predictions, not a smooth linear curve within a leaf.

Such properties are not inherently good or bad. They are inductive biases: a decision rule made from simple thresholds is easy to communicate, but it may be unstable or inefficient for highly smooth relationships. A future Random Forest or Gradient Boosting method can combine trees to improve predictive behavior without magically eliminating all limitations.

19. Post-Pruning: penalize extra leaves with a reproducible example

Pre-Pruning prevents growth; Post-Pruning starts from a grown tree and removes branches when their improvement is not worth added complexity. One common principle is Cost-Complexity Pruning: choose a subtree \(T\) minimizing \(R(T)+\alpha|T|\), where \(R(T)\) is a declared Training impurity measure, \(|T|\) the number of terminal leaves and \(\alpha\geq0\) a complexity penalty. A greater \(\alpha\) makes extra leaves more expensive.

Compare three nested versions of our deliberately depth-two tree. For the three-leaf tree, weighted Training Gini is \(1/9\approx0.111111\). If we remove its Seal split, two leaves remain, weighted Gini is \(5/21\approx0.238095\). If we remove every split, one leaf remains, Gini is \(35/72\approx0.486111\). Therefore:

\[J_3(\alpha)=1/9+3\alpha,\quad J_2(\alpha)=5/21+2\alpha,\quad J_1(\alpha)=35/72+\alpha.\]

The first break-even point follows from \(1/9+3\alpha=5/21+2\alpha\), giving \(\alpha=5/21-1/9=8/63\approx0.126984\). The second follows from \(5/21+2\alpha=35/72+\alpha\), giving \(\alpha=35/72-5/21=125/504\approx0.248016\).

At \(\alpha=0.05\), the three scores are approximately 0.2611, 0.3381 and 0.5361: three leaves win. At \(\alpha=0.18\), the scores are 0.6511, 0.5981 and 0.6661: the stump wins. At \(\alpha=0.30\), the scores are 1.0111, 0.8381 and 0.7861: the root-only tree wins.

Important distinction: these arithmetic examples compare subtrees of our specified depth-two tree. A fully expanded scikit-learn tree may have a different pruning path and additional leaves; do not assume its ccp_alpha thresholds equal these illustrative comparisons. Tune pruning strength on Validation or through leakage-safe Cross-Validation.

Computational cross-check: applying cost_complexity_pruning_path to a Training-fitted tree explicitly capped at max_depth=2 gives effective alphas approximately 0, 0.126984, 0.248016 and corresponding weighted leaf impurities 0.111111, 0.238095, 0.486111. These match our three nested subtrees. This equivalence applies to that capped model, not automatically to an unconstrained, fully grown tree.

20. Missing values, categories and the meaning of a split

Tree rules compare Feature values. For a numeric variable, candidate thresholds are derived from observed Training values; for a genuinely unordered categorical Feature such as packaging type, the allowed category groupings depend on the implementation. One-Hot Encoding may be needed for libraries that expect numeric arrays. Our Seal variable is already binary, so its split at 0.5 is natural and needs no extra Encoding.

A missing sensor reading is not automatically zero. Replacing a missing Hours value with zero could send it down the safest-looking branch even though it actually means unknown. Missingness may carry information about sensor failure, but any imputation or missing-indicator rule must be learned from Training (and separately inside Cross-Validation folds) and applied consistently to Validation, Test and future data.

Some tree implementations handle missing values or categorical Features natively; others require preprocessing. Do not assume all Decision Tree libraries have the same capabilities. Extremely high-cardinality identifiers such as Shipment ID or warehouse serial number are dangerous Features: a deep tree may learn one-off identities that do not generalize, and such identifiers can leak future outcomes when their meaning changes with time.

21. Class imbalance and unequal operational costs

A split minimizing Gini does not directly minimize the company's expected financial loss. In our depth-two tree, the raw class majority defines each leaf. But suppose a False Negative (accepting a spoiled shipment) has hypothetical cost USD 500, while a False Positive (inspecting a good shipment) costs USD 40. On Test, we observed one of each: total illustrative error cost is USD 500 + USD 40 = USD 540 over six shipments.

This comparison is meaningful only under those assumed costs and an appropriately chosen action. We must not call USD 540 real operational savings or infer that a model deployment would reduce spoilage. In the mixed Training leaf with empirical rejected fraction \(2/3\), predict Rejected if the expected cost of predicting rejected, \(40(1-p)\), is less than predicting accepted, \(500p\). This inequality becomes \(40<540p\) or \(p>40/540\approx0.0741\). That threshold is far below 0.5 because missing actual spoilage is assumed much more expensive.

However, raw leaf proportions may be poorly calibrated, and the threshold itself should be validated; a business cost calculation cannot repair biased labels or unreliable probabilities. Class weights and cost-sensitive learning may alter how splits and leaf decisions are chosen, but evaluation should still report confusion counts and meaningful class-specific metrics.

22. Feature Importance: useful diagnostic, not causal proof

A common tree importance score adds the weighted impurity reductions credited to each Feature across the fitted tree, then normalizes them to sum to one. On our three-leaf tree, the root Hours split reduces full-dataset Gini from \(35/72\) to \(5/21\), or approximately \(0.248016\). The next Seal split reduces it from \(5/21\) to \(1/9\), or approximately \(0.126984\). Total decrease is \(35/72-1/9=3/8=0.375\).

Normalized impurity importances are therefore Hours: \(0.248016/0.375\approx0.661376\) and Seal: \(0.126984/0.375\approx0.338624\). This quantifies the contribution to Training impurity reduction in this fitted tree, not how strongly either variable causes spoilage. Correlated Features can share or exchange importance; continuous variables and those with many potential splits can receive inflated impurity importance.

As a complementary check, Permutation Importance shuffles one Feature in Validation and observes the degradation in a suitable metric. Its interpretation is also limited: shuffling correlated variables can create implausible combinations, and the result depends on the Evaluation sample and metric. Never turn either importance score into a causal recommendation without separate evidence.

23. Stability, Bias–Variance and how trees lead to ensembles

A single deep tree can change dramatically after changing only one observation because the best threshold is selected from many candidates. This is a form of high variance. A very shallow tree has a stronger restriction on possible rules and can have higher Bias. As depth increases, Training fit almost always improves or stays equal; Validation error need not do so.

Bagging reduces sensitivity by fitting trees on bootstrap-resampled Training sets and averaging their predictions or votes. A Random Forest adds Feature-level randomness when choosing splits. Boosting builds a sequence of trees in which later steps focus on a remaining optimization signal. These methods deserve their own guides; here the essential bridge is that understanding one tree's splits, leaves and errors is a prerequisite for understanding ensembles.

For structured data where explanations matter, a well-pruned single tree can still be useful. For strong predictive performance, ensembles frequently outperform a single tree—but that is an empirical possibility, not a guaranteed law for every dataset.

One changed outcome can change the entire first question

We can measure tree instability on the same synthetic twelve-shipment dataset instead of assuming it. Imagine that T02, the shipment with Hours = 1.0 and Seal = 1, is relabeled from Accepted (0) to Rejected (1) after an inspection review. This is a hypothetical sensitivity test, not a claim that the original label was wrong. All Features, sample sizes, thresholds under consideration, and every other label remain unchanged.

Originally the root chooses Hours at 3.75 h because its weighted Gini \(5/21\approx0.238095\) is smaller than Seal's \(13/36\approx0.361111\). After changing T02, there are eight rejected and four accepted, so the new root Gini is \(1-(8/12)^2-(4/12)^2=4/9\approx0.444444\). Notice that the parent Gini actually decreases; nevertheless the best first question can still change.

Recompute Seal: the six Seal = 0 shipments still contain two rejected and four accepted, with Gini \(4/9\). All six Seal = 1 shipments are now rejected, with Gini 0. Its new weighted score is \((6/12)(4/9)+(6/12)(0)=2/9\approx0.222222\).

Recompute Hours: at 3.75 h the left seven now contain three rejected and four accepted, giving \(G_L=1-(3/7)^2-(4/7)^2=24/49\); the right five are still all rejected. Its score is \((7/12)(24/49)=2/7\approx0.285714\). Rechecking all eleven Hours midpoints confirms that 3.75 h is still the best Hours-only threshold. Yet \(2/9<2/7\), so the tree now chooses Seal at the root. These are alternative models trained on alternative labels; neither version may use Validation or Test to revise Training labels.

24. Reproduce the experiment with Python, without hidden data

The code below uses exactly the Training, Validation and Test records printed in this guide. It compares three depths on Validation, applies the predetermined simpler-on-ties rule, and evaluates Test once. It also fits the six-record Regression Tree used in the manual example. No scaler is needed for these simple tree thresholds: unlike Euclidean-distance methods, an increasing unit conversion preserves the ordering of a numeric Feature (though the printed threshold's units change).

import numpy as np
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, DecisionTreeRegressor, export_text
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, confusion_matrix

train_X = np.array([
    [0.5, 0], [1.0, 1], [1.5, 0], [2.0, 1],
    [2.5, 0], [3.0, 1], [3.5, 0], [4.0, 1],
    [4.5, 0], [5.0, 1], [5.5, 0], [6.0, 1]
])
train_y = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1])

val_X = np.array([
    [1.1, 0], [2.2, 1], [3.2, 1],
    [4.2, 0], [2.8, 0], [3.7, 1]
])
val_y = np.array([0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1])

test_X = np.array([
    [1.2, 1], [2.6, 0], [3.1, 1],
    [4.8, 0], [3.6, 0], [5.2, 1]
])
test_y = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1])

candidates = [1, 2, 3]
results = []
for depth in candidates:
    tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=depth, criterion="gini",
                                  random_state=7)
    tree.fit(train_X, train_y)
    score = accuracy_score(val_y, tree.predict(val_X))
    results.append((depth, score, tree))
    print("Depth", depth, "Validation", f"{score:.6f}")

# Accuracy descending, then simpler tree on ties.
selected_depth, _, model = sorted(results, key=lambda z: (-z[1], z[0]))[0]
print("Selected depth:", selected_depth)
print(export_text(model, feature_names=["hours", "seal"]))

pred = model.predict(test_X)
print("Test predictions:", pred.tolist())
print("Test accuracy:", accuracy_score(test_y, pred))
print("Confusion matrix [0, 1]:")
print(confusion_matrix(test_y, pred, labels=[0, 1]))

reg_X = np.array([[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]])
reg_y = np.array([1., 1., 2., 5., 6., 7.])
reg_tree = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=1, random_state=7)
reg_tree.fit(reg_X, reg_y)
print("Regression tree:")
print(export_text(reg_tree, feature_names=["hours"]))
print("New shipment at 4.2 hours:", reg_tree.predict([[4.2]])[0])

# Sensitivity analysis: alter one Training label only (T02).
modified_y = train_y.copy()
modified_y[1] = 1

# For binary labels, empirical Gini = 2 p(1-p).
def binary_gini(labels):
    p = np.mean(labels)
    return 2 * p * (1 - p)

def weighted_gini(feature_index, threshold, labels):
    left = train_X[:, feature_index] <= threshold
    return ((left.sum() * binary_gini(labels[left])
             + (~left).sum() * binary_gini(labels[~left])) / len(labels))

midpoints = (train_X[:-1, 0] + train_X[1:, 0]) / 2
best_hours = min((weighted_gini(0, t, modified_y), t) for t in midpoints)
seal_impurity = weighted_gini(1, 0.5, modified_y)
changed_model = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, criterion="gini",
                                       random_state=7).fit(train_X, modified_y)
print("T02 changed from 0 to 1 (Training only)")
print("Best Hours split: threshold", f"{best_hours[1]:.2f}",
      "weighted Gini", f"{best_hours[0]:.6f}")
print("Seal split weighted Gini:", f"{seal_impurity:.6f}")
print("Changed root feature:", ["hours", "seal"][changed_model.tree_.feature[0]])

# Cross-check pruning numbers for the original capped depth-2 model.
prune = model.cost_complexity_pruning_path(train_X, train_y)
print("Capped-tree effective alpha:", np.round(prune.ccp_alphas, 6).tolist())
print("Capped-tree leaf impurities:", np.round(prune.impurities, 6).tolist())

Notice that the program does not let Validation or Test alter the fitted tree; fit() sees Training only, whereas predict() applies its frozen questions. We print both the selected depth and the learned split thresholds so you can compare code to our hand derivation. On larger projects, use a carefully designed Cross-Validation strategy, immutable data splits and recorded package versions.

25. Common mistakes, with precise corrections

1. Assuming Gini is a spoilage probability

Gini summarizes mixture of class proportions in a node; predicted class proportions come from counts in leaves and require Calibration assessment.

2. Selecting splits using Validation labels

Splits must be learned from Training; Validation compares fitted candidate structures only.

3. Choosing a threshold by its purity without group sizes

Use weighted impurity. A one-record pure branch cannot be allowed to hide a badly mixed large branch.

4. Equating a perfect Training fit with generalization

A depth-three tree fits all twelve Training labels but does not beat depth two on the six Validation records.

5. Calling leaf proportion 1 a guaranteed 100% risk

Five of five Training records do not prove an event is certain in a new population.

6. Confusing max_depth and min_samples_leaf

The first caps path length; the second imposes minimum child sample size. They affect different aspects of complexity.

7. Using Test to break a Validation tie

Predefine a policy such as simpler-on-ties and keep Test unseen until the model has been selected.

8. Dividing by leaf count instead of record count

Weighted impurity divides by the parent sample size; every observation contributes according to its group.

9. Confusing Gini and Information Gain

Information Gain is a decrease in Entropy; Gini reduction uses a different impurity formula.

10. Reusing Classification majority vote for Regression

A Squared Error Regression Tree predicts the average continuous Target in each leaf.

11. Treating missing sensor values as zero

Unknown exposure is not necessarily safe exposure; specify and fit a missing-data strategy.

12. Interpreting Feature Importance as causality

Importance reflects how the fitted tree used observed Features; intervention effects require separate causal evidence.

13. Assuming pruning always improves Accuracy

Pruning exchanges Training fit for simplicity; the effect on held-out performance must be tested.

14. Reporting synthetic costs as observed benefits

Illustrative error costs illustrate decision tradeoffs, not real financial savings.

15. Ignoring repeated route or warehouse observations

Random splitting related records can exaggerate performance; use grouped or temporal evaluation when appropriate.

16. Treating all trees as the same implementation

Support for native categories, NaNs, tie-breaking and pruning differs by package and version.

The recurring theme is that Decision Trees are transparent only when we accurately explain the learned rules, their empirical basis and their uncertainty. A short if statement is easy to read; justifying why the statement was learned and whether it will generalize requires careful statistical reasoning.

26. Worked exercises: calculate, explain and transfer

1. Root class fraction — Calculate rejection proportion and parent Gini.

There are 7 positives and 5 negatives among 12, so \(p=7/12\). Thus \(G=1-(7/12)^2-(5/12)^2=70/144=35/72\approx0.486111\). The node is mixed, almost maximally for two classes.

2. Candidate 1.75 — Compute weighted Gini at 1.75 hours.

Left has three accepted and zero rejected: \(G_L=0\). Right has 7 rejected and 2 accepted among 9: \(G_R=2(7/9)(2/9)=28/81\). Weighted score \((3/12)0+(9/12)(28/81)=7/27\approx0.259259\). It is worse than 3.75.

3. Why use midpoint? — Explain why 3.75 represents thresholds between 3.5 and 4.0 in Training.

No Training value lies strictly between 3.5 and 4.0, so any threshold within that open interval makes the same partition of Training rows. The midpoint 3.75 is a reproducible representative; new values near it may be routed differently if a different representative were chosen.

4. Root Feature choice — Compare the Hours and Seal Gini scores.

Hours = 3.75 scores \(5/21\approx0.238095\); Seal scores \(13/36\approx0.361111\). Lower weighted impurity is preferred, therefore Hours is the root question. The difference is approximately \(0.123016\).

5. Second split — Reconstruct the left-node Seal split.

Four left-node cases with Seal = 0 are accepted, giving Gini 0; three with Seal = 1 have labels 0,1,1, giving \(4/9\). Local weighted Gini is \((4/7)0+(3/7)(4/9)=4/21\approx0.190476\). The unsplit left node was \(20/49\approx0.408163\), so this improves homogeneity.

6. New observation — Predict Hours = 2.6, Seal = 1.

Because \(2.6\leq3.75\), go left; because Seal = 1, go to the mixed leaf. Predict Rejected (majority 2 of 3). The raw leaf rejection proportion is \(2/3\), not a calibrated population probability.

7. Training confusion — Rebuild the Training Confusion Matrix.

The model predicts positive for T02,T04,T06,T08–T12: eight predicted positives, of which seven truly positive. So \(TP=7,FP=1\). It predicts negative for T01,T03,T05,T07, all truly negative: \(TN=4,FN=0\). Accuracy \((7+4)/12=11/12\).

8. Entropy — Calculate Information Gain for the root Hours split.

Root Entropy is about 0.979869 bits. Left Entropy is 0.863121 bits, and right Entropy zero. Weighted child Entropy \((7/12)(0.863121)=0.503487\) bits; gain \(0.979869-0.503487=0.476382\) bits.

9. Pruning at 0.18 — Compute the three subtree penalties at alpha = 0.18.

Three leaves: \(1/9+3(0.18)=0.651111\); two leaves: \(5/21+2(0.18)=0.598095\); one leaf: \(35/72+0.18=0.666111\). The two-leaf subtree wins among these three candidates.

10. Regression leaf — Derive the best constant for targets 1,1,2.

The SSE-minimizing constant is their mean \((1+1+2)/3=4/3\) kg, found by setting derivative \(-2\sum(y_i-c)=0\). Residual squares are \(1/9,1/9,4/9\) kg², summing to \(2/3\) kg².

11. Regression improvement — Compare root SSE and split SSE.

At the root, \(SSE=116-22^2/6=106/3\) kg². With threshold 3.5, the children yield \(2/3+2=8/3\) kg². Reduction is \((106-8)/3=98/3\) kg². Divide by six to get MSE reduction \(49/9\approx5.4444\) kg².

12. Validation choice — Why select depth 2 rather than depth 3?

Both candidates correctly classify five of six Validation cases, so their observed Accuracy ties. Under a preregistered simpler-on-ties rule, choose depth 2. Looking at Test to favor depth 3 after the fact would contaminate model selection.

13. Test measures — Recalculate Precision, Recall and Accuracy.

On Test, \(TP=3,TN=1,FP=1,FN=1\). Precision \(=3/(3+1)=0.75\); Recall \(=3/(3+1)=0.75\); Accuracy \(=(3+1)/6=2/3\). Six observations cannot justify a precise deployment claim.

14. Importance versus causation — Explain why normalized impurity importance is not causal effect.

Hours receives about 0.661 and Seal about 0.339 of impurity reduction in this fitted tree. Those shares depend on selected splits and correlated Features. They do not prove that intervening on Hours would reduce spoilage by 66.1% or any other fraction.

15. Cost-sensitive choice — With FP cost $40 and FN cost $500, derive the rejection decision threshold.

Expected cost of predicting Rejected is \(40(1-p)\); of predicting Accepted it is \(500p\). Prefer Rejected if \(40(1-p)<500p\), hence \(40<540p\) and \(p>40/540\approx0.07407\). This requires meaningful, calibrated probabilities to apply reliably.

16. Transfer scenario — What changes if shipment IDs are used as numerical Features?

A high-cardinality ID may allow splits that memorize the original records and exploit accidental ordering or future information. Remove nonpredictive identifiers, use appropriately time/group-based evaluation, and test the intended generalization scenario.

17. Sensitivity test — Can one corrected Training label reverse the root Feature?

Change only T02 from Accepted (0) to Rejected (1). Now eight of twelve observations are rejected, with root Gini \(1-(8/12)^2-(4/12)^2=4/9\). For Seal = 0, the two rejected and four accepted give Gini \(4/9\); for Seal = 1, all six are rejected and Gini is zero. The weighted Seal score is \((6/12)(4/9)=2/9\approx0.222222\). At the original Hours threshold 3.75, the seven left records now contain three rejected and four accepted, giving \(24/49\); the right five remain pure. Weighted Hours Gini is \((7/12)(24/49)=2/7\approx0.285714\). An exhaustive check of Hours thresholds leaves 3.75 as its best candidate, but Seal now scores lower. The root changes from Hours to Seal. This is a sensitivity demonstration on synthetic Training labels, not a new held-out performance result.

These questions deliberately cover three kinds of evidence of learning: conceptual understanding (explaining), mathematical competence (recalculating) and practical transfer (diagnosing a new situation). The worked answers expose intermediate operations rather than simply announcing a number.

27. Glossary, learning connections and final synthesis

Essential vocabulary
ConceptExplanation
RootInitial node containing all Training observations.
SplitA question partitioning the current node into children.
BranchOne possible answer to a split question.
LeafTerminal node assigning a class or numeric prediction.
DepthNumber of edges from the root to a node.
Gini ImpurityClass mixing measure: one minus the sum of squared class proportions
EntropyClass uncertainty measured by minus sum of p times log base 2 of p
Information GainReduction in weighted Entropy after a split.
Greedy AlgorithmMakes the best locally available decision at each step.
Regression TreeUses leaf means under Squared Error loss.
Pre-PruningLimits growth with constraints such as maximum depth.
Post-PruningRemoves already grown branches based on a complexity tradeoff.
ValidationHeld-out data used for model configuration decisions.

OverfittingLearning idiosyncrasies of Training that do not generalize.

We began with twelve synthetic inspection records and learned a decision rule from first principles: choose the strongest impurity-reducing question, repeat inside a nonpure child, stop at a stated complexity, interpret the leaves, and evaluate independently. We also derived the Regression Tree's leaf means and showed how pruning penalizes unnecessary structure. A tree is powerful because its rules are explicit; those rules are not automatically causal, stable, calibrated or ready for deployment.
