1. A prediction based on resemblance: what does “neighbor” mean?

A bicycle-share operator must decide which docking stations may need additional bicycles before the afternoon commute. A newly installed station has no long operating history, but established stations do. Rather than fitting a single straight line or a global decision boundary, the operator can ask: which established stations resemble this one, and what happened at those stations? This is the central idea of k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN).

A neighbor is not necessarily a station located nearby on a city map. It is an observation with similar Feature values under a specified distance measure. A station three minutes from a transit stop and receiving about 300 incoming commuters per day may be similar, for prediction purposes, to stations with comparable characteristics even when they lie in different neighborhoods. The choice of Features, measurement units and distance function determines what “similar” means; the algorithm does not discover this definition for us.

k-NN is a Supervised Learning method: each Training observation includes Features and a known Target. At prediction time we find the nearest labeled observations, select the closest \(k\), and aggregate their Targets. For Classification, aggregation is commonly a vote; for Regression, it is commonly an average. It is a lazy or instance-based method in the sense that it retains the Training examples rather than estimating a small global vector of coefficients. “Little fitting” does not mean “no learning”: selecting a distance, preprocessing method and \(k\) is a meaningful learning design.

The examples below are deliberately synthetic. Their purpose is to make every calculation auditable; neither their small-sample Accuracy nor their business labels should be interpreted as real transport-system performance.

Learning goals. By the end, you should be able to calculate k-NN predictions by hand; explain Euclidean, Manhattan and weighted distances; compare Classification and Regression; show how units and Scaling affect neighbors; choose \(k\) using Validation rather than Test; and identify the main limitations of similarity-based learning.

2. Define the task, the Features, and what we are allowed to know

We observe two explanatory variables for each bicycle docking station at an agreed decision time, before the afternoon rush. \(x_1\) is the walking time, in minutes, from the station to the nearest rapid-transit entrance. \(x_2\) is the estimated number of arriving commuters, measured in hundreds of people per day, using data already available by prediction time. Thus \(x_2=3\) means approximately 300 arrivals, not three people.

Our Classification Target, \(y\), equals 1 if the station subsequently needed an unscheduled afternoon bicycle replenishment and 0 otherwise. The Regression Target, \(r\), records a continuous quantity: the number of bicycles rented during a fixed later operating window. These Targets are known for historical stations only after the relevant period has ended. A new station's future replenishment outcome or future rentals must never appear among its input Features: that would be Data Leakage.

The two Targets answer different questions. \(\widehat y=1\) means “predict a replenishment event”; \(\widehat r=25\) means “predict roughly 25 rentals.” Neither result proves that moving a station closer to transit would cause demand to change. For this educational case, the observations are independent station-period examples, and we ignore repeated-station correlations; a real project must assess location groups and time-aware splitting.

Decision before calculation: Which outcome is available at prediction time? Only historical measurements and commuter forecasts computed without future information. This timing restriction belongs to the problem definition, not merely to the last section about deployment.

Consider a new station Q, with \((x_1,x_2)=(3,3)\). We do not know its class or rental count. Those are the values to predict. This tiny dataset intentionally includes borderline observations. A close neighbor may have a different outcome from another close neighbor; this is realistic in spirit because not all demand determinants are measured. A useful algorithm must accommodate noisy, incomplete descriptions rather than pretend that two Features perfectly determine a city's bicycle demand.

4. Distance as a mathematical definition of resemblance

The most common k-NN distance for continuous Features is Euclidean Distance: the straight-line distance between two points in Feature Space. With two variables,

\[d_2(\mathbf x,\mathbf q)=\sqrt{(x_1-q_1)^2+(x_2-q_2)^2}.\]

This comes from the Pythagorean Theorem: differences along the first and second feature axes form perpendicular sides of a right triangle. The squared differences make negative and positive deviations contribute equally; the square root restores the units of the coordinate scale, provided those units are comparable. For mixed units, an unscaled Euclidean result has no simple physical unit; that is precisely why we must study Scaling later.

For station A relative to Q: \(d_A=\sqrt{(3-3)^2+(2-3)^2}=\sqrt{0+1}=1\). For B: \(d_B=\sqrt{(4-3)^2+(2-3)^2}=\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt2\approx1.4142\). Notice that we are computing distance between two Feature Vectors, not between their Targets.

For C, the computation is \(\sqrt{(2-3)^2+(4-3)^2}=\sqrt2\). Therefore B and C are tied at the same Euclidean distance. Ties in distance do not necessarily cause trouble when all tied neighbors have the same class; here both are class 0. When a tie straddles the \(k\)-th neighbor and tied observations disagree, the implementation needs a stated deterministic policy. We will not silently resolve ties in favor of the answer we want.

6. Classification: calculate the vote for k = 1, 3, and 5

The hyperparameter \(k\) is the number of neighbors considered; it is not learned by taking derivatives of a Loss Function. For a binary Classifier with uniform votes and an odd \(k\), the prediction is the majority class among the selected neighbors. For our new station Q:

With \(k=1\), only A votes. Its Target is 1, so \(\widehat y_{k=1}(Q)=1\). With \(k=3\), the neighbors are A, B and C, whose labels are \((1,0,0)\). Count class 1: one vote; class 0: two votes. Thus \(\widehat y_{k=3}(Q)=0\). With \(k=5\), include D and E: labels \((1,0,0,1,1)\) give three votes for class 1 and two for class 0, so \(\widehat y_{k=5}(Q)=1\).

Choice Selected neighbors Class-1 votes Class-0 votes Prediction
\(k=1\) A 1 0 1 — replenishment
\(k=3\) A, B, C 1 2 0 — no replenishment
\(k=5\) A, B, C, D, E 3 2 1 — replenishment

The predictions alternate because the neighborhood expands into regions with different local compositions. None of the three is automatically “the truth.” We do not know Q's future outcome. This teaches an important limitation: k-NN is a rule for combining observed outcomes, not a guarantee that spatially near records are causally identical.

7. What does the fraction of neighbor votes mean?

For uniform-weight binary k-NN, the proportion of positive neighbor labels is often reported as a class score, or by software as an estimated class probability. At \(k=3\), \(\widehat p_{1}(Q)=1/3\approx0.3333\); at \(k=5\), \(\widehat p_1(Q)=3/5=0.60\). Both derive from the same observations but different neighborhood definitions.

These are local empirical fractions. They are not automatically calibrated probabilities: seeing a 0.60 score does not establish that exactly 60% of such future stations will need replenishment. Calibration would require enough held-out observations to examine how often events actually occur among predictions around 0.60. With nine historical records, a probability claim would be particularly uncertain.

For Multiclass Classification, the same procedure votes among three or more named labels, such as low, moderate and high demand. A tie is possible even for odd \(k\) when there are more than two classes: three neighbors might vote A, B and C. Choosing the smallest numerical class label as a tiebreaker is deterministic but potentially arbitrary. We should document the policy or use a more informative cost-aware decision rule.

8. Regression: average the neighbors' numerical Targets

We can use the same Features for a different question: how many bicycles will be rented in the later window? Here the Target \(r\) is a count, but we treat its conditional expected value as a Regression prediction. k-NN Regression uses a local average instead of a class vote:

\[\widehat r_k(\mathbf q)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in N_k(\mathbf q)}r_i.\]

The set \(N_k(\mathbf q)\) consists of the indices of the \(k\) nearest Training observations. With \(k=3\), A, B and C have rentals 32, 20 and 18. The sum is \(32+20+18=70\), so \(\widehat r_3(Q)=70/3\approx23.3333\) bicycles. With \(k=1\), the prediction is 32. With \(k=5\), the sum is \(32+20+18+28+34=132\), giving \(132/5=26.4\).

Notice the distinction between an expected quantity and a valid operational integer. We could plan for 23 or 24 bicycles after defining an operational rounding policy; the Regression model itself naturally emits fractional predictions. Rounding before evaluation changes MAE and should not be done without a reason. And the prediction cannot extrapolate beyond the smallest and largest Training Targets when using a nonnegative weighted average: this is a structural limitation of standard k-NN Regression.

9. Distance-weighted k-NN: nearer observations can contribute more

Uniform voting gives A at distance 1 exactly the same influence as C at distance 1.4142. Sometimes we prefer nearby examples to carry more weight. A common choice is inverse-distance weighting, \(w_i=1/d_i\), with a policy for zero distances. We normalize the weights so they sum to one:

\[\widehat r_{\mathrm w}(Q)=\frac{\sum_{i\in N_k(Q)}w_i r_i}{\sum_{i\in N_k(Q)}w_i},\qquad w_i=\frac1{d_i}.\]

For \(k=3\), the distances are \(1,\sqrt2,\sqrt2\), so the weights are \(1,1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2\approx1,0.7071,0.7071\). Their total is \(2.4142\). The weighted rental numerator is \(1(32)+0.7071(20)+0.7071(18)\approx58.8701\). Dividing gives \(\widehat r_{\mathrm w,3}(Q)\approx24.385\) bicycles, above the uniform average 23.3333 because A has the highest Target and receives extra influence.

For Classification with \(k=3\), the class-1 weight is 1 and class-0 weight is \(0.7071+0.7071=1.4142\); class 0 still wins. If a query matches a Training point exactly, \(1/d\) would divide by zero. A practical convention is to use only zero-distance matches, averaging their labels/Targets rather than returning infinity. Weighting may help local responsiveness, but it is not inherently superior; compare options on Validation.

10. Euclidean, Manhattan and Minkowski: different geometry, different neighbors

Manhattan Distance (L1) adds absolute coordinate differences, as if traveling along a rectangular street grid: \(d_1(\mathbf x,\mathbf q)=|x_1-q_1|+|x_2-q_2|\). For E relative to Q: \(|5-3|+|4-3|=2+1=3\); Euclidean Distance for E is \(\sqrt5\approx2.2361\). For G, Manhattan gives \(|6-3|+|1-3|=3+2=5\), while Euclidean gives \(\sqrt{13}\approx3.6056\).

The Minkowski Distance unifies these: \(d_p(\mathbf x,\mathbf q)=(\sum_j |x_j-q_j|^p)^{1/p}\). When \(p=1\), it is Manhattan; when \(p=2\), it is Euclidean. The choice expresses which geometric differences matter and can change rankings; selecting \(p\) should be based on the problem and Validation, not on the nicest-looking chart.

A generalization often called Mahalanobis Distance additionally accounts for variable covariance: \(\sqrt{(x-q)^\top S^{-1}(x-q)}\) when the covariance estimate \(S\) is invertible. It can be useful with correlated Features, but estimation becomes unstable with too few samples or many dimensions. We mention it to show the broader design choice; we do not pretend nine observations are sufficient to estimate a reliable high-dimensional covariance model.

11. Feature units can overturn a prediction: a complete Scaling counterexample

Our \(x_2\) is expressed in hundreds of commuters. What if we record exactly the same information in individual commuters instead? Q changes from \((3,3)\) to \((3,300)\), A becomes \((3,200)\), and D becomes \((1,300)\). A's raw Euclidean distance becomes 100, while D's becomes only 2. The physically identical dataset now ranks D ahead of A! The full three nearest become D, A and B (breaking no class conflict at the boundary here), with labels \((1,1,0)\). The raw-unit \(k=3\) prediction is therefore class 1, whereas the hundreds-scale \(k=3\) prediction was class 0.

A model cannot reasonably change its substantive decision just because an analyst replaced “hundreds of riders” by “individual riders.” This is why distance-based algorithms are sensitive to units. Conversion from persons to hundreds is a fixed rule (divide by 100), while StandardScaler must learn the Training mean and standard deviation.

For our nine Training observations, \(\mu_1=30/9=3.3333\) minutes and \(\mu_2=34/9=3.7778\) hundreds/day. Using the population-form divisor \(n=9\), as in scikit-learn's StandardScaler, \(\sigma_1\approx2.2111\) and \(\sigma_2\approx1.9876\). Transform Q one coordinate at a time, so every calculation remains visible:

\[z_1(Q)=\frac{3-3.3333}{2.2111}\approx-0.1508.\]
\[z_2(Q)=\frac{3-3.7778}{1.9876}\approx-0.3913.\]

Its standardized Feature Vector is therefore approximately \(\mathbf z(Q)=(-0.1508,-0.3913)\). Using the same Training-fitted parameters for the historical stations, distances to A, B and C are about 0.5031, 0.6765 and 0.6765. The three votes remain \((1,0,0)\), so class 0 wins again for \(k=3\).

Critical chronology: calculate \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) using Training only; then use these frozen values to transform Validation, Test and future observations. Recalculating the means with Validation may change neighbors and contaminates the model-selection process even when Validation labels are not used.

12. Missing values, categories and meaningful Features

Euclidean Distance requires a numerical vector with values for every required Feature. If commuter arrivals are missing, we cannot silently treat the missing value as zero—zero arrivals and unknown arrivals have different meanings. A typical Pipeline first fits an Imputer on Training, then transforms new observations using its stored statistics. We must fit that Imputer inside each Training fold during Cross-Validation.

A station may also have a categorical Feature, such as residential, university or business district. Assigning codes 0, 1 and 2 and inserting them into Euclidean Distance assumes ordered numerical spacing that may not exist. One-Hot Encoding or a purpose-built mixed-type distance is more defensible, but even One-Hot Encoding imposes a relative penalty that should be tested against numerical Features.

Irrelevant Features can also alter which observations are nearest. If we add a random station ID or a future outcome proxy, k-NN may select neighbors for reasons unrelated to underlying demand. Good Feature Engineering includes asking whether a coordinate carries predictive information, is available on time, and reflects a sensible meaning of similarity.

13. Decision boundaries: why k changes the map

A Decision Boundary separates regions of Feature Space that receive different predicted classes. With \(k=1\), each Training observation defines a nearest-neighbor region, producing boundaries related to a Voronoi diagram; the prediction can change suddenly on crossing a region border. Increasing \(k\) averages over more observations and often smooths the boundary, though unusual local class patterns can still produce irregular shapes.

The figure shows the exact nine synthetic observations and grid predictions for different values of \(k\), not a generic decoration. Boundaries depend on the coordinate scales and distance definition. They are properties of the trained rule, not walls in the real city. A smooth boundary may be easier to generalize, but smoothing too aggressively can erase a genuine small high-risk neighborhood.

14. Bias, variance and the choice of k

The extreme \(k=1\) allows very local predictions. It can follow fine structure, but a mislabeled or unusual nearest observation can change the answer completely: low smoothing and potentially high variance. A very large \(k\) pools observations from unlike parts of Feature Space: predictions become stable but may overlook important local structure, producing higher bias. These descriptions are tendencies, not universal mathematical guarantees for every dataset.

For our nine Training stations, \(k=9\) ignores all distance rankings: five of nine labels are positive, so every query gets class 1. That is little more than a global majority-class rule. Conversely, \(k=1\) predicts each stored Training observation's own label when no contradictory identical Feature Vectors exist; a Training Accuracy of 100% in that situation is not evidence of future generalization.

The correct value of \(k\) is a Hyperparameter selected using out-of-sample evidence and a relevant metric. A heuristic such as \(\sqrt{n}\) may help choose an initial search grid but does not replace Validation. For rare replenishment events, Precision and Recall may matter more than Accuracy, and a cost-sensitive policy can be more useful than a simple vote.

For \(k=1\), correct predictions occur at V2, V3 and V4: 3 of 5, hence \(\mathrm{Accuracy}=3/5=0.60\). For \(k=3\), all five are correct: \(5/5=1.00\). We deliberately keep Test untouched until after the choice is finalized. In larger projects, Cross-Validation compares choices more reliably, and if we repeatedly redesign the candidate grid after seeing Validation results, we risk overfitting to Validation itself.

We can explain the local computation exactly, but we cannot confidently identify the real-world cause of the error without more station information. Do not change \(k\) after inspecting T1 and then report the same Test as unbiased.

17. Baselines, business costs and uncertainty in deployment

Five of nine Training labels are positive. A majority-class Baseline would predict replenishment for every new station. On the three Test observations above it would be correct for T1 and T3 and wrong for T2: again 2/3 Accuracy. That ties k-NN on this tiny Test set, although their error types differ. A model does not earn a real-world deployment solely because it uses a sophisticated distance calculation.

Suppose an unnecessary replenishment trip costs $20 and a missed required trip incurs an illustrative operational penalty of $120. Our k-NN Test makes one False Negative and no False Positives: hypothetical error cost \(1(120)+0(20)=\$120\). The all-positive Baseline makes one False Positive and no False Negatives: cost \(1(20)+0(120)=\$20\). Under these invented costs and on this tiny Test sample, the Baseline has lower cost despite equal Accuracy. Choosing a deployed strategy requires realistic cost estimates, sufficient evaluation data and operational constraints—not selecting the most flattering metric after the fact.

An operational system should monitor changing commuter patterns, newly opened transit lines, station saturation and whether the Training archive still represents deployment stations. Record both the decision and the available Features at the prediction time. Observed outcomes may arrive later and must be joined without accidentally leaking future information into retraining or evaluation.

18. The Curse of Dimensionality: why “nearest” can become uninformative

In two dimensions we can draw neighborhoods. With many Features, points can become sparse. Imagine each standardized coordinate lies in a unit interval and, for a simple geometric thought experiment, points are spread uniformly and independently. A hypercube covering 20% of the range along each coordinate occupies a volume fraction \(0.2^d\) in \(d\) dimensions. For \(d=2\), this is \(0.2^2=0.04\), or 4%; for \(d=10\), it is \(0.2^{10}=0.0000001024\), approximately one ten-millionth.

This is not a universal formula for “probability of finding a neighbor” in an arbitrary dataset—the uniformity and independence assumptions are essential. It does demonstrate how the amount of data needed to populate a small local region can grow quickly with dimension. In higher dimensions, even the nearest observations may be far apart, and many distances may become relatively similar. Irrelevant and noisy Features can worsen the problem.

Possible remedies are meaningful Feature Selection, Dimensionality Reduction (for example, PCA), better domain-informed distances and collecting more representative data. None is universally best. Dimensionality Reduction and feature selection must be learned inside Training folds when tuning models, because otherwise Validation can influence which Features are kept.

19. Computational cost and why k-NN is called a lazy learner

A simple brute-force prediction computes the distance from one query to all \(n\) stored Training observations. With \(d\) Features, this typically requires work on the order of \(O(nd)\), plus selecting or sorting neighbors. Storing all \(n\) Training vectors requires \(O(nd)\) Feature storage (and the Targets). Training can be cheap when it mainly stores the data, but prediction can be expensive at scale.

Indexing structures such as KDTree and BallTree may accelerate search in suitable low- or moderate-dimensional settings; their gains depend on geometry, dimension and the metric. Approximate Nearest Neighbor systems deliberately trade some exactness for speed on very large datasets. Brute force can still be competitive in high dimensions or when implemented efficiently with vectorized operations.

The term nonparametric means the model's effective complexity is not summarized by a fixed small set of coefficients like \(b_0,b_1\) in Linear Regression. It does not mean there are no settings: \(k\), distance metric, weighting rule, feature representation and preprocessing are real design choices.

20. Reproduce the calculations in Python and avoid leakage

The short example below uses the complete nine-row Training dataset and five-row Validation dataset shown earlier. It constructs a Pipeline so StandardScaler is fitted only on Training when .fit() is called. Then the same Scaler transforms Validation in .predict(); no refitting occurs. The candidate search is performed on Validation for teaching purposes; in a larger sample, prefer Cross-Validation with the Pipeline inside each fold.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier, KNeighborsRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score

X_train = np.array([[3,2],[4,2],[2,4],[1,3],[5,4],[0,4],[6,1],[2,7],[7,7]])
y_train = np.array([1,0,0,1,1,0,0,1,1])
rentals = np.array([32,20,18,28,34,13,11,36,39])
X_val = np.array([[3,3],[1,2],[5,2],[6,5],[6,3]])
y_val = np.array([0,1,0,1,0])

for k in [1,3,5]:
    model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k))
    model.fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(k, model.predict(X_val).tolist(), accuracy_score(y_val, model.predict(X_val)))

reg = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=3))
reg.fit(X_train, rentals)
print('rentals at Q:', reg.predict([[3,3]])[0])

Expected validation predictions are, for \(k=1\), [1,1,0,1,1] and Accuracy 0.60; for \(k=3\), [0,1,0,1,0] and 1.00; for \(k=5\), [1,0,0,0,0] and 0.40. The Regression output is approximately 23.3333. Since two neighbors at the third-neighbor boundary have the same label in the illustrated query, the manual conclusion is stable for that case. In other problems, equality of distances at the cut-off requires additional care.

21. When k-NN is useful, when it struggles, and what it cannot prove

k-NN can be a valuable Baseline when a local similarity principle is credible, the feature representation is meaningful and we have enough comparable examples. It is easy to explain a particular prediction by listing neighbors, distances and votes; yet that is not a causal explanation. The approach is sensitive to Scaling, irrelevant dimensions, mislabeled examples, missing observations and the historical sample's coverage.

It also raises privacy and security considerations: nearest examples may contain sensitive details if explanations expose entire stored records. Production systems should use permissible Features, restrict access to historical data and summarize neighbor contributions without unnecessarily identifying individuals. In a geographic problem, nearby observations can also be correlated; group- or time-aware validation is often more defensible than a random split.

A practical modeling review should compare k-NN with a simple majority or mean Baseline and with other appropriate methods. Ask whether the method is accurate enough, computationally affordable, stable under plausible changes and aligned with operational costs. A colorful Decision Boundary is useful for teaching but is not itself evidence of deployability.

22. Common mistakes: how to recognize and correct them

1. Calling geographical proximity the same thing as feature similarity. A station close on a map can have very different commuter traffic. Define and justify the Feature Space explicitly.

2. Reusing raw Euclidean Distance on incomparable units. The individual-commuters counterexample changed the predicted class without changing the actual stations. Standardize on Training, and explain the chosen distance.

3. Re-fitting StandardScaler on Validation or Test. That changes the reference distribution using held-out information. Fit only on Training (or each Training fold); then transform new data with frozen settings.

4. Choosing \(k\) after inspecting Test. Tuning on Test turns the purported final assessment into another Validation set. Lock \(k\) before Test evaluation.

5. Assuming \(k=1\) is best because Training Accuracy is perfect. Every nonduplicate Training point may be its own nearest neighbor. That is an in-sample artifact, not evidence of generalization.

6. Reporting vote fractions as calibrated probabilities. The fraction 3/5 describes votes under \(k=5\). Calibration needs independent empirical assessment, not a label attached to the fraction.

7. Ignoring tied distances or votes. An even \(k\) can produce a two-class tie, and Multiclass ties can arise with odd \(k\). Document deterministic resolution or a policy of abstaining.

8. Treating missing as zero. A missing commuter count may indicate a faulty sensor, not absence of commuters. Use appropriate Training-fitted preprocessing and consider a Missingness Indicator if justified.

9. Numerically coding nominal categories and assuming ordered gaps. Business district = 2 is not necessarily “twice as far” from residential = 0 as university = 1. Choose an encoding or mixed-data distance thoughtfully.

10. Assuming more Features always improve predictions. Irrelevant coordinates can dominate distance and aggravate high-dimensional sparsity. Validate Feature Engineering and selection.

11. Confusing Regression averaging with Classification voting. A rental forecast of 23.33 is a quantitative estimate; a class score of 1/3 represents a local fraction of labels.

12. Claiming k-NN identifies causes. Neighbor similarity supports analogical prediction, not interventions or causal claims about transit proximity.

13. Forgetting the time and entity structure. Nearby records from the same station and week can leak information across random splits; use Group- or Time-Aware Validation when required.

14. Comparing only Accuracy when costs differ. The Test example tied on Accuracy while the hypothetical intervention costs diverged. Define metrics before selecting the model.

15. Assuming a large \(k\) always makes predictions better. With \(k=9\), the model predicts the Training majority class everywhere. Smoothing can erase local structure.

23. Transfer case: customer-service priority changes when distance is scaled

We now transfer the mathematical idea to a new industry, instead of repeating the bicycle data. A customer-support team wants to predict whether a newly arrived request should receive a priority review (class 1) or standard routing (class 0). Its two available Features are the number of minutes the request has already waited and the number of tickets the same account generated in the previous 30 days. Labels are historical expert-routing decisions, recorded after those requests were reviewed. This is a synthetic educational dataset, not a validated or recommended customer-service policy.

Crucial timing: the previous-30-day count must be computed from information already available when the new request is triaged. A future resolved-priority label or activity after triage cannot be an input Feature. As in Guide 6, we also need to keep repeat accounts grouped in a real evaluation; this tiny example assumes each row comes from a different account.

Why raw Euclidean Distance is misleading

Suppose we naively apply \(d(Q,i)=\sqrt{(w_i-8)^2+(t_i-400)^2}\), where \(w\) is minutes and \(t\) is a count of tickets. This combines incompatible numeric units; it is not a meaningful physical distance. It is deliberately the wrong design to demonstrate why Feature Scaling matters.

\[d(Q,B)=\sqrt{(3-8)^2+(405-400)^2}=\sqrt{25+25}=\sqrt{50}=7.0711.\]
\[d(Q,A)=\sqrt{(2-8)^2+(395-400)^2}=\sqrt{36+25}=\sqrt{61}=7.8102.\]
\[d(Q,C)=\sqrt{(1-8)^2+(390-400)^2}=\sqrt{49+100}=\sqrt{149}=12.2066.\]

These are the nearest three records without Scaling: B, A, C, with labels (0, 0, 0). Thus, for \(k=3\), the raw prediction is class 0. Notice how differences of 5–10 prior tickets dominate differences of several minutes in the chosen numeric coordinates. Other measurement units would change that ranking.

Learn Min–Max Scaling from Training alone

Min–Max Scaling maps Training minima to 0 and maxima to 1 for each Feature: \(z_j=(x_j-\min_{\mathrm{train}}x_j)/(\max_{\mathrm{train}}x_j-\min_{\mathrm{train}}x_j)\). This defines a dimensionless numerical geometry. For the eight Training rows, waiting time ranges from 1 to 10 minutes, and previous tickets from 200 to 750. Hence:

\[z_{\mathrm{wait}}=\frac{w-1}{9},\qquad z_{\mathrm{tickets}}=\frac{t-200}{550}.\]

For Q, \(z_Q=((8-1)/9,(400-200)/550)=(7/9,4/11)\approx(0.7778,0.3636)\). Take Training ticket D = (9,500), which has class 1:

\[d_{\mathrm{scaled}}(Q,D)=\sqrt{\left(\frac{9-8}{9}\right)^2+\left(\frac{500-400}{550}\right)^2}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{81}+\frac{4}{121}}\approx0.2131.\]

Likewise, \(d(Q,E)=120/550=0.2182\) and \(d(Q,F)=\sqrt{(2/9)^2+(80/550)^2}=0.2656\). The three nearest scaled neighbors are D, E, F, whose labels are (1, 1, 1). The prediction therefore changes to class 1, even though neither the records nor their labels changed.

Implement the algorithm without a k-NN library

The following Python code implements the Euclidean distance, ranking and vote directly, then independently checks all six candidate configurations against scikit-learn. The MinMaxScaler is fitted on Training only. We use stable sorting for reproducibility and odd \(k\) to avoid majority-vote ties; in production, explicitly specify what happens when several observations tie exactly at the k-th distance.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.preprocessing import MinMaxScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

# Historical features available at ticket triage: wait (minutes), earlier tickets (30 days).
train = np.array([[2,395],[3,405],[1,390],[9,500],
                  [8,520],[10,480],[8,200],[4,750]], dtype=float)
known_priority = np.array([0,0,0,1,1,1,1,0])
validation = np.array([[9,430],[2,420],[8,530],
                       [3,590],[5,250],[6,370]], dtype=float)
validation_labels = np.array([1,0,1,0,0,1])
new_ticket = np.array([[8,400.]], dtype=float)  # target not known

def brute_knn(training_features, labels, queries, k):
    result = []
    for query in queries:
        distances = np.sqrt(np.sum((training_features - query)**2, axis=1))
        ranked = np.argsort(distances, kind="stable")
        nearest_labels = labels[ranked[:k]]
        result.append(int(nearest_labels.sum() > k / 2))
    return np.asarray(result, dtype=int)

scaler = MinMaxScaler().fit(train)  # Training only, never Validation.
scaled_train = scaler.transform(train)
scaled_validation = scaler.transform(validation)
scaled_ticket = scaler.transform(new_ticket)
assert np.allclose(scaler.data_min_, [1,200])
assert np.allclose(scaler.data_max_, [10,750])

for kind in ("raw", "scaled"):
    train_features = train if kind == "raw" else scaled_train
    validation_features = validation if kind == "raw" else scaled_validation
    for k in (1,3,5):
        manual = brute_knn(train_features, known_priority, validation_features, k)
        sklearn_model = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k, weights="uniform")
        sklearn_model.fit(train_features, known_priority)
        library = sklearn_model.predict(validation_features)
        assert np.array_equal(manual, library)
        hits = int((manual == validation_labels).sum())
        print(kind, "k=", k, "predictions=", manual.tolist(), "correct=", hits, "/6")

raw_vote = brute_knn(train, known_priority, new_ticket, k=3)[0]
scaled_vote = brute_knn(scaled_train, known_priority, scaled_ticket, k=3)[0]
assert (raw_vote, scaled_vote) == (0,1)
print("New ticket: raw k=3", raw_vote, "; scaled k=3", scaled_vote)

25. Knowledge checks with worked reasoning

1. What is the difference between a neighbor in geography and a neighbor in k-NN?

A geographic neighbor is close in physical location. A k-NN neighbor has a small distance in the chosen Feature Space. Different Features or scales can produce different neighbor sets. For the bike-station example, commute time and commuter arrivals define proximity, not latitude and longitude.

2. Calculate the Euclidean Distance from Q=(3,3) to E=(5,4).

Subtract coordinates: (5−3,4−3)=(2,1). Square and add: 2²+1²=4+1=5. Take the square root: √5≈2.2361. E is farther than D at distance 2.

3. Recalculate Q's Classification vote for k=3.

The first three neighbors are A, B and C, with labels (1,0,0). Count one positive and two negatives, so the majority class is 0. The local positive-label fraction is 1/3, not a guaranteed calibrated event probability.

4. Why does k=5 give a different classification?

Adding D and E brings two additional positive labels. Among (1,0,0,1,1), class 1 has three votes and class 0 has two. The neighborhood size changed; the observations and their labels did not.

5. Calculate the k=3 rental prediction and identify its units.

Read rentals from A, B, C: 32, 20, 18. Sum 70, divide by 3 to get 23.3333. Its unit is bicycles rented in the defined operating window. It is a continuous Regression estimate, not a class label.

6. Derive the inverse-distance weighted prediction for k=3.

Weights are 1, 1/√2, 1/√2. Their sum is 1+√2≈2.4142. Weighted numerator =32+(20+18)/√2≈58.8701. Divide to obtain approximately 24.385 bicycles. A has extra weight because it is closer.

7. What happens if a query exactly matches two Training points with different labels?

Naively using 1/d divides by zero. A defensible zero-distance convention considers the exact matches only, then resolves their conflicting labels using a documented voting/tie policy. Software behavior should be checked rather than assumed.

8. Compute Manhattan and Euclidean distances to G=(6,1).

Coordinate differences are (3,−2). Manhattan =|3|+|−2|=5. Euclidean =√(3²+(−2)²)=√13≈3.6056. Neither formula is “incorrect”: they describe different geometries.

9. Explain the unit-change counterexample using actual numbers.

With arrivals in hundreds, Q=(3,3) has A at distance 1 and D at distance 2. With arrivals in individual persons, Q=(3,300), A=(3,200) and D=(1,300); their distances become 100 and 2. Hence the nearest-neighbor order can change solely because of units.

10. Why must StandardScaler be fitted only on Training?

The mean and standard deviation are learned parameters, not universal constants. Validation/Test should evaluate the complete trained procedure. Using their Feature distributions to update those parameters changes the procedure being evaluated and can bias model selection.

11. Select k using the Validation table and calculate accuracies.

For k=1, correct rows V2,V3,V4 give 3/5=0.60; for k=3, all five rows give 5/5=1.00; for k=5, V3,V5 give 2/5=0.40. We choose k=3 from these prespecified candidates, without claiming five rows prove a reliable future accuracy.

12. Calculate the locked k=3 Test Confusion Matrix.

T1 is an actual 1 predicted 0 (FN); T2 is 0 predicted 0 (TN); T3 is 1 predicted 1 (TP). Thus TP=1, TN=1, FP=0, FN=1. Accuracy =2/3, Recall =1/2, Precision =1, with extremely high sampling uncertainty.

13. Compare the business costs of k-NN and an all-positive Baseline.

Using $120 per FN and $20 per FP, k-NN incurs 1×$120=$120, while the all-positive Baseline incurs 1×$20=$20. Both have Test Accuracy 2/3. The example shows that equal Accuracy can hide different operational consequences; the values are illustrative.

14. Calculate the fraction 0.2^10 and explain its assumptions.

0.2^10=0.0000001024. It describes the volume of a ten-dimensional axis-aligned hypercube occupying 20% of the range in each coordinate, within the independent uniform unit-cube thought experiment. It is not the actual probability of a neighbor for all real datasets.

15. Why is k=9 similar to a majority-class Baseline in our dataset?

There are only nine Training records; considering all nine produces the same five positive and four negative votes for every query. Because distance no longer changes which records enter the vote, the classifier predicts 1 everywhere.

16. When is k-NN Regression unable to predict a rental value greater than 39?

With uniform or nonnegative normalized neighbor weights, the prediction is a convex combination of stored rental Targets. It must stay between the minimum (11) and maximum (39), so it cannot extrapolate beyond 39 without changing the method or using another model.

17. New data: recompute the raw and scaled votes for Q at k = 3.

Raw nearest tickets are B, A, C, all labeled 0; hence raw vote = 0. From Training only, the min–max denominators are 10−1=9 and 750−200=550. After Scaling, D, E, F are nearest, all labeled 1; thus scaled vote = 1. Same data, different geometry: scaling is part of the model specification, not harmless formatting.

18. Why does scaled k = 3 fix V5 when scaled k = 1 does not?

For V5=(5,250), the scaled nearest rankings begin G, B, A. Labels are 1, 0, 0. With k=1 the classifier predicts 1, contrary to known label 0. With k=3 the majority is 0 (two votes out of three), which matches the label. This is not evidence that k=3 is always preferable.

19. Does 6/6 Validation Accuracy prove perfect generalization?

No. The sample is only six historical requests; 6/6 is an observed fraction, not a population accuracy estimate with negligible uncertainty. Comparing six candidates on the same small Validation set may itself introduce optimistic selection effects. The selected MinMax + k=3 Pipeline must be evaluated on a fresh representative Test set before any credible release claim.

20. A future ticket has 900 prior requests, exceeding Training maximum 750. How do you transform it?

Keep the Training minimum 200 and maximum 750 frozen. Its second normalized coordinate is (900−200)/(750−200)=700/550≈1.2727. Do not recompute the maximum using the new ticket: doing so would change the learned distance geometry and invalidate the previously selected pipeline.

26. Glossary and connections to the next chapters

Nearest Neighbor: historical example with minimum selected distance to a query. Feature Space: coordinate system defined by the explanatory variables. Distance Metric: mathematical rule for separating observations, such as L1 or L2. Hyperparameter \(k\): number of observations used in each local prediction. Uniform Weights: equal influence per selected observation. Distance Weights: weights that shrink as distance grows. Decision Boundary: feature-space border between predicted classes. Curse of Dimensionality: deterioration of local-data coverage and distance informativeness as dimensions increase under relevant assumptions.

Standardization: rescaling using Training-estimated mean and standard deviation. Validation: data used to compare candidate settings, not to fit those settings. Test: held-out data for final evaluation after model selection. Data Leakage: information reaching a model-development step that should not be available there. Lazy Learning: retaining examples and making much of the computation at prediction time. Nonparametric Learning: predictions not restricted to a fixed-size global coefficient vector. Calibration: alignment between probability-like scores and observed outcome rates.

The central lesson is that k-NN makes a simple but powerful assumption: similar Feature Vectors may have similar outcomes. This assumption must be checked empirically, defended through Feature choices and evaluated with genuinely unseen observations. Our next chapters will examine other ways to learn a predictive rule; comparing them with k-NN helps reveal which aspects of modeling arise from the data and which from the algorithm.
