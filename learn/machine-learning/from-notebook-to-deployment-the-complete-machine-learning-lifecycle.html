1. A model is not a production system

A logistic model that works in a notebook is only one ingredient of a prediction service. A real service also needs a prediction contract (what the inputs mean and when they exist), a repeatable training procedure, a verified artifact, a decision policy, reliable infrastructure, monitoring and a safe way to reverse changes. The final capstone therefore asks not simply “Can we train a classifier?” but “Can another person reproduce, deploy, check and safely retire its decisions?”

Our synthetic logistics operator considers whether to send a human dispatcher an early warning of an order that may arrive late within 24 hours. One binary Feature, backlog_high, is observed at dispatch: 1 means the number of queued orders exceeds a documented threshold; 0 means it does not. We deliberately keep the model simple. A complex network would obscure the lifecycle decisions we wish to teach. The business action is review, not automatic cancellation or driver punishment.

A risk Score is not a guarantee; similarly, predicting which orders run late does not demonstrate that human review prevents lateness. That would require a separate intervention evaluation. We work exclusively with invented observations, costs and operational thresholds, so no conclusion about a real logistics fleet follows from the example.

Learning outcomes: reproduce the risk model and Validation decisions; package and test an immutable artifact; explain online versus batch inference; compute a distribution-shift statistic; design canary, monitoring and rollback procedures; and recognize where statistical evidence stops and human governance begins.

This guide also includes a fully calculated synthetic manufacturing transfer in Section 29, corresponding to the approved course’s predictive-maintenance capstone.

2. Define the decision and the information boundary

The unit is one order at its dispatch timestamp \(t_0\). The outcome \(y=1\) means that the order arrives after its promised deadline during the next 24 hours; \(y=0\) means it is on time. Historical completed shipments supply Labels, whereas a new order's Label remains unknown until its delivery window closes. backlog_high must be computed from the queue measured no later than \(t_0\). Using the actual arrival time, the final route delay, or a later dispatcher adjustment as a day-zero Feature would be Target Leakage.

Suppose the backlog threshold is 20 queued orders. If 23 are queued at dispatch, backlog_high=1; if 17, it equals 0. The threshold is part of the Feature definition, not a model coefficient. The service must record its units and exact cutoff because silently redefining “high” to mean 30 orders changes the statistical input even when the JSON field keeps its name.

The target's meaning also needs an operational definition. A damaged shipment, canceled order or incomplete delivery event should not silently be labeled on time. We would document which records are eligible, how cancellations are treated and when Labels have matured. For this teaching dataset, we assume every order has a correctly observed 24-hour outcome.

Contract boundary: at \(t_0\) the service may receive an order ID and the current backlog indicator; it may not receive post-delivery information. In the live system an upstream Feature process must timestamp and validate that indicator. Our small demonstration uses a simple JSON request without pretending to validate the upstream event history.

4. Why Train, Validation and Test must play different roles

Training estimates numerical model parameters. Validation chooses candidate models or decision thresholds without claiming that the chosen Validation score is a final independent estimate. Test assesses the frozen decision once. This separation is not an arbitrary software convention: repeated choices based on the same evaluation Labels can make the reported result look better than truly future performance.

In our chronological illustration, Train ends before Validation, which ends before Test. We assume Training and Validation Labels have fully matured before the later stage is run. A live system must enforce an as-of date and should consider repeated customers, routes or regions when those create correlations between rows. A split performed only by row ID may fail to protect independence when one customer has many deliveries.

We will estimate two risks only from the twelve Training rows, compare three thresholds using only the six Validation rows, then calculate Test once at the selected threshold. The fact that Test looks identical in certain aggregate metrics in this synthetic dataset is a consequence of the constructed rows, not evidence that future performance is predictable.

A useful thought experiment: if we inspect Test and then alter the threshold to reduce Test errors, that Test is now development information. We would need another untouched cohort before claiming a final independent evaluation. A production-monitoring feedback loop similarly requires a new, explicitly documented validation design.

5. Fit the model completely: conditional frequency and smoothing

Start with the unsmoothed observed frequencies: low backlog has \(1/6=0.166667\) late orders; high backlog has \(4/6=0.666667\). These fractions are understandable, but in very small groups the unsmoothed estimate can equal zero or one. Such extreme predictions may be fragile and generate arbitrarily large Log Loss on a contradictory observation.

We adopt Laplace smoothing with one pseudo-success and one pseudo-failure. For group \(b\in\{0,1\}\), let \(s_b\) denote late orders and \(n_b\) all orders in that group. We define a simple probability estimator:

\[\hat p_b=\frac{s_b+1}{n_b+2}.\]

Why add 2 to the denominator? The numerator adds one hypothetical late order and the denominator adds two hypothetical observations, one late and one on time. Equivalently, this equals the posterior mean under a Beta(1,1) prior and a Binomial sampling model; that Bayesian interpretation assumes exchangeable observations and a common conditional rate in each group. We use it as an illustrative regularized estimator, not a universally calibrated probability.

Calculate both groups without shortcuts:

\[\hat p_0=\frac{1+1}{6+2}=\frac28=0.25,\qquad \hat p_1=\frac{4+1}{6+2}=\frac58=0.625.\]

Verify: the probabilities lie between zero and one, and the high-backlog group receives higher estimated risk because Training contains more delayed orders there. These two numbers are the learned model parameters. All subsequent Validation, Test and serving scores must reuse them unchanged unless a formal retraining process creates a new version.

6. Translate the fitted model into a reproducible prediction function

For any eligible order, the Score function is determined entirely by its binary Feature:

\[f(b)=\begin{cases}0.25,&b=0,\\0.625,&b=1.\end{cases}\]

The decision rule adds a threshold \(\tau\): \(\hat y=1\) if \(f(b)\geq\tau\), otherwise \(\hat y=0\). The Score and the threshold are conceptually different. Training estimates a score; Validation selects how a potential human-review action uses that score. Neither calculation directly measures whether intervention helps.

Example: order V01 has high backlog, so \(f(1)=0.625\). At threshold 0.50 the predicted class is 1, and V01's actual Label 1 makes it a True Positive. V02 has the same backlog and Score but actual Label 0, so it becomes a False Positive. One score is not equivalent to one outcome. A score can rank or express uncertainty while individual deliveries remain uncertain.

A reproducible scoring contract must also define how to reject invalid inputs. Boolean true and false are accepted by the demonstration service; the text "yes", missing fields, unknown schema versions and nonsensical values are rejected rather than silently converted. Converting a string to a boolean inconsistently can change actual decisions without changing the model file.

8. Evaluate Scores before changing decisions

Classification metrics evaluate decisions, whereas a proper scoring rule evaluates probabilistic Scores. The Brier Score is the average squared difference between probability \(p_i\) and binary Label \(y_i\):

\[\mathrm{Brier}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i-y_i)^2.\]

On Validation the three high-backlog rows have outcomes \((1,0,1)\) and Score .625; the three low-backlog rows have outcomes \((0,1,0)\) and Score .25. Substitute all six observations:

\[\begin{aligned}\sum(p_i-y_i)^2&=2(0.625-1)^2+(0.625-0)^2\\&\quad+2(0.25-0)^2+(0.25-1)^2\\&=2(0.140625)+0.390625+2(0.0625)+0.5625\\&=1.359375.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(\mathrm{Brier}=1.359375/6=\boxed{0.2265625}\). Its unit is squared probability, not kilograms, orders or percentages. Lower is better on the same evaluation cases; the value does not demonstrate perfect calibration.

For a constant baseline using only the Training prevalence \(p=5/12\), Validation has three positive and three negative Labels. Therefore the baseline Brier Score is

\[\frac{3(1-5/12)^2+3(0-5/12)^2}{6}=\frac{3(49/144)+3(25/144)}6=\frac{37}{144}\approx0.2569444.\]

The model is better on this tiny Validation cohort by approximately \(0.03038\) squared-probability units. Do not infer that this improvement is stable outside these six synthetic observations. Log Loss could also be computed as the mean \(-[y\ln p+(1-y)\ln(1-p)]\); it penalizes highly confident incorrect predictions particularly strongly. We preserve the Brier calculation because it is transparent and easy to verify by hand.

9. Select a Decision Threshold and explain each cost

Probability prediction and resource allocation are different questions. Let \(\tau\) denote a proposed decision threshold. A predicted positive order is sent for manual review. For this exercise only, suppose each False Positive review carries an illustrative cost of 3 units, and each False Negative late shipment that was not flagged carries a cost of 5 units. We set the incremental costs of TP and TN to zero for simplicity, not because real reviews or delays are free.

\[C(\tau)=3\,FP(\tau)+5\,FN(\tau).\]

At \(\tau=0.20\), both .25 and .625 exceed the threshold, so all six Validation orders receive a review flag. Thus \(TP=3\), \(FP=3\), \(FN=0\), \(TN=0\); \(C=3(3)+5(0)=9\) units. At \(\tau=0.70\), neither Score reaches the threshold: \(TP=0\), \(FP=0\), \(FN=3\), \(TN=3\); \(C=3(0)+5(3)=15\). At \(\tau=0.50\) only high-backlog orders are flagged: \(TP=2\), \(FP=1\), \(FN=1\), \(TN=2\), giving \(C=3(1)+5(1)=\boxed{8}\).

10. Open Test only after the release choice is frozen

With model parameters \(p_0=.25\), \(p_1=.625\) and threshold \(\tau=.50\) all frozen, evaluate the six Test orders E01–E06. The labels and Scores are displayed in the held-out table above. The resulting counts are \(TP=2\), \(FP=1\), \(FN=1\), \(TN=2\). Their sum is 6. Thus:

\[\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{6}=\frac46=0.666667,\]
\[\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}=\frac23=0.666667,\quad\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}=\frac23=0.666667.\]

On Test, the squared probability error equals the same \(1.359375\) total as Validation because we built an analogous pattern; Brier \(=0.2265625\). The illustrative decision cost equals \(3(1)+5(1)=8\) units. We did not select these values using Test. This is a worked demonstration, not an independent real-world benchmark.

What is the statistical limitation? Each estimated 2/3 metric has a very small denominator, so one changed outcome could move the fraction by tens of percentage points. Even perfect chronological separation cannot compensate for poor sample size or an unrepresentative deployment population. A real launch would require a larger time-appropriate holdout, confidence intervals where valid, checks on difficult subgroups and operational constraints.

Finally, the score's role is limited to flagging a human review. The Test result does not demonstrate that reviews actually reduce delays. A causal impact assessment must compare interventions under a defensible study design, not interpret the classifier's Recall as intervention efficacy.

11. Freeze more than two coefficients: the release artifact

A notebook can recompute the same formula with silently changed preprocessing, an updated dataset or different library behavior. Production needs an immutable release artifact, a collection of mutually compatible assets. In this example the model stores risk_by_group={"0":0.25,"1":0.625}, threshold=0.50, schema_version="1.0", model_version="v1.0.0", the Target definition and the Training cutoff. The same release should retain dataset identity, source-code commit, environment, evaluation evidence and approval status.

A cryptographic hash such as SHA-256 converts an exact byte sequence to a 256-bit digest. If the artifact bytes change, its digest is overwhelmingly likely to change. However, a hash alone does not show the artifact is scientifically sound, trusted or safe: the publisher must also verify provenance and, when relevant, an authenticated digital signature. Two JSON files with different whitespace can have different hashes despite semantically identical objects, so our training program uses sorted keys and fixed separators before hashing.

Package the decision threshold with the model. If one server uses .50 but another loads .70, they produce different actions with identical risk parameters. Package the feature definition as well: backlog measured at dispatch is not interchangeable with backlog after delivery. Finally, record whether the artifact passed all required offline tests and who is authorized to promote it.

Release versions do not replace experiment records. The development history may include candidates that never reached production; only the approved package should be retrievable as the active version.

12. The prediction contract: schema, semantics and rejected inputs

An inference schema states what fields the caller must send and what fields the model returns. Our small JSON request looks like this (shown as source data, not a real production endpoint):

{"schema_version":"1.0","order_id":"P101","backlog_high":true}

The successful response is {"order_id":"P101","risk":0.625,"flag_for_review":true,"model_version":"v1.0.0"}. A request with backlog_high=false returns risk .25 and a false flag. The response identifies its model version so that an operator can later reproduce the action. The order ID is passed through and is not a predictor.

Why reject "backlog_high":"yes"? JSON strings and JSON booleans are distinct types. Allowing inconsistent coercion might make a value appear false in one language and true in another. Unknown fields and incompatible schema versions should trigger a clear validation error. Our miniature service returns HTTP 422; real services should define standard error codes, ownership, authentication, authorization and request logging policies.

Event-time checks are different from schema checks. A well-formed JSON boolean can still be computed using post-dispatch data. Production validation must ensure the upstream Feature is generated from permitted events as of the dispatch timestamp. Otherwise a flawless HTTP API can still be a leaking predictive system.

13. Batch prediction and online prediction are different services

Batch inference scores many already-defined orders together, perhaps every night before planning morning operations. Inputs are usually read from a table; outputs are a versioned file or database partition. Batch often values total throughput, deterministic reruns and large-scale reconciliation more than instant response.

Online inference scores a single new order when it is dispatched. Inputs arrive in a request and the service returns a response under a latency budget. It must defend against malformed payloads, bursts of traffic, retries, timeouts and unavailable dependencies. Both modes must use the same frozen transformation and threshold: otherwise the same order may be flagged in one system and ignored in the other.

For our model, a batch of three backlog indicators \([1,0,1]\) produces Scores \([.625,.25,.625]\) and review flags \([1,0,1]\) at threshold .50. The online service should produce exactly these results when those three orders are sent separately. That is a simple batch–online parity test.

An online response must not rely on a new fit operation. Learning from a single freshly received order would leak the role of inference into training, create inconsistent model versions and prevent repeatability. If a Feature is missing, a controlled error or documented fallback is preferable to inventing a value silently.

14. Lab 1: train, choose, evaluate and freeze from scratch

The following Python script uses only the standard library. It includes every synthetic observation, estimates both conditional probabilities, evaluates Validation candidates, opens Test after threshold selection, and writes a canonical JSON artifact. It is autonomous: copy the whole block into an empty directory and run it with Python 3.10 or newer. It does not require a notebook state or external dataset.

# Capstone lab 1: fit, select on Validation, test once, freeze artifact.
# Synthetic data only. Python 3.10+, standard library; no external dependencies.
import json, hashlib, math
from collections import Counter
from pathlib import Path
rows = [
    ('T01', 'Train', 0, 0),
    ('T02', 'Train', 0, 0),
    ('T03', 'Train', 0, 1),
    ('T04', 'Train', 0, 0),
    ('T05', 'Train', 0, 0),
    ('T06', 'Train', 0, 0),
    ('T07', 'Train', 1, 1),
    ('T08', 'Train', 1, 1),
    ('T09', 'Train', 1, 0),
    ('T10', 'Train', 1, 1),
    ('T11', 'Train', 1, 0),
    ('T12', 'Train', 1, 1),
    ('V01', 'Validation', 1, 1),
    ('V02', 'Validation', 1, 0),
    ('V03', 'Validation', 0, 0),
    ('V04', 'Validation', 0, 1),
    ('V05', 'Validation', 1, 1),
    ('V06', 'Validation', 0, 0),
    ('E01', 'Test', 1, 1),
    ('E02', 'Test', 0, 0),
    ('E03', 'Test', 1, 0),
    ('E04', 'Test', 0, 0),
    ('E05', 'Test', 1, 1),
    ('E06', 'Test', 0, 1),
]
train = [r for r in rows if r[1] == "Train"]
valid = [r for r in rows if r[1] == "Validation"]
test = [r for r in rows if r[1] == "Test"]

def fit_smoothed(part):
    counts = {str(b): (sum(r[3] for r in part if r[2] == b),
                       sum(r[2] == b for r in part)) for b in (0, 1)}
    risks = {k: (late + 1) / (n + 2) for k, (late, n) in counts.items()}
    return counts, risks

def evaluate(part, risks, threshold):
    outcomes = Counter()
    brier_terms = []
    for row_id, split, backlog, actual in part:
        p = risks[str(backlog)]
        predicted = int(p >= threshold)
        outcomes["TP" if predicted and actual else
                 "FP" if predicted else "FN" if actual else "TN"] += 1
        brier_terms.append((p - actual) ** 2)
    return outcomes, sum(brier_terms) / len(brier_terms)

counts, risks = fit_smoothed(train)
assert risks == {"0": 0.25, "1": 0.625}
print("Training counts", counts, "smoothed risks", risks)
for threshold in (0.20, 0.50, 0.70):
    cm, brier = evaluate(valid, risks, threshold)
    cost = 3 * cm["FP"] + 5 * cm["FN"]
    print("Validation", threshold, dict(cm), "cost", cost, "Brier", brier)
threshold = min((.20, .50, .70), key=lambda t: (lambda c: 3*c["FP"]+5*c["FN"])(evaluate(valid, risks, t)[0]))
assert threshold == .50
cm, brier = evaluate(test, risks, threshold)  # No Test-driven tuning.
print("Frozen Test", dict(cm), "Brier", brier)
assert (cm["TP"],cm["FP"],cm["FN"],cm["TN"]) == (2,1,1,2)
assert abs(brier - .2265625) < 1e-12
artifact = {"model_version":"v1.0.0", "schema_version":"1.0",
            "feature":"backlog_high", "risk_by_group":risks,
            "threshold":threshold, "train_last_id":"T12",
            "label":"late_within_24h", "synthetic":True}
canonical = json.dumps(artifact, sort_keys=True, separators=(",", ":")).encode()
artifact_hash = hashlib.sha256(canonical).hexdigest()
Path("logistics-risk-v1.json").write_bytes(canonical)
print("SHA256", artifact_hash, "saved", "logistics-risk-v1.json")

Reading the main functions: fit_smoothed counts positive and total rows within Train and applies \((s+1)/(n+2)\). evaluate converts Scores to classes, increments mutually exclusive TP/FP/FN/TN counters and computes Brier. The threshold-selection expression compares the three declared candidates using Validation; Test is evaluated once after the selection. The script prints each intermediate count and verifies the two final probabilities and the Test metrics with assert.

The last stage serializes the artifact with sorted JSON keys, computes SHA-256 and writes logistics-risk-v1.json. Do not mistake writing a model file for deploying it: the script demonstrates artifact creation, not authentication, a model registry, rollback or production access controls.

Reproduction check: if a student changes T03's Label from 1 to 0, the low-backlog risk changes from \((1+1)/8=.25\) to \((0+1)/8=.125\). The script's existing assertion then fails deliberately. That failure identifies that the canonical dataset no longer matches the published calculations and illustrates why a single source of truth is useful.

15. Lab 2: serve a local HTTP request and reject errors

A production application generally exposes a stable interface, rather than allowing arbitrary callers to open the model file and guess how to use it. The second script demonstrates a miniature HTTP endpoint only on the local loopback interface (127.0.0.1). It starts a short-lived server on an available port, sends good and malformed requests, checks responses, and shuts down automatically. The frozen model parameters are embedded so that this lab also runs independently of Lab 1.

# Capstone lab 2: autonomous local HTTP inference + contract tests + drift.
# Python 3.10+, standard library; binds only to local loopback on a random port.
import json, math, threading, urllib.request, urllib.error
from http.server import BaseHTTPRequestHandler, HTTPServer

# Frozen, audited values from the Training/Validation process in Lab 1.
# A real production service would load a signed/versioned artifact from a registry.
artifact = {"model_version":"v1.0.0", "schema_version":"1.0",
            "feature":"backlog_high", "risk_by_group":{"0":.25,"1":.625},
            "threshold":.50, "train_last_id":"T12",
            "label":"late_within_24h", "synthetic":True}

def score(payload):
    if set(payload) != {"schema_version", "order_id", "backlog_high"}:
        raise ValueError("Unexpected or missing fields")
    if payload["schema_version"] != artifact["schema_version"]:
        raise ValueError("Unsupported schema version")
    if not isinstance(payload["order_id"], str) or not payload["order_id"].strip():
        raise ValueError("Invalid order_id")
    if type(payload["backlog_high"]) is not bool:
        raise ValueError("backlog_high must be a boolean, not 0, 1 or text")
    p = artifact["risk_by_group"][str(int(payload["backlog_high"]))]
    return {"order_id":payload["order_id"], "risk":p,
            "flag_for_review":p>=artifact["threshold"],
            "model_version":artifact["model_version"]}

class Handler(BaseHTTPRequestHandler):
    def do_POST(self):
        try:
            if self.path != "/predict":
                raise ValueError("Unknown endpoint")
            n = int(self.headers.get("Content-Length", "0"))
            if n <= 0 or n > 4096:
                raise ValueError("Invalid body size")
            result = score(json.loads(self.rfile.read(n).decode("utf-8")))
            status = 200
        except (ValueError, TypeError, KeyError, json.JSONDecodeError) as exc:
            result, status = {"error":str(exc)}, 422
        body=json.dumps(result,sort_keys=True).encode()
        self.send_response(status)
        self.send_header("Content-Type","application/json")
        self.send_header("Content-Length",str(len(body)))
        self.end_headers(); self.wfile.write(body)
    def log_message(self,*args):
        pass  # No order IDs in demonstration server logs.

server=HTTPServer(("127.0.0.1",0), Handler)
thread=threading.Thread(target=server.serve_forever,daemon=True)
thread.start()
def post(payload):
    request=urllib.request.Request(
        f"http://127.0.0.1:{server.server_port}/predict",
        data=json.dumps(payload).encode(),
        headers={"Content-Type":"application/json"},method="POST")
    try:
        with urllib.request.urlopen(request,timeout=3) as response:
            return response.status,json.loads(response.read())
    except urllib.error.HTTPError as exc:
        return exc.code,json.loads(exc.read())
try:
    a=post({"schema_version":"1.0","order_id":"P101","backlog_high":True})
    b=post({"schema_version":"1.0","order_id":"P102","backlog_high":False})
    bad=post({"schema_version":"1.0","order_id":"P103","backlog_high":"yes"})
    print("High",a,"Low",b,"Invalid",bad)
    assert a[0]==b[0]==200 and a[1]["risk"]==.625 and b[1]["risk"]==.25
    assert bad[0]==422 and a[1]["flag_for_review"] and not b[1]["flag_for_review"]
finally:
    server.shutdown(); server.server_close(); thread.join(timeout=3)

reference = {"high":.5,"low":.5}  # 6 of 12 Training orders are high.
current = {"high":.75,"low":.25}    # 6 of 8 future orders are high.
psi = sum((current[k]-reference[k])*math.log(current[k]/reference[k]) for k in reference)
print("Population Stability Index", round(psi,9))
assert abs(psi - .2746530721670274)<1e-10
# Local HTTP is an educational demonstration, NOT internet-safe production serving.

The test checks that order P101 with backlog_high=true receives risk .625, a review flag, HTTP 200 and version v1.0.0. P102 with false receives .25 and no review flag. P103 sends "yes" as a string and receives HTTP 422; rejecting it is deliberate contract enforcement. The program finishes by verifying a PSI calculation introduced later.

Important boundary: this demonstration has no identity management, TLS, persistence, rate limits, persistent audit log, distributed retry handling or authorization. It must not be bound to a public network interface or treated as a deployable production service. A real implementation should use a maintained serving framework, monitored infrastructure and secured model artifacts, with appropriate threat modeling and operational review.

16. Container images and dependency pinning

Packaging answers “What exactly runs?” rather than simply “Which Python file contains the model?” A deployable package typically contains application code, a specific interpreter and dependency set, the approved model artifact, a machine-readable input schema, health endpoints, and operational configuration. A container image can bundle much of that environment so staging and production execute equivalent binaries. Containers reduce environment variation, but they are not a mathematical guarantee of reproducible results or a substitute for security hardening.

For example, use an explicitly supported Python 3.12 base image by immutable digest in a real build, pin the serving framework and other libraries in a locked dependency file, and add automated vulnerability checks. Our capstone scripts require no third-party Python modules; that is convenient for teaching but does not remove the need to maintain the operating system or review dependencies in a deployed service. The actual image digest and dependency versions must be recorded when built, not invented in a study guide.

An image should not bake credentials into its layers. Supply secrets through an approved secret manager at runtime. The service process should run with least privilege, avoid unnecessary network access and have read-only access to the signed model artifact where practical. Logs should identify model version and request correlation IDs without exposing sensitive client information.

Separation of environments: Training produces an artifact; a registry stores and authorizes it; serving loads it without retraining. Staging checks compatibility before traffic reaches production. If production differs in schema, time-zone interpretation or feature computation, identical model coefficients are not enough to guarantee consistent predictions.

17. Unit, integration and contract tests each catch different faults

Unit Tests examine a narrow function with controlled inputs. For score(), assert the input true returns .625, false returns .25, and the text "yes" raises a validation error. These tests directly protect model parity and the binary input contract. They do not prove that the production queue-counting process produces the correct boolean.

Integration Tests exercise components together. Our local HTTP lab checks encoding, request handling, returned status and output JSON. In a real service we would additionally test feature-store retrieval, timeout behavior, registry loading, TLS and persistence. A Contract Test checks compatibility between a producer and consumer: for schema 1.0, backlog_high must be a JSON boolean, not an optional text field. Changing its meaning requires explicit version management.

Regression Tests compare a new release with a known accepted set of inputs and outputs. If the published version produces [0.625, 0.25, 0.625] for backlog [1,0,1], a new binary should not change those numbers unless the release intentionally revises the model and updates its expected behavior. Property Tests can assert every valid risk is in \([0,1]\), order IDs do not affect risk, and risk for high backlog is not lower than for low backlog in this particular frozen artifact.

Do not confuse these with statistical validation. Passing a hundred software tests cannot establish that a probability estimate generalizes to next month's deliveries. Conversely, a model can show excellent Test Accuracy and still crash on a malformed API request. Production readiness requires both families of evidence.

18. Continuous Integration and the release gate

Continuous Integration (CI) automatically runs checks when code or configurations change. For this capstone, a meaningful CI sequence would validate Python syntax, run Unit and Contract Tests, check dependencies and container security, reproduce the small Training model, compare the artifact with expected values, and verify schema compatibility. A separate controlled Continuous Delivery step promotes an already verified artifact after operational approval.

Do not let CI silently retrain using future Labels. Training should use a recorded snapshot with an allowed cutoff; candidate comparison and Test access must follow the evaluation protocol. If a developer edits Training rows, new model parameters should receive a new version and full re-evaluation. If the newly built artifact's digest differs from the approved digest unexpectedly, stop the release rather than treating all passing syntax tests as sufficient evidence.

The release gate asks both scientific and software questions: Were the proposed costs and eligible population defined? Was Test used only after selection? Are subgroup results reviewed appropriately? Does the service reject malformed inputs? Can the exact previous model be restored? Who owns incident response? Any one critical open failure can block deployment regardless of average performance scores.

A realistic CI report records PASS, FAIL, NOT RUN and N/A separately. A Test never executed should not be described as passing; a code test unrelated to Data Leakage cannot substitute for the Data Leakage audit. This mirrors our Master Standard 3.0 academic QA philosophy.

19. Shadow, canary and gradual deployment

Deploying to every dispatcher at once multiplies exposure if the model is wrong. Shadow deployment sends live-shaped inputs to a candidate service while continuing to act using the approved old process. Candidate predictions are logged for comparison but not used for operational decisions. Shadow testing checks stability and discrepancies; it does not tell us whether acting on predictions would improve delivery outcomes.

A canary release exposes a small, explicitly chosen slice of real traffic to the new version, for example 5% of eligible requests. This number is illustrative, not a recommended universal percentage. Track error rate, latency, drift, alert volume and human-review burden. Expand only when predeclared safeguards pass. When downstream actions affect customers, canary design requires stronger risk controls, human approval and a plan to halt or reverse the change.

An A/B experiment randomizes eligible units between decision policies to assess the effect of the intervention, not merely the classifier's predictive skill. The randomization unit must account for shared routes, drivers or customers; interference can invalidate simplistic comparisons. Do not claim a causal improvement from an uncontrolled before/after change in late-delivery rate.

A valid rollout plan includes a named owner, stop criteria and a functioning rollback procedure before deployment. Reverting only model weights while leaving an incompatible preprocessing schema deployed is not a safe rollback.

20. Latency, reliability and capacity are measurable

Latency is elapsed time from receiving a request until responding; throughput is the number of requests served per unit time. They measure different things. Suppose a dispatch application expects at most 250 ms at the 95th percentile (p95), with an additional network allowance. That would mean at least 95% of measured requests should complete within 250 ms under the specified test distribution. A p95 from five local tests is not a production latency estimate.

An operational Service Level Indicator (SLI) might be the proportion of valid requests returning a successful response in under 250 ms. A corresponding Service Level Objective (SLO) could be 99.9% during a documented period. These are examples, not measured achievements of our demonstration. If 10,000 eligible requests occur and 15 fail the SLI, then \(9,985/10,000=99.85\%\), below a 99.9% SLO. We need 10 or fewer failures among 10,000 requests to meet 99.9% under that simple definition.

Capacity planning also constrains how many orders can be reviewed. If a model flags 400 of 1,000 orders but the team can inspect only 100, threshold .50 is operationally insufficient despite its Validation cost. The system must explicitly rank orders, queue them or revise the policy under capacity constraints. Merely changing the threshold in production without new Validation would bypass the approved decision design.

Robust serving requires timeouts, bounded request bodies, retry policies, idempotency for repeated order IDs, monitoring of memory/CPU and safe fallback behavior. A retry should not create duplicate human work simply because the same prediction was requested twice.

21. Compute Population Stability Index from first principles

When the production distribution of a Feature changes, model performance may change. An unlabeled shift can be detected before the new delivery outcomes mature. Suppose Training has six high-backlog and six low-backlog orders: reference shares \((r_H,r_L)=(.50,.50)\). In a fresh unlabeled batch of eight orders, six have high backlog and two have low: current shares \((c_H,c_L)=(.75,.25)\).

A commonly used descriptive statistic is Population Stability Index (PSI):

\[\mathrm{PSI}=\sum_k(c_k-r_k)\ln\left(\frac{c_k}{r_k}\right).\]

Here \(k\) identifies a Feature bin, \(r_k\) its reference share and \(c_k\) its current share. In our two-bin example each proportion is positive, so the logarithms are defined. Substituting:

\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{PSI}&=(.75-.50)\ln(.75/.50)\\&\quad+(.25-.50)\ln(.25/.50)\\&=.25\ln(1.5)-.25\ln(.5)\\&=.25(0.4054651)+(-.25)(-0.6931472)\\&=0.1013663+0.1732868=\boxed{0.2746531}.\end{aligned}\]

PSI is dimensionless and nonnegative in this two-bin setting. Different bin definitions and handling of zero-frequency categories produce different results; small smoothing constants should be documented, not hidden. There is no universal PSI cutoff proving model failure. Our eight new observations provide very weak evidence about the underlying population.

Even before labels arrive, the mean model Score changes mechanically. Training's mean Score was \(.5(.625)+.5(.25)=.4375\); the current batch's mean would be \(.75(.625)+.25(.25)=.53125\), an increase of \(0.09375\). Neither PSI nor this difference tells us whether late deliveries truly became more frequent or whether the model became less accurate.

22. Distinguish Data Drift from Concept Drift

Data Drift refers to a change in the input distribution \(P(X)\); our backlog share changing from 50% to 75% is a simple example. Concept Drift concerns a change in the relationship between inputs and outcomes, such as \(P(Y\mid X)\) changing even though the backlog distribution remains constant. The overall outcome rate \(P(Y)\) can shift for several reasons; it does not uniquely identify which mechanism changed.

To see the difference numerically, imagine that the low-backlog and high-backlog true late rates used to be 20% and 60%, respectively, and their population shares were each 50%. The overall late rate was \(.5(.2)+.5(.6)=.4\). If the group rates stay fixed but the high-backlog share becomes 75%, the overall late rate becomes \(.25(.2)+.75(.6)=.50\). This is a covariate/composition shift; no within-group probability changed.

In contrast, if group shares remain 50/50 but both conditional rates rise by .10, the overall late rate becomes \(.5(.3)+.5(.7)=.50\). The same overall late rate results, but this second scenario has Concept Drift. These scenarios are hypothetical and separate from our 24-row teaching dataset.

In real systems, \(P(Y\mid X)\) usually cannot be evaluated immediately because Labels may arrive 24 hours later or require data reconciliation. A drop in observed on-time deliveries may reflect a genuine change, reporting delay, definition drift, selection effects or a shift in operations. The investigation must match data availability, not assume every alert is model degradation.

23. Monitoring requires two clocks and multiple types of signals

A prediction has a request clock and an outcome clock. At dispatch time we can monitor request validity, feature distributions, model versions, latency, error rate and number of review flags. At least one day later—after all relevant outcomes are verified—we can evaluate predictive error, Brier Score, subgroup performance and operational effects. Reporting outcome-based Recall on the same day as newly created orders would quietly misclassify unknown outcomes as negatives.

A production dashboard should separate four layers of evidence: service health (availability, latency, 4xx/5xx patterns), data health (null values, schema mismatch, missing categories, feature freshness, PSI), model quality (Brier, precision, recall, calibration once labels mature), and decision effects (review workload, possible downstream costs, fairness). A graph of average risk alone is insufficient.

Example alert design: if invalid-input responses spike from 1% to 12%, check a client deployment or schema mismatch before assuming model drift. If high backlog increases from 50% to 75%, verify the queue counter's definition and investigate PSI, but do not retrain automatically. If matured Labels later show a sustained rise in Brier, assess sampling uncertainty, relevant subgroups and recent operational changes before proposing a model update.

Define a runbook for each alert: owner, measured signal, investigation steps, evidence required to close, conditions for pausing traffic and communication responsibilities. This transforms Monitoring from decorative dashboards into an operational process.

24. Retraining is a new experiment, not automatic overwriting

Retraining means estimating new parameters using an explicitly defined new Training cohort. It is not changing probabilities directly inside the running service. When enough new Labels have matured and there is a justified reason to update, freeze a fresh data snapshot, document exclusions and missing outcomes, rerun preprocessing and fit, select choices using a separate Validation cohort and reserve a new untouched Test or an appropriate time-respecting evaluation design.

For instance, suppose a later new Training window contains eight low-backlog orders with two late and eight high-backlog orders with six late. The updated smoothed probabilities would be \((2+1)/(8+2)=.30\) and \((6+1)/(8+2)=.70\). These are hypothetical numbers showing how an update changes parameters; they do not replace the approved values .25 and .625 until an evaluation and release gate explicitly approve a new version.

Compare the champion (currently approved model) and challenger (candidate). Evaluate both on the same relevant held-out cohort and under the same cost assumptions, examining subgroup effects and stability. If the challenger wins only on its own Training data, there is no convincing generalization evidence. After approval, publish a new version and keep the previous bundle available for rollback.

Beware feedback loops: dispatchers acting on model alerts can change delivery outcomes, meaning future Training data may reflect the earlier policy. The learning problem itself may become selective; consider exploration safety, intervention records and causal evaluation where needed. More data is not automatically unbiased data.

25. Incident response and rollback must be rehearsed

Imagine that after deploying v1.1.0, one warehouse's requests begin returning HTTP 422. The first question is operational: are callers now sending a string such as "true" instead of the JSON boolean true? If the feature producer changed its schema, the model coefficients may be perfectly valid while the serving system is unusable. A rollback plan that restores only the model but retains the incompatible producer does not solve the incident.

A useful runbook follows this sequence: detect (measure failures and impact), contain (pause promotion or route affected traffic to a safe fallback), diagnose (compare schema and model versions, timestamps and representative failed requests), restore (promote a known-good, compatible bundle), and verify (check both service health and model parity). Finally document the incident, corrections, affected requests and prevention steps.

A rollback requires the complete compatible set: model parameters, threshold, Feature schema and transformation, application code and dependencies when necessary. The last approved version should be accessible in the registry with its digest. Verify before launch that restoring it is technically possible; merely writing “rollback available” in a planning document does not prove recovery.

Suppose failures begin at 14:00 and the old system is restored at 14:12. The measured recovery time is 12 minutes, not the target or guarantee for a future incident. If a dispatched order was already incorrectly flagged, software rollback does not reverse downstream human actions; audit and remediation may still be necessary.

26. Reproducibility and audit trails beyond a filename

A reproducible result must identify which data, which code, which environment, which parameters and which evaluation policy produced it. A filename such as best_model_final_v3.pkl does not reliably answer any of those questions. A release manifest should include the artifact hash, source commit, Training as-of cutoff, data snapshot ID, model family, random seed when applicable, Feature schema, threshold, metrics, applicable environment versions, approval and rollback reference.

Data lineage records how raw queue events became backlog_high: source system, timestamp, order eligibility, aggregation window, queue threshold and missing-data handling. That lineage matters even though our model has only one Feature. If two warehouses calculate backlog at different stages of the shipment lifecycle, the data are semantically inconsistent despite sharing a column name.

A model Registry records candidate and production versions and helps enforce promotion permissions. An Experiment Tracker records unsuccessful and successful training runs and their settings. A Feature Store may manage shared feature computation. These are different responsibilities; one platform may combine some of them, but no tool alone supplies a trustworthy methodology.

To reproduce a production decision later, record a permitted request snapshot or privacy-preserving equivalent, active artifact version, output, decision threshold and a timestamp. Keep access logs and retention limited to legitimate operational needs. A hash only establishes byte identity; without provenance and access controls an attacker could replace both the file and the claimed hash.

27. Privacy, subgroup fairness and human oversight

Even a logistics predictor can have consequences for workers, customers and regions. An alert might redirect dispatcher attention toward particular delivery zones. Before deployment, evaluate whether error rates, eligibility rules or review capacity vary in ways that cause unfair or unjustified harm. This does not mean that every statistical difference proves discrimination; it means we need appropriate evidence, context and safeguards.

A subgroup audit separates False Positive Rate \(FP/(FP+TN)\), True Positive Rate \(TP/(TP+FN)\), review frequency, calibration and downstream outcomes when the denominators exist. Our 24 synthetic rows have no demographic or region variable and therefore cannot support a group-fairness claim. Adding invented protected attributes to make the dashboard look complete would be misleading. Real audits require lawful access, privacy controls, representative sample sizes and an explicit fairness question.

Our service should collect the minimum data necessary. The model itself needs a boolean backlog indicator; the order ID only supports correlation and auditing. Do not log detailed customer addresses or identifiers by default. Sensitive operational records should be access-controlled and retained according to documented policy. Anonymization, pseudonymization and aggregation solve different privacy problems and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Human-in-the-loop means more than placing a person next to the model. The reviewer must understand the Score's meaning, be able to contest incorrect inputs and decline an unsupported action. Monitor reviewer workload and overrides because automatic deference to Scores can make nominal human review ineffective. Decisions with serious consequences require proportionate governance and escalation beyond a single model metric.

28. Scientific limitations: what this capstone cannot establish

There are several fundamentally different limitations. Statistical: 24 synthetic orders are insufficient to estimate generalization, probability calibration or subgroup disparities reliably. Modeling: a one-Feature lookup may ignore route, weather, warehouse operations and other drivers of lateness. Operational: a correctly scored request may still arrive too late for intervention; review capacity may be inadequate. Causal: alerting about an order does not establish that reviewing it improves on-time delivery.

Our Training estimate assumes orders within each backlog group are exchangeable with a stable conditional lateness probability. Delayed events, repeated drivers, seasonal changes and correlated deliveries could violate that assumption. Laplace smoothing prevents certain extremes but does not correct selection bias, temporal drift or missing variables. A well-formed API makes those limitations operationally accessible; it does not remove them.

Data drift, score drift and model-quality drift also differ. We calculated PSI on unlabeled backlog observations; it does not tell us whether late deliveries increased or the model's Brier Score deteriorated. Monitoring must wait for suitable matured Labels and must compare compatible data windows. Retraining on bad labels can amplify errors instead of resolving them.

Finally, responsible modeling is ongoing. A release that was appropriate at approval time may become inappropriate after workflows, regulations, populations, incentives or measurement systems change. Versioning and Monitoring are necessary precisely because validation results are conditional on a moment and context, not permanent guarantees.

29. A practical complete-release walkthrough

Follow one new order through the system. The dispatcher creates P101; an upstream queue event says 23 orders are waiting when the reference threshold is 20. Therefore the eligible Feature is backlog_high=true at dispatch. The caller sends P101, schema 1.0 and that boolean to the approved service. The server validates the field types and the schema, loads v1.0.0 (without fitting), maps true to .625 and compares with the frozen .50 threshold. It returns a review flag and the version.

The operator receives the flag and may inspect route constraints. That review is an intervention, not part of the model's score computation. The request is recorded with appropriate privacy controls. When delivery is completed and the 24-hour outcome matures, the organization can compare risk .625 with actual \(y\in\{0,1\}\), calculate a squared-error contribution and update monitored aggregates for the relevant evaluation period.

Suppose P101 is on time, so \(y=0\). The Brier contribution is \((.625-0)^2=.390625\); because the order was flagged, this is a False Positive. The existence of one False Positive does not prove the model is faulty; it may be expected from a model with uncertain Scores. If more matured errors accumulate, the team investigates reproducibility, Data Drift, Concept Drift and subgroup patterns rather than blindly adjusting the threshold in production.

The operational record should connect this request with the release version and approved data definition; otherwise, six months later, the same risk value may no longer be traceable to the correct model and Feature computation.

Monitor feature mix now; evaluate model quality when labels mature

Four of eight Training machines have high vibration, so the reference distribution is 50% high / 50% low. In a separate, unlabeled synthetic production window, 15 of 20 observations have high vibration: 75% high / 25% low. Population Stability Index (PSI) on these two bins is:

\[\begin{aligned} PSI &=\sum_{k\in\{high,low\}}(p_{new,k}-p_{ref,k})\ln\frac{p_{new,k}}{p_{ref,k}}\\ &= (0.75-0.50)\ln\frac{0.75}{0.50}+(0.25-0.50)\ln\frac{0.25}{0.50}\\&=0.25\ln(3)\approx \boxed{0.274653}.\end{aligned}\]

Both terms are positive in this example; calculate 0.25·ln(1.5) ≈ 0.101366 and (−0.25)·ln(0.5) ≈ 0.173287. Their sum is 0.274653 (dimensionless). This indicates a distributional difference for investigation, but it does not prove more failures, worse calibration or Concept Drift. The illustration assigns no universally valid PSI cutoff. For predictive quality we must wait for the seven-day outcome window and any reporting delay; until then, missing labels are not negative outcomes.

30. Common mistakes and why each matters

  1. Fitting on Test: inspecting Test to pick a threshold contaminates the final evaluation. Freeze the choice using Validation first.
  2. Calling .625 a guaranteed 62.5% real-world risk: the tiny synthetic smoothed rate has no validated external calibration.
  3. Using an order ID as a predictive Feature: arbitrary identifiers may encode history or leak split information; our model deliberately excludes them.
  4. Ignoring event time: today's backlog at 18:00 is not the backlog known at the 10:00 prediction moment.
  5. Changing a Feature definition without a schema bump: numerical compatibility does not imply semantic compatibility.
  6. Keeping the threshold outside the artifact: servers using different thresholds will make inconsistent decisions.
  7. Hashing a file and declaring it trustworthy: integrity is not provenance, scientific validation or authenticated authorization.
  8. Passing Unit Tests and claiming generalization: software correctness and statistical validity are different.
  9. Reporting mean latency as p95: an average cannot stand in for a tail percentile.
  10. Counting unknown outcomes as negative: this creates invalid metrics when Labels are delayed.
  11. Equating PSI with model failure: distribution change is a diagnostic, not an outcome-based performance test.
  12. Retraining automatically on every alert: Drift may reflect a pipeline bug or temporary composition change.
  13. Rolling back coefficients alone: schema, code, threshold and dependencies may also need restoration.
  14. Treating human review as guaranteed benefit: prediction does not establish causal action effectiveness.
  15. Claiming fairness from tiny or absent subgroups: the denominator and relevant protected dimensions may be missing.
  16. Declaring a release ready because a notebook ran once: production requires evidence, controls, ownership, incident plans and monitoring.

Every item refers to a specific failure mode, not a decorative checklist. If a risk cannot be evaluated yet, record what evidence is missing and what decision remains blocked.

31. Nineteen solved exercises and transfer questions

Attempt each exercise before opening the solution. The nineteen problems now extend from the logistics pipeline to the complete manufacturing transfer, with mathematical verification, deployment controls and limitations.

1. Recompute the low-backlog risk from Training

Six low-backlog Training orders include one late order. The smoothed formula is (late+1)/(total+2)=(1+1)/(6+2)=2/8=0.25. We add one hypothetical late and one on-time case to regularize an unstable small sample. The result is a model estimate, not a verified population probability.

2. Recompute the high-backlog risk and the risk gap

High backlog includes four late orders among six: (4+1)/(6+2)=5/8=0.625. The high–low risk gap is 0.625−0.25=0.375, or 37.5 percentage points in estimated probability. This is association conditional on backlog, not a causal effect of increasing the queue.

3. Why is V02 a False Positive?

V02 has backlog_high=1, so its frozen Score is 0.625. Because 0.625≥0.50, the review flag is positive; its actual Label is 0. A positive prediction on a negative observation is FP. Its Brier contribution is (0.625−0)²=0.390625; changing the threshold would be a separate model-selection decision.

4. Verify the six-row confusion counts

Validation has two high-backlog late rows (V01,V05), one high-backlog on-time row (V02), one low-backlog late row (V04) and two low-backlog on-time rows (V03,V06). Hence TP=2, FP=1, FN=1, TN=2 and the total equals 6. Precision=2/(2+1)=2/3; Recall=2/(2+1)=2/3.

5. Calculate Brier Score for V04

V04 is a low-backlog order with a late Label y=1. Its Score is p=0.25. The squared probability error is (p−y)²=(0.25−1)²=(−0.75)²=0.5625. The contribution is relatively large because the model assigned a low risk to an event that occurred.

6. Recalculate the selected threshold cost

At 0.50, FP=1 and FN=1. Under costs 3 per FP and 5 per FN, C=3×1+5×1=8 units. The all-positive alternative costs 3×3=9, while all-negative costs 5×3=15. Therefore 0.50 is best among these predeclared candidates on Validation, not necessarily universally optimal.

7. What if False Negatives cost 12 instead of 5?

Holding all predictions fixed, the 0.50 rule costs 3(1)+12(1)=15 units; all-positive costs 3(3)+12(0)=9; all-negative costs 12(3)=36. Therefore the preferred Validation threshold changes to 0.20 among the candidates. Different costs require a new Validation decision, not Test-based tuning.

8. Why can Test not choose another threshold?

Test is meant to estimate how a frozen model-selection procedure behaves on held-out data. If Test Labels determine a revised threshold, their errors influence the candidate itself, so the set is no longer independent. Reserve a fresh later cohort to evaluate the revised decision, or use a valid nested or temporal design.

9. Compute a batch–online parity example

For backlog indicators [1,0,1], the model returns risks [0.625,0.25,0.625]. At threshold 0.50, flags are [true,false,true]. A batch process and three online requests must agree exactly under the same artifact and contract. If not, first investigate input coercion, feature availability and version mismatch.

10. Detect invalid JSON semantics

The payload {"backlog_high":"yes"} encodes a string, not a JSON boolean. In our strict request schema the payload also needs schema_version and order_id. It is invalid, and the demonstration endpoint returns HTTP 422. Accepting arbitrary strings silently could make predictions depend on implementation language instead of the documented model contract.

11. Compute PSI from two bins

Reference shares are (.50,.50); current shares are (.75,.25). PSI=.25 ln(1.5)−.25 ln(.5)=.25×.4054651+.25×.6931472≈.2746531. This indicates a feature-distribution difference. The calculation contains no outcome labels, so it cannot demonstrate deteriorated predictive accuracy.

12. Calculate the current mean Score without labels

The current mixture has 75% high and 25% low backlog. Expected average score under the frozen model is .75(.625)+.25(.25)=.46875+.0625=.53125. The Training mixture average was .5(.625)+.5(.25)=.4375. The increase is .09375 probability points (9.375 percentage points), not 9.375% relative growth.

13. What is the SLO result after 15 failures?

Given 10,000 eligible requests, 15 failing the stated SLI leave 9,985 successes. Success rate=9,985/10,000=.9985=99.85%. This is less than a 99.9% SLO, whose failure budget would be 10 per 10,000 under that simple period definition.

14. Calculate an updated risk without promoting it

A proposed new Training window has 2 late of 8 low-backlog orders and 6 late of 8 high-backlog orders. Laplace smoothing gives (2+1)/(8+2)=0.30 and (6+1)/(8+2)=0.70. These are challenger parameters; releasing them requires a new evaluation and approval, not silent editing of v1.0.0.

15. Compute the Brier contribution of a monitored order

P101 receives p=0.625 and later is confirmed on time (y=0). The squared contribution is (0.625−0)²=0.390625. If the Label is not yet mature, the contribution is unknown and must not be recorded as if y=0. Aggregation requires a properly defined outcome window.

16. Design a safe rollback after schema mismatch

First contain the release by stopping promotion or routing traffic to a known-safe path. Diagnose which client changed its boolean field, restore the approved model+schema+threshold+compatible application, verify valid/invalid requests and parity, then document impact and remediation. Restoring only model coefficients while leaving incompatible inputs cannot fix the incident.

Transfer challenge: devise a second monitoring cohort that changes the proportion of high backlog but leaves the conditional late rates unchanged. Explain which drift category it exemplifies and why without Labels a dashboard cannot prove this underlying mechanism. A defensible answer uses the same logic as our 20%/60% rate example and explicitly distinguishes observed frequencies from assumptions.

17. Manufacturing: reconstruct the frozen Test Brier Score

Four Test machines yield errors: high/failed (2/3−1)²=1/9; high/not-failed (2/3−0)²=4/9; low/not-failed (1/3−0)²=1/9; low/failed (1/3−1)²=4/9. Sum 10/9, divide by four: 10/36=5/18≈0.277778 squared-probability units. Do not select a new threshold using this Test result.

18. Manufacturing: compute the feature-shift PSI

Reference shares (high,low)=(.50,.50), current=(.75,.25). PSI=(.75−.50)ln(.75/.50)+(.25−.50)ln(.25/.50)=.25ln(1.5)−.25ln(.5)=.25ln(3)=0.274653. Since the live labels have not matured, the result establishes input shift only, not a failure in prediction quality.

19. Manufacturing: decide whether to promote the canary

The candidate has 6 HTTP errors in 100 requests, so error rate=6/100=6%, above the predeclared 2% limit by 4 percentage points. Its measured p95 latency is 310 ms, 60 ms above the 250 ms limit. Both separately trigger stop. Pause promotion, restore the compatible approved service, preserve incident evidence, and investigate before trying a revised release. These metrics are operational, not confusion-matrix errors.

33. Glossary and the final conceptual synthesis

Artifact: immutable model inputs and decisions shipped together. As-of cutoff: latest time allowed for learning or computing a Feature. Batch inference: scoring many records together. Brier Score: mean squared probability error. Canary: small, controlled exposure of a candidate release. Champion/Challenger: current approved system and proposed alternative. CI: automatic code and verification pipeline. Concept Drift: change in the conditional target relationship. Contract Test: check compatibility of exchanged data. Data Drift: change in input distribution. Experiment Tracker: system recording trial configurations and outputs. Feature lineage: provenance and transformation history of a Feature. Inference: using fixed parameters to calculate a prediction. Model Card: structured description of intended use and limitations. Model Registry: approved version storage and promotion control. PSI: descriptive statistic comparing binned proportions. Rollback: restoring a known-compatible previous release. Shadow: candidate predictions that do not affect decisions. SLO/SLI: stated reliability target and measured indicator. Target Leakage: future or disallowed information enters a prediction. Versioned schema: recorded, enforceable input/output definition.

Return to the whole lesson as a single reasoning chain: (1) define the prediction moment and allowed data; (2) estimate Training probabilities .25 and .625; (3) check probabilistic error using Validation; (4) choose threshold .50 for the declared illustrative costs; (5) evaluate untouched Test; (6) package parameters, schema and threshold; (7) verify batch and online parity; (8) stage and gradually deploy with a rollback; (9) monitor data immediately and outcomes only after labels mature; (10) consider retraining as a new validated experiment.

The final lesson of the 33-guide Machine Learning sequence is not that every model should look like our two-number estimator. It is that a model deserves operational trust only when its assumptions, evidence, interface, use and failure modes remain inspectable after it leaves the notebook. The same discipline scales to logistic regression, tree ensembles, neural networks and more complicated production architectures.

The manufacturing transfer validates the same reasoning at another level: feature definitions must exist at scoring time, a threshold is selected on Validation rather than Test, a drift dashboard cannot see immature labels, and a failed operational canary must not be promoted. None of these synthetic checks constitutes permission to operate a real maintenance system.
