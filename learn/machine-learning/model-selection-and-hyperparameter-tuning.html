1. Why the best Training score is not the best model

A telecom company wants to identify customers who may cancel their service (churn) next month. A model trained on historical customers produces a score for each new customer. But which model should the company trust: Logistic Regression, a Decision Tree, a Random Forest, or k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)? Even after choosing an algorithm, it must decide its settings. Those are two related but distinct questions: Model Selection chooses among modeling strategies, while Hyperparameter Tuning chooses settings within a strategy.

Training performance answers how well the model fits data it has used to learn. It does not tell us reliably how the model will perform on future customers. The tempting approach—try many models and report the best number on Test—contaminates the evaluation. We will instead build a defensible sequence: reserve Test; specify a metric; use Training/Validation folds to compare choices; refit the winner on development data; and inspect Test once at the end.

Our first example is small enough to calculate by hand. Our second is a reproducible Python experiment with 360 synthetic telecom customers. Both study the same business question, but the second adds more realistic Features and preprocessing. All results are educational, not forecasts of real telecom performance.

A high score is not evidence of a good selection process. We must ask: **Who was allowed to see which data, and at what stage?**

2. Learning objectives, prerequisites and what will be proven

By the end, you should be able to distinguish a Parameter from a Hyperparameter; construct three Cross-Validation folds; compute their Accuracy; perform a complete Grid Search; compare it with Randomized Search; identify the source of selection optimism; explain Nested Cross-Validation; and audit a Pipeline for leakage. Finally, you should be able to defend the decision to reserve Test even when the selected model disappoints.

You only need elementary averages, fractions and an understanding that a supervised classifier maps Features to a categorical Target. We will introduce Balanced Accuracy, regularization strength, stratification, refitting and selection bias before using them mathematically. Readers familiar with Guides 5, 6, 12, 15 and 21 will recognize preprocessing, evaluation, k-NN and ensemble models; however, this chapter provides the necessary definitions.

The intellectual roadmap is: one small dataset → predictions inside folds → a complete search → bias and uncertainty → Nested CV → a reproducible comparison of algorithms → untouched Test. Notice that estimating how well the whole selection procedure performs is a harder question than measuring one already specified model. Nested CV exists precisely to separate these two questions.

We will call the set that is available for tuning the development set. It contains observations used for model fitting and inner validation. The Test set is held outside every decision. When we later fit the final model on all development observations, it will no longer have internal holdouts, but Test will remain untouched.

4. Parameters versus Hyperparameters: who learns what?

A Parameter is a quantity estimated from Training. Logistic Regression learns an intercept and slopes; a Decision Tree learns split locations; k-NN effectively retains labeled Training cases. A Hyperparameter is an externally specified control over how this learning happens. Examples include the number of neighbors \(k\), a tree's max_depth, the Logistic Regression inverse-regularization strength \(C\), and the number of CV folds.

Consider k-NN. Given a new decline value \(x_*\), we calculate the distance to each Training customer, \(d_i=|x_i-x_*|\); sort distances; and take a vote of the \(k\) nearest labels. The locations and labels are learned/stored from Training; \(k\) is set outside the fit. In Logistic Regression, a coefficient such as \(\beta_1\) is learned, while \(C\) controls the degree of regularization imposed when learning coefficients.

Model Learned from Training Chosen through tuning
k-NN Stored labeled records n_neighbors, weights
Logistic Regression Intercept, coefficients C, penalty choice
Decision Tree Splits and leaf predictions max_depth, min_samples_leaf
Random Forest Trees and fitted leaf estimates n_estimators, max_depth

Do not assume that every setting needs optimization. Before searching, choose a plausible range based on the data and computational budget. The random seed is generally a reproducibility control, not a meaningful business Hyperparameter to select because a lucky seed scores higher.

5. Choose the metric before seeing the leaderboard

Suppose the company wants to catch departing customers, but each retention call has a cost. Accuracy is the fraction of correct classifications. Recall measures detected cancellations among all actual cancellations. Precision measures real cancellations among the customers flagged. Balanced Accuracy averages Recall for churned customers and Specificity for retained customers.

\[\operatorname{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}\]
\[\operatorname{BalancedAccuracy}=\frac12\left(\frac{TP}{TP+FN}+\frac{TN}{TN+FP}\right)\]

Here, TP means a genuine churn customer flagged, FN a churn customer missed, FP a stable customer flagged, and TN a stable customer correctly left alone. If 90 of 100 customers stay, predicting Stay for all yields 90% Accuracy, but Churn Recall = 0; Balanced Accuracy = \((0+1)/2=0.50\). Therefore, a high Accuracy can be a poor result when churn is rare.

In the 15-customer hand example we use Accuracy to keep voting calculations transparent; in the larger, imbalanced example we commit to Balanced Accuracy. This change of metric reflects a change in instructional experiment, not a post-hoc attempt to pick a higher-looking score. Operational cost, probability calibration and intervention capacity should also be assessed before deploying a system. Choose the principal metric and tie-breaking rule before conducting the search.

6. Three-fold Cross-Validation: the exact data partitions

A single Training/Validation split can make one candidate look unusually good or bad. K-Fold Cross-Validation (CV) repeats the assessment with several held-out subsets. Here K = 3; do not confuse this capital K (number of folds) with lowercase \(k\) (neighbors in k-NN).

CV round Validation customer IDs Training customer IDs Size
1 1, 4, 7, 10, 13 All others 10 train + 5 validate
2 2, 5, 8, 11, 14 All others 10 train + 5 validate
3 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 All others 10 train + 5 validate

During round 1, fit a new k-NN model using only the ten customers not in Fold 1. Predict the five Fold 1 customers and record the errors. Discard that fitted state. Round 2 creates a fresh model with its own ten Training customers; the previous fold's Validation records may now participate in Training, but never in the same fold where they were held out. Repeat for Fold 3. Thus each customer is evaluated once by a model that did not train on that customer's label.

With equal fold sizes, mean fold Accuracy equals total correct out-of-fold predictions divided by 15. If fold sizes differ, these aggregates are not necessarily the same: mean fold score gives equal fold weight, while pooled accuracy gives equal observation weight. Neither convention is universally optimal. We must state which quantity we calculate.

9. Grid Search: all candidates, not only the winner

Grid Search evaluates every combination in a predefined discrete grid. We choose five neighbor counts, \(k\in\{1,3,5,7,9\}\), and two voting rules: uniform (each neighbor gets one vote) and distance (closer neighbors receive more weight). The Cartesian product contains \(5\times2=10\) configurations. With three folds, we perform 30 independent fits. This matters because the cost scales with candidates times folds.

k Uniform accuracy Distance-weighted accuracy
1 53.33% 53.33%
3 66.67% 66.67%
5 66.67% 66.67%
7 80.00% 66.67%
9 66.67% 66.67%

The best candidate under the prespecified CV mean Accuracy is k=7 with uniform voting. Why does distance weighting not necessarily improve predictions? Its weights emphasize very close neighbors, including noisy ones; proximity alone does not guarantee a correct label. A larger \(k\) smooths some local noise but can also blur genuinely different customer groups. We do not claim that \(k=7\) is universally optimal—only that it wins among these ten candidates for these exact folds and this metric.

A search space is more than a list of names: define parameter ranges, the metric, CV design, computational budget, and a tie rule. Without these choices recorded in advance, the selection is difficult to audit or reproduce.

10. What variation across folds does—and does not—mean

Even within the same candidate, observed validation performance changes when different customers are held out. For example, the scores of \(k=3\) are 0.80, 0.60 and 0.60; their mean is 0.6667. The sample standard deviation across folds is:

\[s=\sqrt{\frac{(0.80-0.6667)^2+2(0.60-0.6667)^2}{3-1}}\approx0.1155\]

The spread is 11.55 percentage points. For \(k=7\), all three scores are 0.80; their empirical sample standard deviation is zero. That does not mean zero uncertainty about future customers! The folds are small, overlap substantially in Training and all come from the same sample. Their scores are statistically dependent, and a standard error computed naively as \(s/\sqrt3\) is not automatically a valid uncertainty interval for the deployed model.

For development we can inspect fold distributions, compare repeated or appropriately grouped splits, and assess confidence intervals on an independent evaluation sample when sample size permits. Never turn a CV standard deviation into a precise guarantee about production performance. Nor should we choose a candidate only because its mean is higher by a tiny, unstable amount; model complexity, operational constraints and a prespecified tie rule also matter.

The variation we observe is a property of this evaluation setup. **Low measured fold variability is not a proof of robust generalization.**

11. Refit once, then open the small Test set

Once \(k=7\), uniform voting is selected, what do we train on? We refit one final k-NN model on all 15 development customers. We do not average the three fitted CV models or choose whichever fold model happened to score highest: those fits existed to evaluate settings. The final model uses all available development labels, and its preprocessing (if any) is likewise refitted only on development.

Our untouched five Test customers have usage declines 2, 8, 14, 20 and 26, with true outcomes 0, 0, 0, 1 and 1. The fitted \(k=7\) model predicts 0, 0, 1, 1 and 1.

This completes the first full selection exercise. Next we examine why selecting among many noisy comparisons tends to make the winning reported CV score optimistic.

12. Selection bias and the winner’s curse

Imagine 20 candidate configurations that actually have identical true performance of 0.65. Their observed Validation scores fluctuate because samples are finite. Suppose, solely as a numerical demonstration, each observed score behaves like \(0.65+\varepsilon\), with independent zero-mean Normal noise of standard deviation 0.06. Each candidate's reported score is individually unbiased: its expectation is 0.65. Yet after picking the maximum across 20 candidates, we have selected the most favorable noise realization.

\[\widehat j=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\le j\le20}\widehat S_j\]
\[E[\max_j\widehat S_j]>0.65\]

The inequality is a manifestation of selection optimism under this simulation. It does not mean the true quality of the winning model improved. We can reproduce the phenomenon by drawing 20 noisy values per simulated experiment and recording the largest. The following graph uses a fixed random seed across 2,500 experiments; it is not a measurement from our telecom customers.

This is related to multiple comparisons: the more configurations, transformations and thresholds we try on the same Validation signal, the more likely that some apparently great result is just luck. A common error is to call the winning CV score an unbiased estimate of the whole search procedure. It is generally an estimate used for ranking candidates, not a completely independent performance audit. This motivates an outer holdout or Nested CV.

We cannot remove selection bias by using a fancier search algorithm if we still select and evaluate on the same information. The separation of data roles—not the search brand—is the protection.

13. The preprocessing trap: fit belongs inside each fold

Suppose usage decline is missing for some customers, and we impute missing values with the median. Suppose we also Standardize a variable before Logistic Regression or k-NN. Both steps learn quantities: Training median, Training mean and Training Standard Deviation. If we calculate those numbers on the entire development set before CV, each Validation fold influences the transforms it is supposed to evaluate—even if its churn labels were not used.

For a simple numerical contrast, let a Training fold contain usage declines 10, 20 and 30, while its Validation fold contains 100. Training Standardization learns mean \(\mu=20\) and population-form standard deviation:

\[\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{(10-20)^2+(20-20)^2+(30-20)^2}{3}}\approx8.165\]
\[z_{Validation}=\frac{100-20}{8.165}\approx9.798\]

If we improperly pool 10, 20, 30 and 100 to fit preprocessing, the mean becomes \(40\), standard deviation \(\sqrt{1250}\approx35.355\), and the same Validation point becomes \((100-40)/35.355\approx1.697\). It has not changed; our preprocessing reference has been contaminated.

Correct workflow inside every fold: imputer.fit(X_fold_train), scaler.fit(imputed_fold_train), model.fit(transformed_fold_train,y_fold_train); then predict on fold Validation with those same frozen fitted transformations. Pipeline automates this order when passed directly into a CV search. The final refit learns transformations again from all development data, never from Test.

15. Pipeline design: learned transformations never see Validation

For numerical columns, we apply SimpleImputer(strategy='median') then StandardScaler(). For categories, we apply OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown='ignore'). A ColumnTransformer combines the two branches; a scikit-learn Pipeline places the classifier after that transformer. In each CV fold, GridSearchCV.fit clones the entire Pipeline and fits every step using only the inner Training rows.

Step Learns from fold Training? Applied to fold Validation?
Numeric median imputation Yes: a median for each numeric Feature Uses stored medians
StandardScaler Yes: means and scales Uses stored settings
OneHotEncoder Yes: observed category vocabulary Uses frozen columns
Logistic Regression Yes: coefficients and intercept Predicts; no refit

The categorical encoder handles previously unseen categories without changing the number of output columns. This is different from creating dummy columns independently for Training and Validation; independent encoding can shift Feature dimensions and meanings.

A frozen transformation does not mean we cannot process new customers. It means that when we call transform on Validation, Test or production, we do not use those rows to relearn statistical settings. The larger example will have missing values intentionally, so this difference is not theoretical. The complete source code is given later in the guide, and the cross-validation search receives the full Pipeline rather than a pre-transformed matrix.

16. Four-fold GridSearchCV: every Logistic C score

Logistic Regression estimates coefficients by minimizing a penalized Log Loss. In scikit-learn, \(C>0\) is inverse regularization strength: smaller \(C\) means stronger penalization; larger \(C\) means weaker penalization. The learned coefficients are Parameters; \(C\) is the Hyperparameter. We prespecify \(C\in\{0.1,1,10\}\) and use four stratified folds on the 288 development customers.

C Fold 1 Fold 2 Fold 3 Fold 4 Mean Balanced Accuracy
0.1 0.5243 0.5695 0.5338 0.6053 0.5582
1 0.6822 0.6221 0.5844 0.6316 0.6301
10 0.6822 0.6390 0.5750 0.6485 0.6362

The average for \(C=10\) is:

\[\overline B_{C=10}=\frac{0.6822+0.6390+0.5750+0.6485}{4}\approx0.6362\]

Each value was produced by fitting a new Pipeline with that fold's Training records and predicting only its Validation records. With three candidates and four folds, the search performs \(3\times4=12\) model fits, plus one final refit on the full development set when refit=True.

The best mean score is only slightly higher for \(C=10\) than for \(C=1\): approximately 0.0061, or 0.61 percentage points. That is not compelling evidence of a universal advantage. We report the winner by the predetermined criterion without pretending this numerical difference establishes statistical superiority. Test remains untouched.

17. Compare algorithms fairly: Logistic Regression versus Random Forest

Model Selection means comparing families of fitted models, not only numbers within one family. We also tune a Random Forest with 150 trees and six combinations of max_depth in {3, 7, None} and min_samples_leaf in {2, 8}. The same four CV folds and the same Balanced Accuracy metric are used as for Logistic Regression. We do not allow Random Forest access to a different Test sample or a more favorable scoring rule.

Candidate family Tuning design Best development CV Balanced Accuracy
Logistic Regression C = 0.1, 1, 10 0.6362 (C = 10)
Random Forest 3 depths × 2 leaf sizes 0.6051 (depth = 7, leaf = 2)

A defensible selection rule specified in advance is to choose the family with the higher mean Balanced Accuracy using equivalent development folds. Here, Logistic Regression wins. We could alternatively include model complexity, inference time, or an explicit minimum Recall constraint—but these criteria must be stated before Test is opened. A threshold chosen to maximize operational profit would also require a cost model and separate validation inside development.

This example does not prove Logistic Regression is generally better than Random Forest. We searched small finite grids, with synthetic data generated by a logistic mechanism, which naturally favors some linear structure. The result supports only our defined selection protocol. A larger search budget or a different underlying data-generating process could change the comparison.

18. Randomized Search: probability of exploring a good region

Randomized Search samples a fixed number of parameter configurations from specified distributions, instead of evaluating the full Cartesian product. This can save computation in wide search spaces containing both influential and less influential controls. It is not guaranteed to find the best possible candidate, but its budget is explicit and usually easier to scale.

Imagine 20 equally likely independent candidate draws, where three configurations lie in a region we would deem promising. The probability of not drawing a promising configuration once is \(17/20=0.85\). With eight independent draws with replacement, the probability of missing that region on all eight draws is:

\[P(\text{no success in 8 draws})=\left(\frac{17}{20}\right)^8\approx0.27249\]
\[P(\text{at least one success})=1-0.85^8\approx0.72751\]

So there is roughly a 72.75% chance of entering that promising region under these assumptions. This is not a guarantee that we select a good model: evaluating a candidate well requires reliable CV, and the 'promising region' itself is hypothetical. If we sample without replacement from a finite set, or use a nonuniform distribution, the arithmetic changes.

For Logistic Regression it is often sensible to sample \(C\) on a logarithmic scale (for example from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^2\)); sampling C uniformly between 0.001 and 100 would concentrate nearly all draws in the high-C part of that range. Choosing parameter distributions is itself an informed modeling decision, not a neutral technical detail.

19. Search budgets, computation and reproducibility

For a fixed Grid Search with \(J\) candidates and \(K\) folds, the approximate count of fits is \(J K\), plus any final refit. For Randomized Search with \(n_{iter}\) draws, it is \(n_{iter}K\). For example, six forest candidates and four folds require \(6\times4=24\) forest fits; three Logistic candidates require \(3\times4=12\). A fitted Random Forest contains many trees, so one 'fit' is more expensive than one modest Logistic Regression fit.

\[\text{Search fitting cost}\approx\text{Candidates}\times\text{CV folds}\times\text{Cost per fit}\]

Grid Search exhaustively covers its selected discrete grid, not the infinite set of all conceivable settings. Randomized Search provides broader coverage of some large spaces per unit cost, but may skip useful combinations. Other methods, including Bayesian Optimization and successive halving, use past results or partial resource allocation to guide new evaluations. Their existence does not eliminate the need for clean validation.

Document the train/test split seed, fold definitions, parameter values or sampling distributions, main metric, software version and decision rule. Use deterministic seeds when possible, but do not cherry-pick a random seed after inspecting scores. When comparing a broad search with a narrow search, be explicit about compute budgets so that the performance comparison does not silently reward a larger search allowance.

20. Why ordinary CV is not a full audit of the tuner

We must distinguish two targets of evaluation. Question A: “For this specific candidate \(C=10\), what is its performance under four-fold validation?” This is estimated by CV scores for that candidate. Question B: “If I use a workflow that selects C among several alternatives using development data, how well will that whole procedure perform on genuinely new data?” The second includes randomness and potential optimism induced by choosing the winner.

Ordinary GridSearchCV.best_score_ is computed from folds used to select the candidate. Reusing that value as an independent audit of the search is optimistic in general. An untouched Test split can provide one external assessment of the finally chosen procedure. Nested Cross-Validation provides repeated outer assessments without requiring the final Test to participate in any selection.

Nested CV has two different fold systems. The inner folds select Hyperparameters using only the outer Training subset. The outer validation fold evaluates the entire inner-tuned procedure, and is never used to choose a value of C for that outer iteration. If we let outer labels influence inner tuning, it stops being a valid outer holdout.

The key intellectual shift: in Nested CV, the “model” being evaluated is not just Logistic Regression; it is the complete algorithmic procedure preprocess → search → select → refit → predict. We will reproduce three outer evaluations next.

21. Nested CV: three complete outer-fold outcomes

Take the same 288 development customers, leaving the 72-customer Test completely sealed. Use three outer stratified folds: each iteration trains on 192 and assesses on 96 held-out development customers. Inside each outer Training subset, another three-fold CV searches \(C\in\{0.1,1,10\}\). The search then refits its winning C on all 192 outer-Training customers and predicts the 96 outer-Validation customers once.

Outer fold Inner-selected C Inner best Balanced Accuracy Outer Balanced Accuracy
1 10 0.6391 0.5659
2 1 0.6089 0.5530
3 10 0.5724 0.6318

The mean outer performance is:

\[\overline B_{outer}=\frac{0.565934+0.552958+0.631831}{3}\approx0.583574\]

The inner winners are often higher than the outer estimates, and the winning Hyperparameter changes between outer folds. That is exactly the kind of uncertainty this procedure reveals. The three outer outcomes are not confidence guarantees, and the difference between \(0.6362\) (ordinary tuning score) and \(0.5836\) (nested estimate) must not be interpreted as a universal constant bias correction: different training subset sizes and fold designs also influence results.

In a deployment workflow, after such an assessment, we can conduct the designated final search on all development data, refit the chosen configuration and then evaluate once on reserved Test. Nested CV estimates the selection procedure; it does not create three deployed models that must somehow be averaged.

Reconstruct one outer score from its predictions

Its confusion counts were TN = 55, FP = 15, FN = 17, TP = 9. These are predictions on the outer fold, not a Training Confusion Matrix.

Scope of this Nested CV must be stated precisely

The three outcomes above evaluate one algorithm family: a Logistic Regression Pipeline whose hyperparameter \(C\) is selected by an inner search. They do not independently evaluate the larger process that also compares Logistic Regression against Random Forest. To estimate that full two-family selector with Nested CV, each outer Training partition must run both searches inside the inner folds, choose a family and hyperparameters on inner evidence alone, refit the selected Pipeline on the outer Training partition, and score the untouched outer Validation partition once. Repeat for every outer fold. The 0.583574 result cannot be relabeled as a nested estimate for the combined two-family procedure. Our final reserved Test assesses the actually selected pipeline once, subject to its small sample size.

22. Uncertainty: what small score differences can support

The larger experiment's Logistic candidates achieved mean Balanced Accuracy 0.6301 for \(C=1\) and 0.6362 for \(C=10\). Their difference is approximately 0.0061. Both have fold-to-fold variation of several percentage points. Therefore it would be unjustified to announce that \(C=10\) is categorically better, even though it wins our prespecified rule. The search outcome is a decision under finite evidence, not a proof of a uniquely correct Hyperparameter.

In Nested CV, our three outer Balanced Accuracy values are 0.5659, 0.5530 and 0.6318. Their mean is about 0.5836. An empirical standard deviation describes variability across these three folds, but because folds use overlapping training observations, naive independent-normal confidence intervals are not automatically valid. For stronger claims about model differences, one may need repeated or grouped CV, appropriate resampling or statistical tests matched to the evaluation design, and an independently specified benchmark.

For real decision-making, questions are broader than score means: Is Churn Recall adequate? What is the cost per outreach? Does performance degrade for month-to-month customers? Are the predictions calibrated? Would a smaller, easier-to-maintain model perform comparably? These are legitimate selection criteria only if addressed transparently and without repurposing Test as a tuning source.

Best CV score means **best observed among the alternatives under this particular procedure**, not “best model in the world,” and not guaranteed future performance.

23. Final Test: verify the frozen selection once

After comparing the two algorithm families using development CV, we selected Logistic Regression and \(C=10\). Its Pipeline has now been refitted on all 288 development customers, including imputation, scaling, encoding and coefficient estimation. Only now do we make predictions for the 72 sealed Test customers. The observed Confusion Matrix has rows = actual outcome and columns = predicted outcome:

Compute Sensitivity (Churn Recall) \(=6/(6+13)=6/19\approx0.31579\). Specificity \(=48/(48+5)=48/53\approx0.90566\). Their average is:

\[B_{Test}=\frac{6/19+48/53}{2}\approx0.610725\]
\[Accuracy_{Test}=\frac{48+6}{72}=0.7500\]

The probabilities yield ROC-AUC ≈ 0.7031 on these same Test records. ROC-AUC assesses ranking across thresholds, while Balanced Accuracy above uses the current decision threshold (0.50). Do not confuse them, and do not pick a new threshold by looking at these Test labels.

We also report that the selected Random Forest candidate, evaluated for comparison only after the family-selection rule was fixed, has Test Balanced Accuracy ≈ 0.5204. That post-hoc comparison is descriptive; it does not change the selected winner or create another independent Test. In a strict operational release report we should emphasize the locked selection and avoid a series of fresh decisions based on Test.

Test Balanced Accuracy (0.6107) is lower than the selected model's mean development CV score (0.6362). This is an ordinary possibility of generalization, not a reason to secretly redo the search.

24. When StratifiedKFold is the wrong CV design

We used StratifiedKFold because our synthetic customers were generated as independent rows and because the 1-class is relatively uncommon. Stratification helps maintain similar class proportions; it does not repair dependence between observations. In real telecom data, records may come from the same household, business account or subscriber observed in multiple months.

If the same subscriber appears in both Training and Validation, a model can appear to generalize while merely recognizing customer-specific patterns. GroupKFold or another group-aware strategy holds all records of a subscriber or household together. With temporal prediction, the model should train on the past and validate on later periods; shuffled folds can unintentionally allow future information into historical predictions. Use forward-chaining or time-aware validation, with a gap when delayed labels or rolling features require one.

Deployment question Suitable evaluation principle
New independent customers from similar distribution StratifiedKFold may be appropriate
Entirely new households or organizations Group-aware holdout
Churn next quarter using last year's data Forward-time validation
Monthly repeated observations for the same accounts Respect both temporal and entity dependencies

A pipeline cannot cure an invalid split design. The order matters: define the prediction time and entity, then choose folds, then place preprocessing inside each fold. RandomizedSearchCV and GridSearchCV both inherit whatever invalid or valid CV strategy we pass them.

25. Reproduce everything in Python: pipeline, searches and Nested CV

The following is a complete, executable study lab using NumPy, pandas and scikit-learn. No CSV download is required: the dataset is generated in the code. The fixed random generators make the results repeatable in compatible library environments. Minor numerical differences across versions may arise from solver/library changes; the logic of the leakage-free procedure is invariant.

Read it in stages: (1) generate customers and their synthetic Target; (2) reserve Test before computing preprocessing statistics; (3) define numeric and categorical preprocessing; (4) run the Logistic and Random Forest searches on development only; (5) use an outer loop to estimate how the tuner generalizes; and (6) evaluate the frozen selection on Test. The code also prints the fold scores to make it possible to compare them with the tables in this chapter.

import numpy as np,pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split,StratifiedKFold, GridSearchCV,cross_validate
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder,StandardScaler
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import balanced_accuracy_score,roc_auc_score,confusion_matrix,accuracy_score
rng=np.random.default_rng(2205)
n=360
tenure=rng.integers(2,73,n)
tickets=rng.poisson(2.3,n)
drop=rng.normal(12,8,n).clip(0,40)
contract=rng.choice(['Month-to-month','One-year','Two-year'],n,p=[.50,.30,.20])
autopay=rng.choice(['Yes','No'],n,p=[.63,.37])
bill=rng.normal(74,17,n).clip(28,139)
logit=-2.35+.050*drop+.38*tickets-.021*tenure+np.where(contract=='Month-to-month',.85,np.where(contract=='One-year',.2,-.5))+np.where(autopay=='No',.32,0)+.009*(bill-75)
prob=1/(1+np.exp(-logit))
y=rng.binomial(1,prob)
X=pd.DataFrame({'tenure_months':tenure,'support_tickets':tickets,'usage_drop_pct':drop,'contract':contract,'autopay':autopay,'monthly_bill_usd':bill})
# introduce missingness only for training-compatible handling
X.loc[rng.choice(n,18,replace=False),'usage_drop_pct']=np.nan
X_dev,X_test,y_dev,y_test=train_test_split(X,y,test_size=.20,random_state=22,stratify=y)
num=['tenure_months','support_tickets','usage_drop_pct','monthly_bill_usd'];cat=['contract','autopay']
prep=ColumnTransformer([('num',Pipeline([('imputer',SimpleImputer(strategy='median')),('scaler',StandardScaler())]),num),('cat',OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown='ignore'),cat)])
pipe=Pipeline([('prep',prep),('model',LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000,random_state=22))])
params={'model__C':[.1,1,10]}
cv=StratifiedKFold(n_splits=4,shuffle=True,random_state=22)
search=GridSearchCV(pipe,params,scoring='balanced_accuracy',cv=cv,refit=True,return_train_score=True,n_jobs=1)
search.fit(X_dev,y_dev)
print('samples',len(X_dev),len(X_test),'churn rates',np.mean(y_dev),np.mean(y_test))
print('class count',np.unique(y_dev,return_counts=True),np.unique(y_test,return_counts=True))
print('scores')
for i,p in enumerate(search.cv_results_['params']):
 print(p, [round(search.cv_results_[f'split{j}_test_score'][i],4) for j in range(4)],round(search.cv_results_['mean_test_score'][i],6), round(search.cv_results_['std_test_score'][i],6))
print('best',search.best_params_,search.best_score_)
y_pred=search.predict(X_test)
print('test score',balanced_accuracy_score(y_test,y_pred), 'accuracy',accuracy_score(y_test,y_pred),'roc auc',roc_auc_score(y_test,search.predict_proba(X_test)[:,1]),'conf',confusion_matrix(y_test,y_pred))
# compare rf with small grid
forest=Pipeline([('prep',prep),('model',RandomForestClassifier(random_state=22,n_estimators=150))]);rfsearch=GridSearchCV(forest,{'model__max_depth':[3,7,None],'model__min_samples_leaf':[2,8]},cv=cv,scoring='balanced_accuracy',n_jobs=1)
rfsearch.fit(X_dev,y_dev)
print('RF best',rfsearch.best_params_,rfsearch.best_score_)

outer=StratifiedKFold(n_splits=3,shuffle=True,random_state=71)
inner=StratifiedKFold(n_splits=3,shuffle=True,random_state=72)

What GridSearchCV is doing behind the scenes

For each candidate \(C\), it clones the entire Pipeline four times. Each clone learns imputations, scales, categories and Logistic coefficients only from that fold's Training subset. It then scores the held-out fold, averages the four values and identifies the candidate with the highest mean. Because refit=True, a new final Pipeline is fitted on the complete development set. That is the object used to call predict(X_test).

An alternative: RandomizedSearchCV

Here is a concrete, executable alternative after running the complete lab above. The log-uniform distribution samples small and large C values across orders of magnitude:

from scipy.stats import loguniform
from sklearn.model_selection import RandomizedSearchCV
random_search = RandomizedSearchCV(
    estimator=pipe,
    param_distributions={'model__C': loguniform(1e-3, 1e2)},
    n_iter=8,
    cv=cv,
    scoring='balanced_accuracy',
    random_state=22,
    refit=True,
    n_jobs=1,
)
random_search.fit(X_dev, y_dev)
print(random_search.best_params_, random_search.best_score_)

Keep the Pipeline as the estimator and leave Test untouched. Random Search has a bounded budget; its name alone does not prevent leakage or selection optimism.

26. Audit a flawed workflow—and repair it completely

Consider the following incorrect sequence: merge development and Test; fit the imputer and scaler on all rows; run a Grid Search; inspect Test scores for several parameter grids; keep whichever configuration has the highest Test number; publish that number as independent evidence. There are three different failures: preprocessing has learned from future evaluation Features; Test influenced model selection; and the published score was optimized on the very sample presented as independent.

A corrected protocol is:

  1. Define the prediction question, unit, timestamp and primary metric before modeling.
  2. Reserve Test and record exactly which customer IDs it contains.
  3. Choose folds appropriate to classes, groups or time. Put every learned preprocessing step inside the Pipeline.
  4. Use CV to select candidates without seeing Test. If assessing the entire search procedure, use Nested CV or an external audit set.
  5. Refit the selected Pipeline using all permitted development rows.
  6. Evaluate Test once. Record mistakes and limitations. Do not retune based on those outcomes and still claim the same Test is untouched.

A subtle failure is to try a new search space after seeing an unsatisfactory Test. At that moment Test has become part of the development process. To claim a new independent assessment, we need fresh, genuinely unconsulted evaluation data, not merely a different random seed on the same Test. Changes driven by live business monitoring likewise require careful temporal assessment before deployment.

A clean split alone is insufficient. Every action informed by held-out labels—including threshold choice, feature choice and error-driven grid redesign—must be counted as part of model development.

27. Common mistakes: diagnose the mechanism, not just the symptom

1. Reporting Training as generalization. Training labels helped fit the model; evaluation on them is optimistically familiar. Remedy: validation folds and an untouched Test.

2. Calling Test “Validation.” If Test is used repeatedly for tuning, it is development, not independent evaluation. Remedy: reserve another future sample for a fresh audit.

3. Fitting StandardScaler before splitting folds. Validation Features influence means and scales. Remedy: put the Scaler inside the CV Pipeline.

4. One-hot encoding the two partitions separately. Columns may not align. Remedy: fit OneHotEncoder inside Training and transform held-out data with stored categories.

5. Changing the scoring metric after looking at scores. The winner then benefits from metric selection. Remedy: define primary metric and secondary diagnostics in advance.

6. Treating CV folds as independent experiments. Training samples overlap. Remedy: interpret fold spread descriptively and use an appropriate uncertainty design.

7. Confusing Grid Search with universal optimization. The winner is only best among tried candidates. Remedy: disclose the grid and budget.

8. Assuming Random Search always wins. It can miss useful regions. Remedy: analyze parameter distributions and budget, then validate choices fairly.

9. Picking the luckiest random seed. A seed acts like another tuned control. Remedy: prespecify seeds or use a principled repeated-design evaluation.

10. Refitting on Test before final evaluation. It destroys the independent holdout. Remedy: refit on development only.

11. Retuning thresholds on Test. Even with fixed coefficients, choosing a threshold using Test labels is selection. Remedy: select the threshold inside development.

12. Using shuffled CV for forecasts. Future data can leak into earlier training. Remedy: forward-time validation.

13. Splitting related accounts across folds. Customer identity can leak across records. Remedy: GroupKFold or entity-aware separation.

14. Interpreting best_score_ as an external audit. It helped pick the candidate. Remedy: Nested CV or reserved Test.

15. Declaring the algorithm with higher CV mean universally superior. Sampling variation and search budget matter. Remedy: qualify results by data, metric, grid, and uncertainty.

16. Ignoring practical deployment costs. Balanced Accuracy cannot measure retention profitability alone. Remedy: include operating constraints and a defensible cost model in future validation.

28. Practice and transfer: 20 solved questions

The exercises are ordered from direct numerical reproduction to auditing decisions. Try each answer before expanding its solution. The objective is not to repeat definitions, but to demonstrate that you can trace data through the selection process and identify when an evaluation loses its independence.

1. Compute accuracy for 12 correct predictions out of 15.

Accuracy is 12/15 = 0.80. The numerator counts all correct predictions and the denominator includes every held-out observation. This does not quantify class-specific performance.

2. Why cannot the target label of a held-out customer be used as a neighbor?

The label is the outcome being predicted. Including that customer in fitting allows their answer to influence their own prediction, invalidating the held-out evaluation.

3. Find the k=1 three-fold average Accuracy.

Fold scores are 3/5=0.60, 3/5=0.60, and 2/5=0.40. Mean = (0.60+0.60+0.40)/3 = 0.5333. Thus k=1 performs worse than k=7 under this protocol.

4. Why does k=7 with uniform voting win the manual search?

Its fold accuracies are 0.80, 0.80, and 0.80. The mean is 0.80, exceeding the means of the other nine tested configurations. This is a win within the finite search grid, not universal optimality.

5. Count the fits for five neighbor choices, two voting rules, and three folds.

There are 5×2=10 configurations and 10×3=30 fold fits. If the procedure refits the winning configuration on all development data, add one final fit, for a total of 31.

6. Interpret the small Test set confusion for k=7.

Predictions are 0,0,1,1,1 and actual labels 0,0,0,1,1. Thus TN=2, FP=1, FN=0, TP=2; Accuracy=(2+2)/5=0.80. One additional mistake would reduce Accuracy by 20 percentage points.

7. A fold learns mean 20 and standard deviation 8.165. Standardize Validation value 100.

Use frozen Training quantities: z=(100−20)/8.165≈9.798. Recalculate neither mean nor scale on Validation; doing so changes the measurement reference and leaks fold information.

8. Why is C=10 preferred over C=1 in the specified search, and what can we NOT conclude?

The CV means are 0.6362 and 0.6301, so C=10 wins the predefined maximum-mean criterion by about 0.0061. That small gap is not proof that it is universally better or even materially better for future populations.

9. Explain why C and a Logistic coefficient are different quantities.

C is fixed by the model-selection procedure and controls inverse regularization. A coefficient is learned by minimizing penalized loss on each fold Training subset. Changing C can change the learned coefficient values.

10. Compute 1−(17/20)^8 and state its assumptions.

The result is about 0.72751 or 72.75%. It assumes eight independent draws with replacement, each with a 3/20 chance of entering the hypothetical promising region. It does not guarantee model quality.

11. What is selection optimism if 20 models have identical true performance?

The maximum of 20 noisy observed validation scores tends to exceed their common true performance even when each individual score is unbiased. The winning model often won partly because of favorable noise.

12. Describe what the outer Validation fold is forbidden to influence.

It cannot choose hyperparameters, fit scalers/imputers, choose features, revise the search grid, or select a decision threshold inside that outer iteration. Its role is evaluating the already inner-tuned procedure.

13. Compute the mean nested outer score from 0.565934, 0.552958 and 0.631831.

Sum = 1.750723; dividing by 3 gives 0.583574 (rounded). This summarizes three outer assessments, not a certified probability of success for every future customer.

14. Compute Test Balanced Accuracy from TN=48, FP=5, FN=13, TP=6.

Churn Recall=6/(6+13)=6/19≈0.31579. Specificity=48/(48+5)=48/53≈0.90566. Average=(0.31579+0.90566)/2≈0.610725.

15. Does 75% Test Accuracy imply acceptable churn detection?

Not necessarily. Although 54/72 labels are correct, only 6/19 churn cases were caught (Recall ≈31.6%). Whether that suffices depends on miss costs, intervention resources and a prespecified operational objective.

16. Choose an evaluation design for the same customer observed each month.

Do not randomly spread months for the same customer across CV. Use group- and time-aware separation that reflects forecasting future months for unseen dates or entities; keep preprocessing and labels temporally valid.

17. A colleague tries a new grid after a disappointing Test score. Can the same Test still be an independent final audit?

No. Test feedback has informed development, making the reused sample part of selection. Freeze the procedure and obtain genuinely unseen future evaluation data for a new independent audit.

18. Propose one future Jupyter exercise that tests transfer beyond memorization.

Change the synthetic churn rate or introduce repeated customer IDs, then choose a new CV design before fitting. Compare inner and outer scores and explain how the changed data structure alters what generalization means.

19. Calculate the paired fold advantage of C = 10 over C = 1 and interpret its size.

Use the identical four Validation folds from Section 16. Their score differences (C = 10 minus C = 1) are 0, +0.0169, −0.0094, +0.0169, so the mean is \((0+0.0169-0.0094+0.0169)/4=0.0061\), or 0.61 percentage points. The sample standard deviation of the differences is about 0.01305 (1.31 percentage points). Because Fold 3 favors C = 1 and folds share Training records, this small average difference does not prove that C = 10 performs better on future customers. The prespecified search rule still selects C = 10; a new inference requires appropriately independent evidence.

20. The company also chooses between Logistic Regression and Random Forest. What exactly must move inside Nested CV?

For each of the three outer folds, reserve 96 customers as Outer Validation and give the remaining 192 to inner tuning. On those 192, use the same appropriate three inner folds to search all three Logistic C values and all six Random Forest configurations with Balanced Accuracy. Thus, there are \(3(3+6)=27\) inner candidate-fold fits per outer round, or \(3\times27=81\) over three outer rounds, excluding refits. Compare the two families' inner winning scores, select one family and its settings, refit only that winner on 192 and evaluate the 96 outer cases. Do not use those 96 labels to pick a family. The existing three-fold Nested CV score of 0.583574 evaluates Logistic C selection only, so it cannot be presented as the verified result of the combined algorithm-family selector.

If you can solve the computations but cannot explain why Test remains untouched, revisit the distinction between selecting candidates and evaluating the selection procedure. Conversely, a good conceptual explanation without reproducible fold calculations is not yet complete mastery. Both are required.

29. Synthesis, glossary and a reproducible future lab

Synthesis. We began with 15 customers and verified every k-NN candidate by holding out observations. We then moved to 360 synthetic customers and compared classifiers using identical development partitions. Grid Search selected Logistic Regression with \(C=10\); Nested CV provided a more stringent view of the selection process; an untouched Test delivered Balanced Accuracy ≈ 0.6107, lower than the winning CV mean. None of these numbers are contradictions: they estimate different things using different information.

Glossary. Parameter: learned model quantity, such as a coefficient. Hyperparameter: externally chosen control such as k, C or tree depth. Cross-Validation: repeated internal holdout assessment. Grid Search: evaluation of a specified parameter grid. Randomized Search: random samples from parameter distributions. Stratification: balancing class proportions across splits. Refit: fit the selected configuration again on all permitted development data. Nested CV: inner selection and independent outer evaluation. Data Leakage: evaluation information inappropriately influences fitting or selection. Selection Bias: optimism induced by selecting the best of noisy evaluations. Balanced Accuracy: mean class-wise sensitivity and specificity for binary labels.
