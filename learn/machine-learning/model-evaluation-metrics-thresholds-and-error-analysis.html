1. Evaluation asks a question about the future, not merely the past

A municipal water utility receives sensor alerts that may indicate a confirmed pipe-leak event. Inspecting every alert wastes crew time, but failing to inspect a genuine leak can delay repairs. The utility wants a prediction system that ranks inspections for human review. The model does not independently authorize water shutoffs, declare water safe, or replace engineering inspection. This distinction keeps the example operationally credible: a statistical prediction supports a decision, but is not itself proof of a physical leak.

Model Evaluation asks whether a predictive system generalizes to observations not used to learn or select it, how it fails, and whether those errors are compatible with the intended decision. Three quantities that often get mixed up must be separated from the outset: a score is the model's numerical output; a predicted class results after applying a Decision Threshold; and an action is a policy choice that can also depend on crew capacity, safety procedures, and costs. A high score is not a measured leak and a perfect score on Training is not evidence about future sensors.

We will examine a small, deliberately synthetic Validation dataset with 12 sites and a separate eight-site Test dataset, all printed below. The probabilities are illustrative outputs from a previously frozen candidate model: we do not claim to have fitted or calibrated that model from these 20 observations. That separation allows us to focus on evaluating predictions, rather than silently inventing coefficients. A short second example uses aircraft de-icing duration to teach Regression metrics; both problems require held-out evaluation, but their errors have different meanings.

By the end, the learner should be able to reconstruct a Confusion Matrix; calculate Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity and F1; examine Class Imbalance; construct ROC and Precision–Recall summaries; compute ROC-AUC, Average Precision, Brier Score and Log Loss; choose thresholds under explicit constraints; evaluate regression errors; and explain why a small, tidy evaluation set is not a guarantee of real-world performance.

3. Before metrics, distinguish a baseline, a model and a policy

A Baseline is a clear reference such as “flag no segments,” “inspect every segment,” or an existing engineering screening rule. It is not necessarily another fitted Machine Learning algorithm. The constant flag-none baseline on Validation labels everyone as 0: it identifies zero leaks, correctly ignores eight sites and misses four. Its Accuracy is \(8/12=66.67\%\), even though its Recall is 0%. This is already enough to see that one headline number cannot summarize practical usefulness.

The candidate model provides 12 numerical scores. A threshold of 0.50 flags sites with \(p\geq0.50\). That creates the list V1–V5, including V2 and V4, whose actual outcomes are 0. Scores alone do not specify which intervention is appropriate; in a water utility, a high-priority alert could mean a scheduled engineering inspection, not automatically an excavation.

We will not describe a “cost saving” as observed unless a real intervention study measures it. A decision-cost table can help compare policies conditionally on assumptions, but whether inspections prevent damage, how crews respond, and what happens to customers require information beyond a Confusion Matrix.

4. Construct a Confusion Matrix from the actual twelve rows

A Confusion Matrix cross-tabulates predicted class and actual class. With a positive class defined as “confirmed leak,” every observation goes into exactly one of four groups. True Positive (TP): a leak flagged; False Positive (FP): no leak flagged; False Negative (FN): a leak not flagged; True Negative (TN): no leak not flagged. The words “positive” and “negative” refer to the class being predicted, not whether the outcome is desirable.

Apply \(p\geq0.50\). V1, V3 and V5 are TP = 3. V2 and V4 are FP = 2. V7 is a missed leak, so FN = 1. V6 and V8–V12 are correctly unflagged, giving TN = 6. Always check \(TP+FP+FN+TN=3+2+1+6=12\). No case disappears because it is inconvenient.

Actual outcome Predicted leak (1) Predicted no leak (0) Row total
Confirmed leak (1) TP = 3 FN = 1 4
No leak (0) FP = 2 TN = 6 8
Column total 5 7 12

For V7, \(p=0.35\) is not “incorrect” as a mathematical number simply because the realized label is 1: probability models are uncertain by construction. The classification decision is a False Negative under our chosen threshold. To assess the score itself we also need proper probability measures such as Brier Score and Log Loss.

5. Derive Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity and F1 with their denominators

Different metrics condition on different groups. Write each denominator in words before substituting numbers. Accuracy asks: of all sites, how many labels were correct? Precision asks: of all sites the policy flagged, how many really leaked? Recall, also called Sensitivity or True Positive Rate, asks: of all actual leaks, how many were flagged? Specificity, or True Negative Rate, asks: of sites without leaks, how many were correctly ignored?

\[\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{N}=\frac{3+6}{12}=0.75\]
\[\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}=\frac35=0.60,\qquad \mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}=\frac34=0.75\]
\[\mathrm{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}=\frac68=0.75,\qquad \mathrm{FPR}=1-\mathrm{Specificity}=\frac28=0.25\]

F1 is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. It rewards having both reasonably high but does not include the utility's actual costs. Substitute \(P=0.6\) and \(R=0.75\):

\[F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}=\frac{2(0.60)(0.75)}{0.60+0.75}=\frac{0.90}{1.35}=0.6667\]

Balanced Accuracy is the average of Recall and Specificity: \((0.75+0.75)/2=0.75\). It gives equal class-level weight to detecting leaks and correctly rejecting nonleaks regardless of prevalence. If \(TP+FP=0\), Precision's denominator is zero; the mathematical quantity is undefined. Some software reports 0 by convention, so the convention must be recorded rather than passed off as a measured precision.

6. Why Class Imbalance changes the story: a 10,000-site calculation

The Validation sample has 33.3% positives. To see why that is atypically convenient, imagine a different hypothetical population of 10,000 independent checks: 100 leaks and 9,900 nonleaks (1% prevalence). Suppose a screening system has 80% Sensitivity and 95% Specificity in this population. The assumptions imply \(TP=0.80(100)=80\), \(FN=20\), \(TN=0.95(9900)=9405\), and \(FP=9900-9405=495\).

Outcome Predicted positive Predicted negative Actual total
Leak 80 20 100
No leak 495 9,405 9,900
Total 575 9,425 10,000

The model has Precision \(80/575\approx13.91\%\) despite 80% Recall and 95% Specificity. The reason is arithmetic, not a flaw in the definition of Precision: even a 5% false-positive rate applied to 9,900 nonleaks creates many more false alerts than 80 correctly detected leaks. The “flag none” baseline achieves 99% Accuracy (9,900/10,000) but detects none of the 100 leaks; our hypothetical detector's Accuracy is \((80+9405)/10000=94.85\%\), lower but operationally different.

Equivalently, Bayes' rule connects prevalence, Sensitivity and Specificity: \(P(\mathrm{leak}\mid\mathrm{flag})=\frac{0.8(0.01)}{0.8(0.01)+0.05(0.99)}=0.1391\). This illustrative calculation assumes the rates transfer to the new setting. They frequently do not: base rates, sensors, weather and labeling can change. Always report prevalence alongside Precision.

7. Decision Thresholds: repeat the count at three cutoffs

A Decision Threshold converts a probability score into a class, without altering the score itself. Under the convention \(\widehat y=1\) when \(p\geq t\), lowering \(t\) generally flags more sites. It cannot guarantee higher Precision: false alerts may enter the selected set. We compare three prespecified thresholds to avoid pretending that the best-looking Test cutoff is an unbiased discovery.

At \(t=0.80\), only V1 and V2 are flagged: \(TP=1,FP=1,FN=3,TN=7\). At \(t=0.50\), our existing counts are \((3,2,1,6)\). At \(t=0.30\), V1–V7 are flagged: \((4,3,0,5)\). The numbers follow directly from the twelve printed rows.

Threshold TP FP FN TN Precision Recall Flags
0.80 1 1 3 7 1/2 = 50% 1/4 = 25% 2
0.50 3 2 1 6 3/5 = 60% 3/4 = 75% 5
0.30 4 3 0 5 4/7 = 57.14% 4/4 = 100% 7

There is no universally superior cutoff. At 0.30 the policy captures all four leaks but asks engineers to examine seven sites. At 0.80 the workload is only two sites but three actual leaks are missed. The appropriate choice depends on how flagged sites will be acted upon and what level of risk and workload is acceptable.

8. Compare policies using explicit costs and a binding capacity constraint

Suppose, strictly for instruction, that a needless inspection (FP) incurs $12 in incremental decision cost, while a missed leak (FN) incurs a $90 penalty. This simplified accounting assigns zero cost to TP and TN and does not measure true physical damage, customer consequences, or the benefits of a repair. It must not be presented as estimated savings.

\[C(t)=12\,FP(t)+90\,FN(t)\]

For \(t=.80\), \(C=12(1)+90(3)=\$282\). For \(t=.50\), \(C=12(2)+90(1)=\$114\). For \(t=.30\), \(C=12(3)+90(0)=\$36\). If cost were the only requirement, 0.30 would be preferred on Validation. But assume the duty team can investigate at most five sites per comparable batch. The lower cutoff flags seven, so it is infeasible. Among these three prespecified candidates, 0.50 is the cheapest feasible threshold on Validation.

This reveals an essential decision distinction: the best numerical classifier and the best feasible operating policy may differ. A richer policy might rank exactly five sites by score rather than choose a global threshold, but this requires capacity-aware validation and tie-breaking rules. The cost assumptions should undergo Sensitivity Analysis; they are not causal evidence that flags prevent any loss.

9. From one Confusion Matrix to an ROC Curve

One threshold produces one pair of coordinates: False Positive Rate on the horizontal axis and True Positive Rate (Recall) on the vertical axis. A Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve varies the threshold through all distinct scores, beginning with no flagged cases \((0,0)\) and ending with all flagged \((1,1)\).

For our descending scores, when V1 (positive) first enters, the point is \((0,1/4)\). When V2 (negative) enters, it becomes \((1/8,1/4)\). When V3 (positive) enters, it becomes \((1/8,2/4)\). Continue down the rankings. After V7 has entered, four positives and three negatives are flagged: the point is \((3/8,4/4)=(0.375,1)\). The remaining five negatives move horizontally until the final point \((1,1)\).

ROC visualizes a ranking trade-off over thresholds. It does not directly tell us inspection workload, expected Precision at a new prevalence, or whether scores are calibrated probabilities. A curve above the diagonal represents better ranking than random guessing on the evaluated sample, but very small samples produce jagged, uncertain curves.

10. Calculate ROC-AUC from positive–negative pairs, not a black box

The Area Under the ROC Curve (ROC-AUC) has a useful equivalent interpretation when there are no tied scores: it is the fraction of randomly selected positive–negative pairs where the positive receives the higher score. Our dataset contains four positives and eight negatives: \(4\times8=32\) such pairs.

For positive V1 (score .95), all eight negatives have lower scores, yielding 8 favorable comparisons. For positive V3 (.75), seven negatives have lower scores (only V2 at .85 is higher). For positive V5 (.55), six negatives have lower scores (V2 and V4 are higher). For positive V7 (.35), five negatives have lower scores (V2, V4 and V6 are higher). Thus:

\[\mathrm{ROC\!\!\text{-}AUC}=\frac{8+7+6+5}{4\times8}=\frac{26}{32}=0.8125\]

This is not an 81.25% Accuracy and not the probability that any particular site leaks. It summarizes pairwise ranking in the evaluated sample, across all score thresholds. AUC is 0.5 for a randomly ordered population in expectation; 1.0 signifies perfect separation of the observed positives and negatives. Ties are customarily assigned half-credit in the pairwise calculation, a detail students should check when reproducing software output.

11. Precision–Recall Curves and Average Precision (AP)

ROC's FPR denominator includes all negatives, which may be enormous when events are rare. A Precision–Recall (PR) Curve makes a different trade-off visible: of the alerts issued, what fraction were genuine (Precision), and what fraction of genuine events were caught (Recall)? For rare-event screening it is often more directly related to review workload, although ROC remains useful for ranking comparisons.

Process the ranked list from highest score to lowest. Precision changes after every selected site, but Recall increases only when a genuine positive enters. At those four increases, the relevant Precision values are: V1 at rank 1 gives \(1/1=1\); V3 at rank 3 gives \(2/3\); V5 at rank 5 gives \(3/5\); V7 at rank 7 gives \(4/7\). With four positives, each positive increases Recall by \(1/4\).

Using scikit-learn's Average Precision (AP) convention, the area-like summary weights Precision by the increases in Recall:

\[AP=\sum_k (R_k-R_{k-1})P_k=\frac14\left(1+\frac23+\frac35+\frac47\right)\approx0.70952\]

AP is not automatically identical to trapezoidal interpolation of the PR points. Those algorithms can produce different numerical areas, so report which summary you used. In a random-ranking reference with stable prevalence, AP is related to the positive-class proportion, but small finite-sample behavior requires care. Our Validation prevalence is 33.33%; one should not project this AP to a 1%-prevalence system without a representative new evaluation.

12. Probability Calibration is a different property from ranking

A ranking model can put leaks above nonleaks yet systematically assign probabilities that are too high. Calibration asks whether groups assigned an estimated probability near \(p\) experience approximately a \(p\) fraction of positive outcomes over repeated comparable observations. A forecast of 0.80 means “among many truly comparable cases assigned 0.80, about 80% should be positive,” not that any particular segment is 80% physically damaged.

To illustrate a reliability table, divide the Validation scores into three prespecified bins. High \([0.70,1]\) contains V1–V3: average score \((.95+.85+.75)/3=.85\), with two leaks out of three (66.67%). Medium \([0.30,0.70)\) contains V4–V7: average \((.65+.55+.45+.35)/4=.50\), with two leaks out of four (50%). Low \([0,0.30)\) contains V8–V12: average \((.25+.15+.10+.05+.02)/5=.114\), with zero leaks out of five.

Score band Segments Mean forecast Observed leak fraction
High 3 0.850 2/3 = 0.667
Medium 4 0.500 2/4 = 0.500
Low 5 0.114 0/5 = 0.000

The mismatch in the high bin is visible but cannot establish miscalibration with three sites. Reliability plots should include bin sizes and uncertainty; a dozen outcomes are inadequate to certify a model's probability quality. Calibration methods such as Platt scaling or Isotonic Regression must themselves be fitted on suitable development/calibration data, not tuned using the final Test labels.

13. Calculate the Brier Score over every site

The Brier Score for Binary Classification is the mean squared error between predicted probability \(p_i\) and binary outcome \(y_i\): \(\mathrm{Brier}=\frac1n\sum_i(p_i-y_i)^2\). Because the outcome is 0 or 1, the individual score ranges from 0 (perfect prediction of the realized label) to 1 (maximal error); smaller is better. Unlike Accuracy, Brier uses the full probability, not an arbitrary classification threshold.

For V1, \(p=.95,y=1\), so \((.95-1)^2=.0025\). For V2, \(p=.85,y=0\), so \((.85-0)^2=.7225\): an overconfident wrong prediction receives a substantial penalty. For V7, \(p=.35,y=1\), so \((.35-1)^2=.4225\). All twelve squared contributions are:

\[.0025+.7225+.0625+.4225+.2025+.2025+.4225+.0625+.0225+.0100+.0025+.0004=2.1354\]
\[\boxed{\mathrm{Brier}_{Validation}=2.1354/12=0.17795}\]

That score alone cannot tell us whether ranking or calibration is responsible for an error; additional analysis is required. A constant probabilistic baseline using the Training event prevalence would be an appropriate comparison if Training prevalence were supplied. We should not invent that Training rate from these Validation labels and call it a legitimate fitted baseline.

14. Calculate Binary Log Loss and interpret its sensitivity

Binary Log Loss, also called Binary Cross-Entropy, evaluates the probability assigned to the actual label. For each case with \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\), the contribution is \(-[y_i\log(p_i)+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)]\); \(\log\) here means the natural logarithm. A truly positive observation contributes \(-\log(p_i)\); a negative contributes \(-\log(1-p_i)\). The mean is the reported Loss.

For V1 (positive, \(p=.95\)), \(-\log(.95)\approx.051293\). For V2 (negative, \(p=.85\)), \(-\log(.15)\approx1.897120\), far worse. For V7 (positive, \(p=.35\)), \(-\log(.35)\approx1.049822\).

The twelve individual values sum to approximately 6.158471, giving:

\[\boxed{\mathrm{LogLoss}_{Validation}=\frac{6.158471}{12}\approx0.513206}\]

Why use the logarithm? A confident error receives increasingly severe penalties: if a negative outcome is assigned \(p=.99\), its Loss is \(-\log(.01)\approx4.605\); with \(p=.90\) it is \(-\log(.10)\approx2.303\). In exact arithmetic, \(p=1,y=0\) has infinite Log Loss. Software often clips extreme probabilities for numerical stability and should disclose that behavior where relevant. Log Loss is a proper probabilistic scoring rule, unlike Accuracy, which discards the magnitude of the score after a threshold is applied.

15. Ranking, probability quality and actions: three separate evaluations

Imagine two models that rank the twelve sites in exactly the same order, but one outputs probabilities concentrated near 0.5 and the other gives extreme values near 0 and 1. ROC-AUC and AP can remain unchanged under a strictly increasing transformation of scores, because the ranking stays identical. Yet Brier Score and Log Loss can change substantially because they evaluate probability magnitudes. And a fixed threshold such as 0.50 may yield different classifications if the scores are transformed.

That is why a single “best model” label is incomplete. Ask three different questions: Ranking—do true leaks tend to have higher scores? Probability quality—are predicted probabilities credible over many comparable outcomes? Decision quality—given a specified threshold and operational policy, how many true and false alerts result, at what workload and assumed cost? An algorithm can be competitive on one and weaker on another.

For our Validation data the point estimates are: ROC-AUC = 0.8125; AP ≈ 0.70952; Brier = 0.17795; Log Loss ≈ 0.513206; at threshold 0.50, Precision = 0.60 and Recall = 0.75. These statistics describe distinct aspects of the same twelve synthetic predictions, not independent confirmations of robustness. They should be accompanied by label-quality review, uncertainty and comparison with useful baselines.

16. A second evaluation problem: Regression predicts quantities, not labels

Classification measures mistakes in categories or probability forecasts. Regression predicts a numerical Target and requires different metrics. Consider an airline operations team estimating the minutes needed to de-ice each aircraft in a scheduled batch. The purpose of the illustrative forecast is planning ground-crew capacity, not compromising de-icing standards. The six held-out observations below contain actual minutes and predictions from a previously trained, frozen candidate; the training records are not used to calculate any new parameters here.

Flight Actual minutes \(y\) Predicted minutes \(\widehat y\) Error \(\widehat y-y\) Absolute error Squared error
F1 10 12 +2 2 4
F2 14 13 −1 1 1
F3 18 20 +2 2 4
F4 22 21 −1 1 1
F5 26 27 +1 1 1
F6 30 34 +4 4 16
Total 120 127 +7 11 27

Here positive error means overprediction, because we deliberately define \(e_i=\widehat y_i-y_i\). Other textbooks define Residuals as \(y_i-\widehat y_i\); both are valid if kept consistent. Signed errors can cancel. Notice that the total signed error is +7 minutes, but the total absolute error is 11 minutes. Evaluation needs penalties that do not allow good and bad signs to hide one another.

17. Derive MAE, MSE, RMSE and R² from all six Regression rows

Mean Absolute Error (MAE) averages the magnitudes of errors. It stays in the same units as the Target: \(\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{2+1+2+1+1+4}{6}=\frac{11}{6}\approx1.8333\) minutes. Mean Squared Error (MSE) averages squared errors: \(\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{4+1+4+1+1+16}{6}=\frac{27}{6}=4.5\) minutes². Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) takes the square root: \(\sqrt{4.5}\approx2.1213\) minutes.

Because MSE squares errors, the +4-minute error contributes 16 of the 27 total squared-error units—about \(16/27=59.26\%\) of SSE—despite accounting for only one of six observations. Squaring makes RMSE more sensitive than MAE to large misses. This may be desirable when large scheduling errors are especially costly, but the choice must follow the application rather than aesthetic preference.

The coefficient of determination compares SSE to the spread of observed Target values around their evaluation-set mean. The mean is \(\bar y=(10+14+18+22+26+30)/6=20\) minutes. Its total sum of squares is \(SST=(10-20)^2+(14-20)^2+\cdots+(30-20)^2=280\) minutes². With \(SSE=27\):

\[R^2=1-\frac{SSE}{SST}=1-\frac{27}{280}\approx0.90357\]

R² is not a percentage of individual predictions that are correct. It may be negative on held-out data when predictions are worse than predicting the held-out mean. Note the distinction: the held-out mean is used here to define the metric, not to train or select our model.

18. Baselines, scales, and residual diagnostics in Regression

Suppose the model-development team chose a constant benchmark of 20 minutes from its Training period, before seeing this held-out batch. Coincidentally, the six evaluation values also average 20; that coincidence is not how the benchmark was fitted. Its absolute errors are \(10,6,2,2,6,10\), summing to 36, so baseline MAE = 36/6 = 6 minutes. Its squared errors total 280, giving baseline RMSE = \(\sqrt{280/6}\approx6.8313\) minutes. The candidate's MAE 1.8333 and RMSE 2.1213 are lower on this invented batch, but these six records cannot establish operational reliability.

Residual plots can reveal more than an average metric: systematic underprediction for lengthy tasks, increasing spread with workload, temporal drift or a small number of extreme cases. Here the candidate has its largest overprediction on F6 (+4). A responsible diagnosis would check flight type, weather, crew availability and data recording, rather than erasing F6 as an inconvenient outlier. The example does not contain those Features, so their roles are hypotheses, not demonstrated causes.

Metrics are sensitive to units. If durations are expressed in seconds instead of minutes, MAE and RMSE multiply by 60; MSE multiplies by \(60^2=3600\), while R² is unchanged under a consistent nonzero linear rescaling of Target and predictions. Compare models on the same units and evaluation rows, and report both a robust metric (such as MAE) and a large-error-sensitive metric when appropriate.

19. Error Analysis: investigate the mistakes that matter

A good evaluation report identifies individual failure modes instead of presenting only aggregate scores. At \(t=.50\), the False Positives are V2 (0.85,0) and V4 (0.65,0); the False Negative is V7 (0.35,1). V2 deserves attention because it was confidently flagged despite no confirmed leak. V7 deserves attention because missing a true leak may impose a larger operational consequence than inspecting an unaffected segment.

A useful Error Review Log records: segment identifier, score, threshold, classification, actual outcome, model and preprocessing version, timestamp, data source, and any independently verified explanation. Potential explanations—sensor drift, pressure changes, maintenance work or delayed labels—must be investigated, not invented from a table that has only scores and outcomes.

Do not hide error composition behind Accuracy. Two policies each achieve 75% Accuracy on Validation: at \(t=.50\) there are two FP and one FN, while at \(t=.30\) there are three FP and zero FN. Those are different policies with different workload and consequences. Additionally, no observed FN in four positive cases does not establish zero FN risk in production. Small denominators produce unstable apparent perfection.

20. Slice analysis, fairness and distribution changes

An overall score can conceal poor results for particular sites, equipment types, seasons or neighborhoods. Slice Analysis calculates metrics within prespecified meaningful subgroups—for example, older versus recently installed sensors or urban versus rural water networks—while reporting each group's positive and negative counts. A subgroup with no actual positives has undefined Recall; an empty denominator is not permission to report “100%.”

Make this concrete with a separate synthetic sample, not our twelve-site dataset: imagine 100 sensors, 50 new and 50 older. Each group has ten true leaks and forty negatives. A fixed screening policy yields the following counts:

Subgroup TP FN FP TN Recall Precision
New sensors 9 1 4 36 9/10 = 90% 9/13 ≈ 69.23%
Older sensors 5 5 8 32 5/10 = 50% 5/13 ≈ 38.46%
Combined 14 6 12 68 14/20 = 70% 14/26 ≈ 53.85%

Overall Recall of 70% masks a 40-percentage-point difference between new and older sensors. We cannot infer that age causes the difference: calibration history, maintenance, inspection frequency and label quality could contribute. The lesson is that aggregate metrics may conceal operationally important failures.

Evaluation slices need both adequate sample sizes and careful interpretation. Differences may reflect sensor placement, maintenance history or unequal inspection coverage rather than intrinsic properties of a community. A risk score based on historically investigated leaks can reproduce selection bias if some neighborhoods were inspected more frequently, making their labels more complete. This is a data-generation issue that no metric formula automatically resolves.

Use temporal slices too: summer vs winter, before vs after a sensor upgrade. A shift in input scores is not necessarily Concept Drift; a changed relationship between inputs and confirmed leaks needs matured outcomes to establish. It is wise to monitor alert rate immediately, then evaluate Precision and Recall when labels become available. For potentially consequential decisions, document who can act on predictions and when a human review is required.

21. Uncertainty: percentages from four positives are fragile

Our Validation Recall is \(3/4=75\%\). One additional missed leak, with the number of positives unchanged, would move it to \(2/4=50\%\)—a 25-percentage-point change. Precision \(3/5=60\%\) also has only five flagged sites in its denominator. These are teaching proportions, not tight performance estimates.

One illustrative way to quantify sampling uncertainty for a binomial proportion is the Wilson Score Interval. With \(\widehat p=k/n\) and a conventional 95% normal critical value \(z=1.96\), its center and half-width are:

\[c=\frac{\widehat p+z^2/(2n)}{1+z^2/n},\qquad h=\frac{z\sqrt{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)/n+z^2/(4n^2)}}{1+z^2/n}\]

For Recall \(k=3,n=4\), substituting gives \(c\approx0.6275\) and \(h\approx0.3269\), hence an approximate 95% Wilson interval of [0.301, 0.954] under suitable independent-Bernoulli assumptions. Substitute every intermediate term: \(\widehat p=.75\), \(z^2=3.8416\), and \(1+z^2/n=1+3.8416/4=1.9604\). The center numerator is \(.75+3.8416/8=1.2302\), giving \(c=1.2302/1.9604\approx.6275\). Inside the half-width square root we obtain \(.75(.25)/4+3.8416/(4\cdot4^2)=.046875+.060025=.106900\). Therefore \(h=1.96\sqrt{.106900}/1.9604\approx.3269\). Subtracting and adding \(h\) gives the reported interval. This calculation shows the source of the result rather than presenting a library command without explanation.

The interval is extremely wide. Correlated sites or selection bias would require different uncertainty methods. This reinforces why a genuine study needs many representative, independent outcomes across relevant conditions; no confidence interval repairs biased data collection.

22. Evaluation design and Data Leakage: where seemingly rigorous studies fail

Evaluation validity starts before computing a metric. Training fits model coefficients and preprocessing; Validation selects features, models, thresholds and operating policies; Test evaluates the frozen choice. If the team tries 40 thresholds and reports the best result from Test as “unseen” performance, Test has become development data, regardless of its filename.

For a water network, repeated measurements from the same segment may be highly correlated. Randomly putting neighboring hours from one sensor into both Training and Test can overstate generalization to new sensors. Group-aware splitting is appropriate when the deployment objective concerns unseen segments; chronological evaluation is needed when the goal concerns future conditions. A gap may be needed if labels mature after delayed inspections or adjacent windows overlap. The right split depends on the deployment question, not a fashionable default.

The same discipline applies to preprocessing and calibration. If a scaler, imputer, feature selector or probability-calibration map learns parameters from Validation or Test while those observations are presented as untouched performance data, the evaluation is contaminated. Inside Cross-Validation, learned transformations must be refitted separately on each Training fold. For fair model comparisons, use the same held-out observations and document all selection decisions, including business-defined costs and capacity.

B. Score ranking, PR summary and the operational policy are not the same question

At \(t=.70\), Precision is \(2/(2+2)=50\%\), Recall is \(2/(2+0)=100\%\), and False Positive Rate is \(2/(2+12)=2/14\approx14.29\%\). Accuracy is \((2+12)/16=87.5\%\); an always-legitimate rule would obtain \(14/16=87.5\%\) while detecting zero frauds. This is why Accuracy alone fails to reveal the added value of fraud alerts.

Ranking quality does not change when the threshold changes. The two positive transactions rank first and fourth. F1 outranks all 14 negatives, and F2 outranks 12 negatives (L1 and L2 have higher scores), so \(ROC\!\text{-}AUC=(14+12)/(2\times14)=26/28\approx0.9286\). Average Precision weights Precision when each positive enters the ranked list: at ranks 1 and 4, Precision is \(1/1=1\) and \(2/4=.5\); therefore \(AP=(1+.5)/2=.75\). These are ranking summaries on sixteen constructed cases, not reliable estimates of production ROC-AUC or PR-AUC.

Now introduce a hypothetical operating policy: each investigated alert costs $5 of review effort (including correctly detected fraud), each missed fraud has an assigned $200 loss, and the team can inspect at most five transactions per batch of sixteen. For this simplified exercise, assign no further costs or savings; the $200 does not represent a measured customer loss. The total objective is \(C(t)=5[TP(t)+FP(t)]+200\,FN(t)\), subject to \(TP+FP\leq5\). At .90, cost is \(5(1)+200(1)=\$205\). At .70, \(5(4)+200(0)=\$20\). At .65 it is $25 (five investigations); .50 and .30 are infeasible with seven and ten investigations. Among the five prespecified Validation cutoffs only, .70 is the lowest-cost feasible choice. Do not optimize on Test; the candidate policy would be locked before any independent Test.

C. Why real-world fraud prevalence can overturn an apparently good Precision

Imagine—hypothetically, not as a measurement—that deployment prevalence were only \(\pi=.005\) (0.5%), while Sensitivity/TPR and FPR at .70 stayed exactly \(1\) and \(2/14\). Bayes' rule then converts conditional error rates into expected alert Precision. The denominator counts both flagged fraud and flagged legitimate transactions in the new population:

\[PPV=\frac{TPR\,\pi}{TPR\,\pi+FPR(1-\pi)}=\frac{1(0.005)}{1(0.005)+(2/14)(0.995)}=0.03398\approx3.40\%\]

Thus 50% Precision in the enriched teaching sample could become approximately 3.40% at a 0.5% base rate, even if TPR and FPR did not change. In practice those error rates can also change, customer behavior can shift, and fraud labels can be delayed or selectively verified. Investigate whether legitimate transactions receive labels comparable in reliability to flagged transactions; otherwise verification bias can invalidate these metrics. Re-estimate prevalence, capacity and costs in the target population instead of transporting Validation Precision unchanged.

D. Quantify uncertainty and check what the new exercise actually proves

Two detected frauds out of two positives produce observed Recall 100%. That is not evidence that future Recall equals 100%. With the standard Wilson score interval (z = 1.96 and n = 2), the illustrative 95% interval for Recall extends from approximately 34.2% to 100% under an independent Bernoulli sampling approximation, already a huge range. Because this dataset was constructed rather than randomly sampled, even that interval is a sensitivity illustration, not a population confidence claim. No Test cohort has been evaluated for this payment example; deployment conclusions require future independent labels, frozen rules and monitoring.

Reproduce the interval by explicit substitution: \(\widehat p=2/2=1\), \(z^2=3.8416\) and \(1+z^2/n=2.9208\); hence \(c=(1+3.8416/4)/2.9208=0.6712\) and \(h=1.96\sqrt{3.8416/16}/2.9208=0.3288\). Therefore \([c-h,c+h]\approx[0.3424,1.0000]\). This uses the general Wilson formula derived in Section 21.

    tp = int(np.sum(predicted & (y == 1)))
    fp = int(np.sum(predicted & (y == 0)))

    results.append((t, tp, fp, fn, tn, alerts, cost))

1. Recalculate the policy if review capacity decreases from five to three alerts.

Threshold .70 needs four reviews, and .65 needs five, so both are infeasible. Threshold .90 needs one and costs $205. Among the five listed candidates, .90 is the only feasible policy; its missed fraud cost makes the limitation visible. If additional cutoffs were considered, they would need separate Validation analysis.

2. Explain why the fraud example has ROC-AUC 26/28 even though Precision is 2/4 at .70.

ROC-AUC evaluates all 28 positive–negative pairs across all rankings: 26 are ordered correctly. Precision at .70 divides only the two true flagged frauds by the four flagged transactions. Different denominators and questions produce different numerical answers.

3. Reconstruct the Bayes denominator using a hypothetical population of 200,000 transactions.

At 0.5% prevalence, expect 1,000 frauds and 199,000 legitimate transactions. With TPR 1, expected flagged frauds = 1,000. With FPR 2/14, expected false flags = 199,000(2/14) ≈ 28,428.57. Expected Precision = 1,000/(1,000+28,428.57) ≈ 0.03398 (3.40%). Expected values can be fractional; actual observed counts are integers.

4. Identify two reasons not to use .70 to deny payments tomorrow.

First, scores have not been shown calibrated, and choosing a threshold using these sixteen constructed Validation rows does not establish generalization. Second, false positives can harm legitimate customers; real decline decisions require separate risk, fairness, consumer-protection and operational validation. The example proposes human review only.

26. Common mistakes: find the incorrect idea and repair it

Mistake 1 — “Accuracy is enough.” With 1% event prevalence, the flag-none baseline reaches 99% Accuracy but never flags a leak. Report Recall, alert burden and class prevalence. Mistake 2 — “Precision is the fraction of leaks we detect.” That denominator describes Recall; Precision conditions on the flagged set. Mistake 3 — “Recall and Specificity have the same denominator.” Recall divides by actual positives, Specificity by actual negatives. Mistake 4 — “F1 includes operational costs.” It is a harmonic mean; costs must be specified and analyzed separately.

Mistake 5 — “ROC-AUC of .81 means 81% of predictions are correct.” AUC is a threshold-independent ranking summary over positive–negative pairs. Mistake 6 — “Average Precision and trapezoidal PR-AUC are interchangeable.” They use different interpolation conventions. Mistake 7 — “A calibrated model always ranks better.” Calibration and discrimination are distinct. Mistake 8 — “A probability of .85 proves the event occurred.” A probability is uncertain; an actual event is an observed label.

Mistake 9 — “Threshold .30 is best because the cost is lowest.” Not if the team can investigate only five segments and the policy flags seven. Mistake 10 — “A False Positive costs $12 in the real world.” Here $12 is an invented teaching assumption, not verified operational expenditure. Mistake 11 — “Five consecutive observations from the same sensor are five independent cases.” Group and temporal dependence can invalidate that assumption. Mistake 12 — “We may adjust the threshold after opening Test.” Doing so makes Test part of development.

Mistake 13 — “R²=.90 means 90% of individual forecasts are accurate.” It compares SSE with target variability; it is not an individual-hit rate. Mistake 14 — “MSE is measured in the original unit.” Squaring minutes produces minutes²; RMSE restores minutes. Mistake 15 — “A perfect four-case Recall is reliable.” Tiny denominators create substantial uncertainty. Mistake 16 — “Missing labels are negative labels.” An unconfirmed or delayed outcome must not silently be coded as 0; label validity comes before metric calculation.

27. Worked knowledge checks: calculate, interpret and challenge

1. How many sites are positive in Validation, and what is prevalence?

V1, V3, V5 and V7 have actual Label 1: four positives. With 12 sites, prevalence is \(4/12=33.33\%\). Do not confuse this proportion with the share *flagged* by a decision rule, which depends on the threshold.

2. Identify TP, FP, FN and TN at t = 0.50.

Scores .95, .85, .75, .65 and .55 are flagged. Three correspond to confirmed leaks (V1,V3,V5): TP=3. Two are nonleaks (V2,V4): FP=2. V7 is FN=1, and six other nonleaks are TN=6. Check that \(3+2+1+6=12\).

3. Why do Precision and Recall differ at t = 0.50?

Precision uses the five flagged sites: \(3/5=.60\). Recall uses the four actual leaks: \(3/4=.75\). The denominators answer different conditional questions—flag reliability versus detection coverage—and should never be swapped.

4. Derive Specificity and FPR at t = 0.30.

At .30, TN=5 and FP=3, so Specificity is \(5/(5+3)=5/8=.625\). FPR is \(3/8=.375\), equivalently \(1-.625\). Both condition on the eight actual nonleaks.

5. Calculate F1 at t = 0.30 without rounding early.

TP=4, FP=3, FN=0. Use \(F1=2TP/(2TP+FP+FN)=8/(8+3+0)=8/11\approx.7273\). This is different from Accuracy \((4+5)/12=.75\), and neither incorporates operating costs.

6. Find the assumed error cost at t = 0.80.

There is one FP and three FN. With $12 per FP and $90 per FN, cost is \(12(1)+90(3)=12+270=\$282\). These are illustrative penalties, not demonstrated financial consequences.

7. Which cutoff is selected when capacity is five flags?

At .30, seven sites are flagged, exceeding capacity. At .50, five sites are flagged and the assumed cost is $114; at .80, two are flagged and cost is $282. Among these prespecified candidates, .50 is the lower-cost feasible choice on Validation.

8. What is the ROC coordinate immediately after V3 enters the ranked list?

V1 and V3 are both positive, while V2 is a negative. Therefore TPR is \(2/4=.50\) and FPR is \(1/8=.125\). The coordinate (FPR,TPR) is (0.125,0.50).

9. Derive ROC-AUC by pairs.

There are \(4(8)=32\) positive–negative comparisons. The positive scores outrank 8, 7, 6 and 5 negatives, respectively: 26 favorable pairs. With no ties, \(AUC=26/32=.8125\), a ranking probability interpretation, not Accuracy.

10. Calculate Average Precision from positive ranks.

Positive ranks are 1, 3, 5 and 7; Precision at those ranks is \(1,2/3,3/5,4/7\). Average them over the four positives: \(AP=(1+2/3+3/5+4/7)/4\approx.70952\). State that this is the AP convention rather than trapezoidal PR area.

11. Show a single Brier contribution and interpret it.

V2 is actually 0 and has score .85. Its squared probability error is \((.85-0)^2=.7225\). This is large because the model placed high probability on an event that did not occur. Averaging all twelve contributions gives .17795.

12. Find V2's Log Loss without a calculator shortcut.

For a negative observation, the probability assigned to the observed class is \(1-p=.15\). The contribution is \(-\ln(.15)\approx1.89712\). Compare V1's \(-\ln(.95)\approx.05129\) to understand the asymmetry between a confident error and a confident correct estimate.

13. What is the mean observed fraction in the high-score calibration bin?

The high bin includes V1,V2,V3, whose outcomes are 1,0,1. Observed fraction is \(2/3=.6667\), while mean forecast is .85. With only three cases this is not decisive evidence of miscalibration; bin size and uncertainty must be reported.

14. Derive Regression MAE and RMSE.

Absolute errors sum to 11 minutes, so \(MAE=11/6\approx1.8333\) minutes. Squared errors sum to 27 minutes², so \(MSE=27/6=4.5\) minutes² and \(RMSE=\sqrt{4.5}\approx2.1213\) minutes.

15. Why is Regression R² not a hit rate?

\(R^2=1-SSE/SST=1-27/280\approx.90357\). It measures improvement relative to the variability of evaluated Target values around their mean. It does not imply that 90.36% of flights were predicted exactly, and can be negative on other held-out data.

16. Evaluate the frozen t = 0.50 policy on Test.

The flagged sites are E1–E4. E1 and E4 are TP=2, E2 and E3 are FP=2, E5 is FN=1, and E6–E8 are TN=3. Accuracy \(=(2+3)/8=.625\), Precision \(=2/4=.5\), Recall \(=2/3\). Do not change the threshold after seeing these outcomes.

17. What is the difference between high ROC-AUC and calibrated probabilities?

AUC uses only relative ordering of positive and negative scores. A strictly increasing transformation of scores retains their ranking and hence AUC, yet can make their numerical values poorly calibrated. Examine a reliability plot and proper scores such as Brier and Log Loss separately.

18. What investigation is justified by the V7 False Negative?

Verify that V7's actual Label 1 is mature and reliably confirmed; audit the input timing, sensor history and relevant site conditions. Do not infer a cause solely from \(p=.35\) and \(y=1\), and do not rewrite the frozen Test policy using knowledge of a particular future error.

28. Glossary and final synthesis

Actual Label: verified observed outcome. Prediction Score: numerical model output; only a calibrated probability if supported by evidence. Decision Threshold: rule mapping a score to a class. TP/FP/FN/TN: four mutually exclusive classification outcomes. Accuracy: share of all labels classified correctly. Precision: detected positives divided by flagged cases. Recall/Sensitivity/TPR: detected positives divided by actual positives. Specificity/TNR: correctly unflagged negatives divided by actual negatives. FPR: false positives divided by actual negatives. F1: harmonic mean of Precision and Recall.

ROC-AUC: area under ROC; interpretable as pairwise ranking quality under appropriate assumptions. Average Precision: PR summary weighting Precision by Recall increments. Calibration: long-run agreement between forecast probability and observed frequency. Brier Score: mean squared probabilistic prediction error. Log Loss: mean negative log-probability assigned to the observed outcome. Class Imbalance/Prevalence: unequal class frequencies and share of positive outcomes. MAE/MSE/RMSE: absolute, squared and root-squared regression-error averages. R²: relative SSE improvement against the evaluation-set mean benchmark. Data Leakage: improper flow of evaluation or future information into model development. Distribution Shift: changes in data distributions over time or settings.

The central principle is evaluate the question the system must actually answer. A correct formula does not rescue a poorly defined target, biased labels, an inappropriate split or an infeasible operating policy. For this synthetic water-monitoring example, ranking, calibration, class metrics, workload and conditional costs offer different views of the same scores. The airline Regression example shows why continuous numerical errors need their own measures. Learn to reconstruct each calculation, state its denominator and assumptions, and explain why no single metric is “the best” in every project.
