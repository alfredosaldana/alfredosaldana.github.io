1. From a business question to a numerical prediction

A factory wants to estimate weekly electricity consumption before scheduling its machines. It knows how many total machine-hours it plans to operate, but not the electricity it will consume. The question is: For a planned level of operating activity, how much electricity should we expect to use? This is Regression: the Target is a numerical quantity, rather than a category such as “high consumption.”

A Feature is information available when the prediction is made. Our first Feature is machine operating time. We measure it in hundreds of machine-hours: \(x=3\) means 300 machine-hours. The Target, \(y\), is weekly electricity usage measured in megawatt-hours (MWh). A megawatt-hour is an amount of energy, not an instantaneous rate of power. In this example, all weeks refer to the same facility and comparable reporting windows.

The goal is to learn a relationship from earlier weeks, then predict electricity use for weeks whose outcomes were not used during fitting. We will deliberately start with one Feature and a small constructed dataset. The numbers are synthetic for teaching; they are not measurements from a real factory. They let us verify every calculation and later identify what this simple model cannot tell us.

Central distinction. Predicting energy usage conditional on operating hours does not show that adding hours causes an exact increase in energy consumption. Ambient temperature, equipment types, maintenance, production mix and measurement errors can influence both variables. Statistical association is not automatically a controlled experiment.

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to translate the practical question into a mathematical model, derive its coefficients from all Training rows, explain a residual, assess predictions on held-out weeks, extend the model to two Features and recognize when its assumptions are questionable.

3. What does a straight-line model actually say?

Simple Linear Regression uses one predictor and represents the estimated mean response with a straight line:

\[\widehat y_i=b_0+b_1x_i.\]

Here \(\widehat y_i\) (pronounced “y-hat”) is the predicted energy for week \(i\), \(b_0\) is the intercept, and \(b_1\) is the slope. Both coefficients will be estimated from Training. We use \(b_0,b_1\) for fitted values; the symbols \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) often denote unknown population-level coefficients in a statistical model.

The slope says how much the fitted prediction changes when \(x\) increases by one unit. Because one unit of \(x\) equals 100 machine-hours, a fitted slope of 1.2 would mean 1.2 additional MWh per additional 100 machine-hours, holding the simple fitted relationship fixed. It would not mean 1.2 MWh for every additional hour.

What does “estimated mean response” mean? Imagine repeating many comparable weeks with exactly 300 machine-hours. Electricity use could still vary because weather, equipment mix and random disturbances differ. Linear Regression aims to describe the central average pattern conditional on the recorded Feature, not to force every week to consume exactly the line's value. We do not have repeated observations at every \(x\) in our tiny dataset; the straight-line assumption connects the different observed hours to estimate that pattern.

The intercept is the fitted prediction when \(x=0\). It may approximate baseline consumption (lighting, cooling, idle equipment), but zero operating hours are outside our observed Training range. We should not interpret the intercept as a reliably measured zero-activity bill.

An important distinction is that the model is linear in its coefficients \(b_0,b_1\), not necessarily that every useful predictor is raw \(x\). For example, \(b_0+b_1x+b_2x^2\) is a linear regression in the coefficients although its fitted curve can bend with \(x\). We will return to that difference in the limitations section.

Prediction is not the same as observation

For a week with \(x=3\), the model generates \(\widehat y\), whereas the measured \(y\) is observed after the week ends. The difference between the two is called a residual:

\[e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i.\]

A positive residual means the actual consumption exceeded the prediction; a negative residual means the model predicted too much. Residuals are measured in MWh, the same unit as the Target. A fitted model generally has a residual for every Training row, because the line need not pass through every observed point.

4. From five observations to the Ordinary Least Squares objective

There are infinitely many possible lines. For example, \(\widehat y=2+1x\) and \(\widehat y=1+2x\) make different predictions for the same operating hours. How do we choose coefficients using all five Training observations, rather than drawing a line through two convenient points?

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) chooses the coefficients that minimize the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) over Training:

\[SSE(b_0,b_1)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[y_i-(b_0+b_1x_i)\right]^2.\]

In this expression, \(n=5\) is the number of Training observations; the \(\sum\) symbol means “add up,” and the superscript 2 means square each residual before adding. If a prediction misses by \(+2\) MWh, its squared error is 4 MWh²; if it misses by \(-2\) MWh, it is also 4 MWh². Squaring prevents positive and negative errors from canceling and penalizes larger misses more strongly.

Why not minimize the raw sum of residuals? Because a \(+2\) error and a \(-2\) error sum to zero even though both predictions are wrong. Why not always use absolute errors? Least Absolute Deviations is a valid alternative with different properties, but the smooth squared-error objective makes OLS especially convenient mathematically. OLS does not guarantee the best model for every business cost or every distribution.

Compare two candidate lines by hand

Before estimating the optimum, consider a baseline line that always predicts 6 MWh, the Training mean. Its five residuals are \(-2,-2,0,+2,+2\); the squared errors are \(4,4,0,4,4\), so SSE = 16 MWh². Another line, \(\widehat y=2+1.2x\), predicts \(3.2,4.4,5.6,6.8,8.0\) MWh. Its squared errors are \(0.64,0.16,0.16,1.44,0\), giving SSE = 2.40 MWh². This second candidate is better by squared Training error, but we still have not proved it is the best possible line. The next section derives the optimal one using the entire dataset.

5. Estimate both coefficients from first principles

For Simple Linear Regression with an intercept, the OLS slope can be calculated from Training as

\[b_1=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2},\qquad b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x.\]

A bar denotes the sample mean. The numerator measures how \(x\) and \(y\) vary together around their means. The denominator measures how much \(x\) varies. If every Training \(x\) were identical, the denominator would be zero; we could not estimate a unique slope.

Step 1 — Calculate both means.

\[\bar x=\frac{1+2+3+4+5}{5}=3,\qquad \bar y=\frac{4+4+6+8+8}{5}=6.\]

The fitted equation is therefore

\[\boxed{\widehat y=2.4+1.2x}\quad\text{(energy in MWh; }x\text{ in 100 machine-hours).}\]

Step 4 — Interpret it. At 300 machine-hours (\(x=3\)), predicted consumption is \(2.4+1.2(3)=6.0\) MWh. At 400 machine-hours (\(x=4\)), the fitted prediction is \(2.4+1.2(4)=7.2\) MWh. That is 1.2 MWh higher when the Feature rises by 100 machine-hours. These are model-based predictions, not guaranteed meter readings or causal effects.

The method is called least squares because these estimates minimize the sum of squared Training residuals among all straight lines with an intercept. We will verify the minimum mathematically rather than asking you to accept the formula on faith.

Why is the sum of Training residuals zero?

With an intercept, OLS satisfies \(\sum_i e_i=0\) on the data used to fit the line. In our table, \(0.4-0.8+0+0.8-0.4=0\). This is a mathematical property of the fitted optimum, not evidence that the predictions are unbiased in future weeks. The sum of residuals on Validation or Test need not be zero.

7. From Residuals to MAE, MSE, RMSE and R²

We now have five Residuals, their absolute values, and their squares. The next question is how to summarize five errors without losing sight of what each summary measures. All the following metrics are computed on Training; they are not yet estimates of future prediction performance.

The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is the average size of the misses in MWh. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) is the mean of squared residuals in MWh²; taking its square root gives Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) in MWh.

\[\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{train}}=\frac{2.4}{5}=0.48\ \mathrm{MWh}.\]
\[\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{train}}=\frac{1.60}{5}=0.32\ \mathrm{MWh}^2.\]
\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{train}}&=\sqrt{0.32}\\&\approx0.566\ \mathrm{MWh}.\end{aligned}\]

Why does squaring emphasize larger mistakes? Compare T2 with T1: the absolute miss at T2 (0.8 MWh) is twice the miss at T1 (0.4 MWh), but its squared contribution is 0.64 rather than 0.16 MWh²—four times as large. This is a property of the loss measure, not evidence that T2 was a more important week for the business.

RMSE exceeds MAE here because squaring weights larger residuals more heavily. Neither number proves that errors will be equally small on new weeks: these are in-sample Training diagnostics.

Explain \(R^2\) without making it a magic score

\(R^2\) compares the fitted Training SSE with the squared error from simply predicting the Training mean \(\bar y=6\) for every Training week. We already calculated that mean-only SSE as 16. Thus

\[\begin{aligned}R^2_{\mathrm{train}}&=1-\frac{SSE_{\mathrm{model}}}{SSE_{\mathrm{mean}}}\\&=1-\frac{1.6}{16}=0.90.\end{aligned}\]

On this Training dataset, the line reduces the squared-error sum by 90% relative to the constant-mean prediction. It does not mean “90% of future predictions are correct,” and it is not a causal measure. Outside the fitting sample, \(R^2\) can even be negative when a model performs worse than the test-set mean benchmark.

8. Why does the OLS formula find the best-fitting line?

We can derive the coefficient formulas by treating SSE as a function of \(b_0\) and \(b_1\). A partial derivative measures how SSE changes as one coefficient changes while the other is held fixed. At an unconstrained minimum of this smooth objective, the derivatives are zero.

\[\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial b_0}=-2\sum_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0.\]

Rearrange: \(\sum_i y_i=nb_0+b_1\sum_i x_i\). Divide by \(n\) to obtain \(\bar y=b_0+b_1\bar x\), hence \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\). This is why our fitted line passes through the point \((\bar x,\bar y)=(3,6)\).

The derivative with respect to the slope is

\[\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial b_1}=-2\sum_i x_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0.\]

Do not skip the algebra between the derivatives and the slope formula. From the intercept condition we already have \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\). Therefore, for any row the fitted residual can be rearranged as follows:

\[y_i-b_0-b_1x_i=(y_i-\bar y)-b_1(x_i-\bar x).\]

Now substitute that expression into the slope condition \(\sum_i x_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0\). Since deviations from a mean always sum to zero, \(\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)=0\) and \(\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0\), we can center the factor \(x_i\) too. The equation then becomes:

\[\begin{aligned} 0&=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)\big[(y_i-\bar y)-b_1(x_i-\bar x)\big]\\ &=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)-b_1\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2. \end{aligned}\]

Move the second term to the right and divide by the strictly positive sum of squared \(x\)-deviations. We recover \(b_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}=12/10=1.2\), then \(b_0=6-1.2(3)=2.4\). This derivation explains why the estimates depend on the two totals calculated in Section 5.

\[\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=b_1\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2.\]

Dividing by the positive denominator gives the slope formula used in Section 5. For our five observations, \(S_{xx}=10\) and \(S_{xy}=12\). The fitted SSE for any proposed slope \(b_1\), after choosing its best compatible intercept \(b_0=6-3b_1\), simplifies to

\[SSE(b_1)=1.6+10(b_1-1.2)^2.\]

This expression cannot be smaller than 1.6, because a square is never negative. It equals 1.6 precisely at \(b_1=1.2\). That is a direct check of the global minimum for this example. If you try slope 0.8, the SSE becomes \(1.6+10(0.8-1.2)^2=3.2\). If you try slope 1.6, it is also 3.2. The graph of SSE against the slope is a convex parabola.

Connection to future topics. In more complex models, we may not have a convenient closed-form coefficient expression. Methods such as Gradient Descent use derivatives to adjust the parameters iteratively. OLS here demonstrates the objective and the optimum before those optimization methods are introduced in depth.

9. Correlation, covariance and the slope: related, not interchangeable

The numerator \(S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=12\) is an unscaled cross-deviation total. When divided by \(n-1\) it becomes the sample Covariance: \(s_{xy}=12/4=3\) (units: 100 machine-hours × MWh). Sample variance of \(x\) is \(s_x^2=10/4=2.5\) (units: squared hundreds of machine-hours). Therefore,

\[b_1=\frac{s_{xy}}{s_x^2}=\frac{3}{2.5}=1.2.\]

Correlation standardizes covariance by both standard deviations: \(r=s_{xy}/(s_xs_y)\). Since \(S_{yy}=\sum(y_i-6)^2=16\), the sample standard deviations are \(s_x=\sqrt{2.5}\) and \(s_y=\sqrt{16/4}=2\). Consequently

\[r=\frac{3}{\sqrt{2.5}\times2}\approx0.9487.\]

Intuitively, a correlation near \(+1\) means observations tend to rise together in an approximately straight-line pattern; a value near \(-1\) indicates an approximately straight descending pattern. Values near zero indicate weak linear association, not necessarily no relationship at all: a U-shaped relationship can have small correlation. Changing energy from MWh to kWh changes covariance and slope numerically, but not correlation, because the scaling cancels in the ratio.

Correlation is unitless and indicates a strong positive linear association in these five synthetic Training observations. Slope has physical units and quantifies the fitted change in energy per increment of operating time. A correlation of 0.9487 does not mean a slope of 0.9487. With an intercept and one Feature, Training \(R^2=r^2\approx0.90\); this identity does not automatically extend to Multiple Regression or arbitrary held-out evaluations.

Interpolation versus extrapolation

Validation \(x=2.5\) and \(4.5\) lie within the observed Training interval \([1,5]\): predicting there is interpolation. Test \(x=6\) and \(7\) lie beyond that interval: this is extrapolation. Although we can insert any \(x\) into the equation, we cannot assume the fitted physical relationship persists indefinitely. A production facility may hit capacity limits, switch equipment or become less efficient under heavy load. Extrapolation demands special scrutiny—even when these two synthetic Test errors happen to be small.

11. Interpret coefficients correctly when units or scales change

Our Feature \(x\) counts hundreds of machine-hours. If we instead define \(h=100x\) as individual machine-hours, substitute \(x=h/100\):

\[\widehat y=2.4+1.2\left(\frac{h}{100}\right)=2.4+0.012h\quad\text{MWh}.\]

Now the slope is 0.012 MWh per machine-hour, or 12 kWh per machine-hour because 1 MWh = 1,000 kWh. The predictions have not changed; only the unit of the input and the numerical slope have changed. If the output is also converted to kWh, multiply the entire equation by 1,000: \(\widehat y_{kWh}=2400+12h\). Using \(h=300\) gives \(2400+12(300)=6000\) kWh = 6 MWh, consistent with the original equation.

This is a fixed unit conversion. Unlike fitting the slope from data, changing machine-hours into hundreds of machine-hours does not learn parameters from Validation or Test. Mixing units between fitting and prediction, however, can produce wildly wrong outputs. A prediction system must specify its schema and check units.

Why a positive slope is not automatically causal

We observed that higher operating hours tend to accompany higher electricity use. Yet weeks with more operating hours may also involve a different product mix or more cooling demand. The slope does not prove what energy would change if we intervened to increase hours while holding everything else constant. A causal claim requires additional assumptions or an appropriate experimental or quasi-experimental design.

12. Multiple Linear Regression: from raw data to the Design Matrix

A new task, not a new algorithmic shortcut. We will use a separate four-row example and keep its predictions and coefficients distinct from the earlier five-row Simple Linear Regression fit. First represent the data mathematically, then estimate the coefficients, and finally interpret and test the solution.

The previous model used only operating time. In practice, electricity use may also depend on whether cooling equipment was running. Multiple Linear Regression connects a numerical Target to two or more Features while remaining linear in the coefficients:

\[\widehat y=b_0+b_1x_1+b_2x_2+\cdots+b_px_p.\]

Here \(p\) is the number of predictor Features, \(b_0\) is the Intercept and each \(b_j\) is a coefficient. For example, \(b_1\) is the fitted change in \(\widehat y\) for one additional unit of \(x_1\) when the other included Features are held fixed. This is a conditional model interpretation, not by itself a causal effect.

Step 1 — Examine a separate, complete four-row dataset

We introduce a new illustrative dataset, independent of the nine weeks used for Simple Linear Regression. It is deliberately small so every matrix calculation can be reproduced by hand.

Step 2 — Build the Design Matrix and explain its dimensions

Each observation becomes one row. We add a column containing 1 in every row so matrix multiplication can include the Intercept. The next columns store operating time and cooling. The response vector contains the observed energy values.

\[X=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\1&1&1\\1&2&0\\1&2&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\6\\7\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}b_0\\b_1\\b_2\end{bmatrix}.\]

The matrix \(X\) has 4 rows × 3 columns. The coefficient vector \(\mathbf b\) has 3 rows × 1 column. Thus \(X\mathbf b\) produces four predictions: one for each observation. For example, row A multiplies to \(1b_0+1b_1+0b_2=b_0+b_1\), and row D to \(b_0+2b_1+b_2\).

\[\widehat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}b_0+b_1\\b_0+b_1+b_2\\b_0+2b_1\\b_0+2b_1+b_2\end{bmatrix}.\]

The first stage is complete: we have represented the observations and shown that Xb computes one prediction per row. We still have to choose b₀, b₁ and b₂; the next section shows the full estimation, not merely its final answer.

13. Derive and solve the Normal Equations, step by step

The Design Matrix tells us how to calculate predictions for any proposed coefficients. It does not yet tell us which coefficients are best. The Normal Equations are the conditions obtained by minimizing the same squared-error objective that we already used for the one-Feature line.

Step 3 — Derive the Normal Equations from the same OLS objective

On a phone, swipe horizontally inside the mathematical blocks to read the entire equation.

Just as before, OLS minimizes the squared differences between actual and predicted values. In matrix notation the objective is \(SSE(\mathbf b)=(\mathbf y-X\mathbf b)^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf b)\). Setting its gradient with respect to the coefficients to zero gives:

\[\nabla_{\mathbf b}SSE=-2X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf b)=\mathbf 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X^TX\mathbf b=X^T\mathbf y.\]

To see why three conditions arise, define the residual for row i as \(e_i=y_i-(b_0+b_1x_{i1}+b_2x_{i2})\). A change in the intercept affects every prediction; changing either slope affects predictions in proportion to the corresponding Feature value. Differentiating SSE with respect to each coefficient and setting each derivative to zero gives:

\[\begin{aligned} 0&=\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial b_0}=-2\sum_i e_i,\\ 0&=\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial b_1}=-2\sum_i x_{i1}e_i,\\ 0&=\frac{\partial SSE}{\partial b_2}=-2\sum_i x_{i2}e_i. \end{aligned}\]

These three familiar derivative conditions are precisely the three components of \(X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf b)=\mathbf 0\). Matrix notation collects the conditions; it does not replace the reasoning behind them.

The superscript \(T\) means transpose: swap the rows and columns. The equation on the right is called the Normal Equation system. It is not a magic formula: it is the multi-Feature counterpart of the two derivative conditions we solved in Section 8.

Step 4 — Calculate \(X^TX\) and \(X^T\mathbf y\), rather than only showing them

The three columns of \(X\) are \(\mathbf c_0=(1,1,1,1)^T\), \(\mathbf c_1=(1,1,2,2)^T\) and \(\mathbf c_2=(0,1,0,1)^T\). Each cell in \(X^TX\) is a Dot Product between two of these columns:

Build the distinct entries of \(X^TX\) from column products.
Matrix entryArithmeticValue
\(\mathbf c_0\cdot\mathbf c_0\)1 + 1 + 1 + 14
\(\mathbf c_0\cdot\mathbf c_1\)1 + 1 + 2 + 26
\(\mathbf c_0\cdot\mathbf c_2\)0 + 1 + 0 + 12
\(\mathbf c_1\cdot\mathbf c_1\)1 + 1 + 4 + 410
\(\mathbf c_1\cdot\mathbf c_2\)0 + 1 + 0 + 23
\(\mathbf c_2\cdot\mathbf c_2\)0 + 1 + 0 + 12

The off-diagonal products repeat symmetrically, so \(X^TX\) is a 3 × 3 symmetric matrix. For \(X^T\mathbf y\), multiply each predictor column by the observed responses and add: \(\mathbf c_0\cdot\mathbf y=4+5+6+7=22\), \(\mathbf c_1\cdot\mathbf y=4+5+2(6)+2(7)=35\), and \(\mathbf c_2\cdot\mathbf y=0+5+0+7=12\).

\[X^TX=\begin{bmatrix}4&6&2\\6&10&3\\2&3&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad X^T\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}22\\35\\12\end{bmatrix}.\]

Step 5 — Solve for all three coefficients by elimination

Substituting the numbers into \(X^TX\mathbf b=X^T\mathbf y\) produces three equations with three unknown coefficients:

\[\begin{cases}4b_0+6b_1+2b_2=22,\\6b_0+10b_1+3b_2=35,\\2b_0+3b_1+2b_2=12.\end{cases}\]

First isolate \(b_2\). Multiply the third equation by 2, obtaining \(4b_0+6b_1+4b_2=24\). Subtract the first equation, \(4b_0+6b_1+2b_2=22\). The \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) terms cancel:

\[(4b_0+6b_1+4b_2)-(4b_0+6b_1+2b_2)=24-22 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2b_2=2\Rightarrow\boxed{b_2=1}.\]

Next solve for \(b_0\) and \(b_1\). Replace \(b_2\) by 1 in the first two equations, then divide each by 2. This leaves \(2b_0+3b_1=10\) and \(3b_0+5b_1=16\). Multiply the first by 3 and the second by 2; subtract:

\[\begin{aligned}6b_0+9b_1&=30,\\6b_0+10b_1&=32 \end{aligned}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad b_1=32-30=\boxed{2}.\]

Substitute \(b_1=2\) back into \(2b_0+3b_1=10\): \(2b_0+6=10\), so \(2b_0=4\) and \(\boxed{b_0=2}\). We have now derived all the coefficients.

This is the estimated solution, not a guess: b₀ = 2, b₁ = 2 and b₂ = 1. The next section independently verifies it with the inverse of XᵀX and translates the numbers back into predicted electricity consumption.

14. Verify the matrix solution, interpret coefficients and recognize limitations

The elimination method in the previous section has already given all three coefficients. We now check the same answer through a matrix operation, apply it to every row, and distinguish an exact arithmetic fit from evidence of useful generalization.

Step 6 — Verify the explicit matrix-inverse solution

On a phone, swipe horizontally inside long matrix formulas to see the entire expression.

Because these three predictor columns are linearly independent, \(X^TX\) is invertible (its determinant is 4, not zero). The closed-form matrix solution can therefore also be written \(\widehat{\mathbf b}=(X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y\). For this small example, the inverse and multiplication are:

\[(X^TX)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{11}{4}&-\frac32&-\frac12\\-\frac32&1&0\\-\frac12&0&1\end{bmatrix}.\]

How do we obtain an inverse? For an invertible square matrix \(A=X^TX\), Gauss–Jordan elimination begins with the augmented matrix \([A\mid I]\). Row operations reduce the left side to \(I\), and the right side becomes \(A^{-1}\). We show the resulting inverse, then verify two entries of \(AA^{-1}\) by ordinary multiplication:

\[\begin{aligned} (AA^{-1})_{11}&=4\left(\frac{11}{4}\right)+6\left(-\frac32\right)+2\left(-\frac12\right)=1,\\ (AA^{-1})_{12}&=4\left(-\frac32\right)+6(1)+2(0)=0. \end{aligned}\]

The first check produces a diagonal 1 and the second an off-diagonal 0, as expected for the Identity Matrix. Checking every entry gives \(AA^{-1}=I_3\). The separate elimination method already showed how to solve for \(\mathbf b\) without requiring the inverse.

\[\widehat{\mathbf b}= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{11}{4}&-\frac32&-\frac12\\-\frac32&1&0\\-\frac12&0&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}22\\35\\12\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}\frac{11}{4}(22)-\frac32(35)-\frac12(12)\\-\frac32(22)+35\\-\frac12(22)+12\end{bmatrix} =\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\\1\end{bmatrix}}.\]

Make the matrix multiplication readable row by row: the first row gives \(b_0=\frac{11}{4}(22)-\frac32(35)-\frac12(12)=60.5-52.5-6=2\); the second gives \(b_1=-\frac32(22)+35=-33+35=2\); the third gives \(b_2=-\frac12(22)+12=-11+12=1\). Each coefficient comes from a row–column Dot Product, not from guessing the final vector.

Both methods—elimination and matrix inversion—give the same result. Software normally uses more numerically stable factorizations such as QR or SVD rather than explicitly computing \((X^TX)^{-1}\), particularly when predictors are nearly dependent.

Step 7 — Make every prediction and interpret the answer

\[\boxed{\widehat y=2+2x_1+1x_2}\qquad X\widehat{\mathbf b}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\6\\7\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf y.\]

For observation A, the prediction is \(2+2(1)+1(0)=4\) MWh; for B it is \(2+2(1)+1(1)=5\); for C, \(2+2(2)+0=6\); and for D, \(2+2(2)+1=7\). Every residual is zero, so the Training SSE is \(0^2+0^2+0^2+0^2=0\) MWh².

How to interpret the coefficients. With cooling held fixed, moving from \(x_1=1\) to \(x_1=2\) increases fitted energy by 2 MWh. At the same operating time, switching cooling from 0 to 1 increases it by 1 MWh. The Intercept 2 MWh is the equation's prediction at \(x_1=x_2=0\), which is outside these four observed combinations. These are conditional fitted differences, not established causal effects.

Why this perfect result is not a modeling triumph: the four responses were chosen to lie exactly on an additive linear equation. Real measurements contain noise, the predictors may interact and four observations cannot establish reliable future performance. If columns were exactly redundant, the coefficients would not have a unique inverse-based solution; a least-squares prediction might still be computable through a pseudoinverse, but coefficient interpretation would require special care.

The stages are intentionally separated: understanding the Design Matrix, solving the Normal Equations, and interpreting the fitted model are related but distinct learning objectives.

Counterexample — perfect collinearity makes coefficients nonunique

Our original three columns (intercept, operating time and cooling) were independent, and that is why the inverse existed. Now suppose someone adds a fourth column \(x_3=2x_1\), which converts every operating-time value into a doubled measure. The new matrix has four rows and four columns:

\[X_{new}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0&2\\1&1&1&2\\1&2&0&4\\1&2&1&4\end{bmatrix}.\]

The fourth column is exactly twice the second. Hence \(\operatorname{rank}(X_{new})=3\), not 4, and \(\det(X_{new}^{T}X_{new})=0\). There is no inverse for this normal-equation matrix. The issue is not that the computer needs more precision; the data cannot separately identify both time coefficients.

We can verify the ambiguity without guessing. Keep the coefficient order \((b_0,b_1,b_2,b_3)\). Both vectors

\[\mathbf b^{(A)}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf b^{(B)}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}\]

produce the same four predictions \((4,5,6,7)^T\). At observation B (\(x_1=1,x_2=1,x_3=2\)), model A gives \(2+2(1)+1(1)+0(2)=5\), while model B gives \(2+0(1)+1(1)+1(2)=5\). At observation C (\(x_1=2,x_2=0,x_3=4\)), both give 6. Because \(2x_1=x_3\) in every row, the equivalence extends to A and D as well. Thus a zero Training SSE does not imply unique or interpretable individual coefficients.

Correct remedy. Remove the redundant Feature or justify a new model constraint. Numerical tools may use a pseudoinverse to select one coefficient vector, but they cannot create information absent from the data. Near-collinearity, rather than exact equality, can also destabilize coefficients; more digits do not solve the underlying design problem. Regularization is treated later in the curriculum.

15. What assumptions does OLS need—and for which conclusions?

It helps to separate computing coefficients from trusting interpretation and statistical inference. OLS will still calculate a best-fitting line for many imperfect datasets. But unbiased coefficient claims, conventional standard errors, hypothesis tests or reliable forecasts require additional conditions.

Concept What it means What can go wrong
Linear conditional mean The mean response is reasonably represented by the chosen linear-in-coefficients terms. Systematic curvature remains in residuals.
Exogeneity / zero conditional mean Unobserved influences have conditional mean zero given included Features. Omitted correlated influences can bias coefficients.
Independence / error dependence Observations and disturbances may be related across time or equipment. Conventional uncertainty estimates may be wrong.
Constant variance (homoskedasticity) Error variance is similar across predictor levels. Standard textbook standard errors may fail without adjustment.
Full column rank No predictor is a perfect linear combination of others. Coefficients may not be uniquely identified.
Normal errors (for some finite-sample inference) A distributional assumption used for certain exact small-sample tests. Without it, OLS predictions can still be computed; inference requires care.

Consider a concrete omitted-variable concern. On hotter weeks, a factory may both run machinery longer and use more electricity for cooling. If we fit only operating hours, the slope may partly absorb cooling demand. Including a valid temperature Feature could improve predictions and alter the hours coefficient. But even then, a causal interpretation would require checking whether the relevant influences have been adequately addressed. The assumption about mean-zero disturbances matters especially when we try to interpret a coefficient beyond mere prediction.

A common misconception is that all these assumptions must be perfectly true for an algorithm to produce any prediction. They do not. Their importance depends on the objective: point predictions, interpretation of a slope, causal interpretation or formal confidence intervals are different tasks.

Residual diagnostics are questions, not magic pass/fail tests

Plot residuals against fitted values or operating hours. If residuals fan out as \(x\) grows, the error spread may depend on activity. If residuals bend systematically, a straight line may miss curvature. In our five-point Training sample, the residual pattern is too small to diagnose much. A real analysis would use many more weeks, domain knowledge and appropriate diagnostics, possibly robust uncertainty estimates or alternative models.

What is the difference between RMSE and a residual standard error?

Training RMSE uses the denominator \(n\), here \(\sqrt{1.6/5}\approx0.566\) MWh. In a simple OLS model with an intercept and slope, a conventional residual standard error estimates noise using \(n-2\) degrees of freedom:

\[s=\sqrt{\frac{SSE}{n-2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1.6}{3}}\approx0.730\ \mathrm{MWh}.\]

The different denominators answer different questions. RMSE summarizes average in-sample squared prediction error; the residual standard error accounts for fitting two coefficients when estimating an error standard deviation under model assumptions. Do not label them interchangeably.

Residuals versus fitted values: draw the actual diagnostic, not an imagined pattern

A diagnostic plot puts the fitted prediction on the horizontal axis and the Training residual on the vertical axis. Both axes have MWh units, unlike the original residual-by-week plot. It helps ask whether mistakes grow with predicted consumption or bend systematically. Here are the exact five points calculated in Section 6; for example T2 contributes \((\widehat y,e)=(4.8,-0.8)\) MWh.

The dots alternate around zero. With only five Training observations, do not conclude homoskedasticity, independence or correct functional form from this appearance. Such conditions require additional data and domain scrutiny. A residual plot can reveal a concern; failing to see a pattern in a miniature educational dataset is not proof that assumptions hold.

16. Outliers, leverage and why one unusual week matters

An outlier in the response has an unusually large vertical residual. A high-leverage observation has an unusual predictor value, far from the average \(x\). For example, if most Training weeks have \(x\) from 1 through 5 but one new week has \(x=20\), that week would have high leverage because its operating time is far from the others; it need not have a large vertical residual. An ordinary \(x=3\) week with unusually high energy might have a large residual but relatively low leverage. Influence concerns how much the fitted coefficients change when an observation is included or excluded.

A point with both high leverage and a substantial residual can exert considerable influence on the fitted slope. These are related but different ideas.

Watch how changing just T5 energy from 8 to 14 MWh affects OLS, while keeping its \(x=5\) value unchanged. This is a deliberately altered scenario, not our original dataset. The new response mean is \((4+4+6+8+14)/5=7.2\); since T5 has \(x-\bar x=+2\) and its \(y\) increased by 6, the cross-deviation total grows from 12 to \(12+2(6)=24\). The denominator remains 10:

\[b_1^{new}=\frac{24}{10}=2.4,\qquad b_0^{new}=7.2-2.4(3)=0.\]

The fitted prediction at \(x=3\) changes from 6 MWh to \(0+2.4(3)=7.2\) MWh even though T3 itself did not change. This is why squared-error methods are sensitive to large deviations. Before deleting a suspicious observation, check whether it is a meter error, a genuine surge, a unit mismatch, or a previously unrecorded operating condition. Removing valid extremes can be just as misleading as keeping erroneous ones.

With sufficiently varied operating conditions, we might compare robust regression, transformations, additional relevant Features or separate operational regimes. Those choices must be validated on separate development data; do not optimize a model by selectively deleting the Test mistakes.

17. Reproduce the calculation in Python—without hiding the learning process

The code below reconstructs the five Training observations, checks the hand-derived coefficients, uses the frozen model for Validation and Test, and compares it with the Training-mean baseline. It does not fetch external data or need a notebook-specific file path.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error, r2_score

x_train = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5], dtype=float).reshape(-1, 1)
y_train = np.array([4, 4, 6, 8, 8], dtype=float)
x_val = np.array([2.5, 4.5]).reshape(-1, 1)
y_val = np.array([5.7, 7.5])
x_test = np.array([6, 7]).reshape(-1, 1)
y_test = np.array([9.4, 11.4])

x = x_train.ravel()
b1 = np.sum((x - x.mean()) * (y_train - y_train.mean())) / np.sum((x - x.mean()) ** 2)
b0 = y_train.mean() - b1 * x.mean()
print("Hand coefficients:", round(b0, 4), round(b1, 4))

model = LinearRegression().fit(x_train, y_train)
print("Library coefficients:", model.intercept_, model.coef_[0])

for split, features, actual in [("Training", x_train, y_train), ("Validation", x_val, y_val), ("Test", x_test, y_test)]:
    predicted = model.predict(features)
    print(split, "MAE:", round(mean_absolute_error(actual, predicted), 4),
          "RMSE:", round(np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(actual, predicted)), 4))

print("Training R²:", round(r2_score(y_train, model.predict(x_train)), 4))
print("Validation baseline MAE:", mean_absolute_error(y_val, np.full(len(y_val), y_train.mean())))
print("Test baseline MAE:", mean_absolute_error(y_test, np.full(len(y_test), y_train.mean())))

Expected checks: hand and library coefficients are 2.4 and 1.2; Training MAE is 0.48, Training RMSE ≈ 0.5657, Training \(R^2=0.90\), Validation MAE = 0.30, Test MAE = 0.40, and the Validation/Test baseline MAEs are 0.90 and 4.40. If your output disagrees, inspect the row order, units and whether you accidentally fitted on Validation.

What fit() actually learned here

fit(x_train,y_train) used Training to estimate the intercept and slope. predict(x_val) and predict(x_test) simply insert new \(x\) values into that already fitted equation; they do not relearn the coefficients. This is the same Training-versus-evaluation discipline introduced for imputers and scalers in Guide 5. In a more elaborate workflow, preprocessing would also be fitted inside Training (or the appropriate Cross-Validation fold).

18. Interpret decisions, recognize limitations and prepare for the next topic

Imagine a manager asks whether 700 machine-hours will consume exactly 10.8 MWh. The correct answer is no: 10.8 MWh is this fitted line's predicted value at \(x=7\), and the actual synthetic Test outcome was 11.4 MWh. A prediction is not a physical guarantee. In a real operation, we would report uncertainty, check equipment and weather conditions, and avoid making high-impact energy decisions from five fitted observations.

A manager might also ask whether lowering operating hours by 100 will certainly save 1.2 MWh. The slope is a predictive association. We would need stronger assumptions or designed operational comparisons to estimate a causal saving. Finally, the negative effects of missed operational variables might appear as patterns in residuals or drift after deployment.

Decision checklist: define the unit and time horizon; establish a credible baseline; split data before fitting; calculate OLS using Training only; examine residuals and appropriate metrics; interpret coefficient units; check extrapolation and assumptions; test independently; document what can and cannot be concluded. A complicated method does not compensate for mistakes in these foundations.

The next guides deepen Loss Functions and Gradient Descent. This chapter has already given them a concrete motivation: choose an objective such as SSE, measure how it changes with coefficients, and find the parameters that make it small. In later chapters, we will analyze these choices in greater generality rather than repeating today's derivations.

19. Common mistakes and why they are mistakes

Mistake 1 — Treating Regression as any relationship between variables. Linear Regression estimates a numerical Target conditional on Features. A scatterplot showing association is useful, but it is not the same as defining, fitting and evaluating a predictive model. State the Target and prediction time before choosing the method.

Mistake 2 — Drawing a line through two convenient points. Two points can determine a line, but OLS generally considers all Training rows. Our coefficient calculation used five complete pairs and minimized their combined SSE.

Mistake 3 — Saying the intercept must be a real physical baseline. The intercept is a fitted prediction at \(x=0\). Because we trained only on \(1\le x\le5\), interpreting \(x=0\) requires extrapolation and knowledge of the facility.

Mistake 4 — Forgetting units. Here \(x=3\) means 300 machine-hours. A slope of 1.2 MWh per 100 hours becomes 0.012 MWh per hour; treating it as 1.2 MWh per hour multiplies the implied effect by 100.

Mistake 5 — Interpreting \(R^2=0.90\) as 90% correct predictions. \(R^2\) compares squared errors with a reference. It is not Accuracy for a Classification problem, and Training \(R^2\) says little by itself about future reliability.

Mistake 6 — Assuming residuals are the same as unknown future errors. Training residuals use outcomes already observed during fitting. A future prediction error is not observable until the corresponding outcome arrives, and it need not have the same distribution.

Mistake 7 — Believing a positive slope proves causation. Other factors can accompany operating time. Conditional association does not identify an intervention effect without appropriate causal assumptions.

Mistake 8 — Re-estimating coefficients using Validation or Test. That contaminates the evaluation. The correct procedure here calculates \(2.4\) and \(1.2\) from Training, then freezes them for both held-out sets.

Mistake 9 — Treating a good Test score on two observations as deployment evidence. A single extra error could greatly change the mean. Use representative independent data and uncertainty assessment before operational claims.

Mistake 10 — Assuming extrapolation is as safe as interpolation. Weeks at \(x=6\) or 7 are outside the Training range. Equipment limits or changing regimes could invalidate the linear extension.

Mistake 11 — Confusing RMSE with residual standard error. RMSE uses \(n\) in our diagnostic; the conventional estimated error standard deviation in simple OLS uses \(n-2\) degrees of freedom. Both require explicit definitions.

Mistake 12 — Believing a tiny \(p\)-value automatically means a useful or causal model. Statistical evidence about a coefficient, predictive performance, effect size and causal interpretation are distinct. A good model report explains all relevant goals and limitations.

Mistake 13 — Deleting every outlier to obtain a cleaner line. A distant point may be valid and operationally important. Investigate records, decide a defensible policy using development data, then evaluate it without looking for convenient Test results.

Mistake 14 — Assuming “linear” means we can never use curved predictors. The model is linear in coefficients. Terms such as \(x^2\) may be included as Features, although flexibility raises overfitting and extrapolation concerns.

20. Knowledge checks: show your reasoning

1. A week has 350 machine-hours. What is \(x\), and why?

Because the Feature is measured in groups of 100 machine-hours, \(x=350/100=3.5\). Substituting 350 directly into the fitted \(2.4+1.2x\) equation would confuse units and produce a nonsensical prediction. Using the correct \(x\), predicted energy is \(2.4+1.2(3.5)=6.6\) MWh.

2. Define an observed value, predicted value and residual for T4.

T4 has \(x=4\) and observed energy \(y=8\) MWh. The fitted prediction is \(\widehat y=2.4+1.2(4)=7.2\) MWh. Its residual is \(e=y-\widehat y=8-7.2=+0.8\) MWh: actual energy exceeded the predicted amount.

3. Recalculate the OLS slope from \(S_{xy}=12\) and \(S_{xx}=10\).

Use \(b_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}=12/10=1.2\) MWh per 100 machine-hours. Then the intercept is \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x=6-1.2(3)=2.4\) MWh. Both coefficients require the sample means and all five rows.

4. Why does a negative residual not mean negative energy?

A negative residual is a *difference*: \(y-\widehat y<0\) means the prediction exceeded the measured energy. In T2, actual energy is 4 MWh and the prediction is 4.8 MWh, giving \(-0.8\) MWh. Neither actual energy nor fitted energy is negative.

5. Calculate Training MAE from absolute residuals \(0.4,0.8,0,0.8,0.4\).

Add the five absolute values: \(0.4+0.8+0+0.8+0.4=2.4\) MWh. Divide by \(n=5\) to obtain \(MAE=0.48\) MWh. This summarizes average *Training* absolute error, not future performance.

6. Calculate Training MSE and RMSE from \(SSE=1.60\).

\(MSE=SSE/n=1.60/5=0.32\) MWh². \(RMSE=\sqrt{0.32}\approx0.5657\) MWh. The square root restores the response units; both metrics come from the same Training residuals.

7. What does Training \(R^2=0.90\) mean?

The constant Training-mean prediction has SSE 16; the OLS line has SSE 1.6. Thus \(R^2=1-1.6/16=0.90\): the line reduces squared Training error by 90% compared with predicting 6 MWh for every Training observation. It is not 90% Classification Accuracy, nor evidence of causality.

8. Why do we hold the coefficients fixed for Validation?

Validation is meant to test a model choice not estimated from its own outcomes. We compute \(b_0=2.4\) and \(b_1=1.2\) from T1–T5. The Validation predictions are then \(5.4\) and \(7.8\) MWh. Refitting using V1 or V2 would let evaluation information influence what is being evaluated.

9. Compare the Validation MAE of our model and the constant baseline.

Model errors: \(|5.7-5.4|=0.3\) and \(|7.5-7.8|=0.3\), so MAE is 0.3 MWh. Baseline predicts the Training mean 6: errors \(0.3\) and \(1.5\), so MAE is 0.9 MWh. Our fitted line does better on these two cases, but two observations are insufficient for reliable model selection.

10. Are Test values at \(x=6\) and \(x=7\) interpolations?

No. Training x-values range from 1 through 5, so 6 and 7 require extrapolation. The equation returns 9.6 and 10.8 MWh, but outside-range predictions could be poor if the factory changes its operating regime. Evaluate this risk explicitly.

11. Convert the slope into kWh per machine-hour.

Our slope is 1.2 MWh per 100 machine-hours. Divide by 100 to obtain 0.012 MWh per machine-hour; multiply by 1,000 to convert energy to kWh: \(0.012\times1000=12\) kWh per machine-hour. This is a unit conversion, not a new model fit.

12. Solve the three Multiple Regression coefficients without guessing them.

The Normal Equations for the four observations are \(4b_0+6b_1+2b_2=22\), \(6b_0+10b_1+3b_2=35\), and \(2b_0+3b_1+2b_2=12\). Double the third equation and subtract the first: \(2b_2=2\), so \(b_2=1\). Substitute into the first two equations and divide by 2 to get \(2b_0+3b_1=10\) and \(3b_0+5b_1=16\). Multiply the first by 3 and the second by 2: subtract \(6b_0+9b_1=30\) from \(6b_0+10b_1=32\) to find \(b_1=2\). Finally, \(2b_0+3(2)=10\) gives \(b_0=2\). The fitted equation is \(\widehat y=2+2x_1+x_2\); verify the predictions \((4,5,6,7)\) and Training SSE = 0. The exact fit was engineered for teaching, not expected in real-world data.

13. If T5 changes from 8 to 14 MWh, what happens to the slope?

The response mean changes from 6 to 7.2. Because T5 lies 2 x-units above the x-mean and rises by 6 MWh, \(S_{xy}\) increases from 12 to 24. With \(S_{xx}=10\), the slope doubles to 2.4; the intercept becomes \(7.2-2.4(3)=0\). This is a concrete illustration of outlier influence.

14. Explain the difference between Training RMSE and residual standard error.

Training RMSE divides the squared-residual total by all five observations: \(\sqrt{1.6/5}\approx0.566\) MWh. A conventional simple-OLS residual standard error divides by \(n-2=3\) because fitting an intercept and slope uses two degrees of freedom: \(\sqrt{1.6/3}\approx0.730\) MWh. Their purposes differ.

15. Does a fitted 1.2-MWh slope prove savings from reducing hours by 100?

No. The slope describes conditional fitted association in the sample. If cooling demand, production mix or equipment differs across weeks, changing operating hours may also change these conditions. Causal savings require a credible intervention comparison or stronger assumptions.

16. Is \(\widehat y=b_0+b_1x+b_2x^2\) a linear regression?

Yes, it is *linear in the coefficients* \(b_0,b_1,b_2\), even though the curve can be nonlinear in \(x\). The squared Feature may capture curvature, but its value and risk of overfitting must be evaluated with proper held-out data.

Transfer checks: explain a fresh problem without copying the factory answers

17. From filtration runtimes 1–4 hours and volumes 5, 7, 8, 12 (100 m³), reconstruct both OLS coefficients. Show the sums.

Training means are \(\bar x=2.5\) and \(\bar y=8\). The paired deviations yield \(S_{xy}=(-1.5)(-3)+(-0.5)(-1)+(0.5)(0)+(1.5)(4)=4.5+0.5+0+6=11\). Squared x deviations yield \(S_{xx}=2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25=5\). Thus \(b_1=11/5=2.2\) hundreds of m³ per hour and \(b_0=8-2.2(2.5)=2.5\) hundreds of m³. Predictions \((4.7,6.9,9.1,11.3)\) imply residuals \((0.3,0.1,-1.1,0.7)\), SSE \(0.09+0.01+1.21+0.49=1.80\), MAE \(2.2/4=0.55\) and RMSE \(\sqrt{1.8/4}=0.6708\). These are Training diagnostics; no Validation or Test outcome was used for fitting.

18. For the held-out filtration day with five runtime hours and 14 (100 m³) observed, compare the frozen model and Training-mean baseline.

Model prediction: \(2.5+2.2(5)=13.5\), so absolute error \(|14-13.5|=0.5\) hundreds of m³ = 50 m³. Frozen Training baseline: \(\bar y=8\), so \(|14-8|=6\) hundreds of m³ = 600 m³. The model wins on this row; one row and an extrapolated x do not support a reliable deployment claim. Never use this observed 14 to refit before reporting the held-out comparison.

19. A colleague appends x₃=2x₁ to the factory Design Matrix. Does the 4×4 inverse exist? Provide two solutions.

No. Because column four equals twice column two, the matrix has rank 3 rather than 4, so \(X^TX\) is singular. Coefficients \((2,2,1,0)\) and \((2,0,1,1)\) each predict \((4,5,6,7)\). For example at \((x_1,x_2,x_3)=(2,1,4)\), they give \(2+2(2)+1=7\) and \(2+1+4=7\). Training fit alone cannot identify which operating-time coefficient is the correct individual effect.

20. What does the new residual-versus-fitted chart show, and what can it not prove?

The five plotted coordinates are (3.6,+0.4), (4.8,−0.8), (6,0), (7.2,+0.8), (8.4,−0.4), all in MWh. Points on both sides of zero show that errors have different signs; their sizes describe in-sample misses. With only five Training points, the apparent lack of a strong pattern does not establish constant error variance, independence, correct specification or reliable future error.

21. Glossary and synthesis

Term Meaning in this chapter
Target Numerical quantity to predict; here weekly electricity usage (MWh).
Feature Measured input known at prediction time; here operating time.
Simple Linear Regression A model with one Feature and an intercept and slope.
Multiple Linear Regression A linear-in-parameters model using several Features.
Parameter Coefficient estimated from Training observations, such as \(b_0\) or \(b_1\).
Intercept Fitted value at \(x=0\); not automatically a reliable physical baseline.
Slope Fitted change in \(\widehat y\) per one-unit change in \(x\).
Prediction (\(\widehat y\)) Value calculated by the fitted model before observing the true response.
Residual Observed minus fitted response on an observed row.
OLS Ordinary Least Squares; chooses coefficients minimizing Training SSE.
SSE Sum of squared residuals, with squared Target units.
MAE Mean absolute error, in the Target's units.
MSE Mean squared error, in squared Target units.
RMSE Square root of MSE, restoring Target units.
\(R^2\) Proportional improvement in squared error against a mean-only reference on the evaluated set.
Covariance Scale-dependent measure of how two variables vary together.
Correlation Unitless standardized linear association.
Interpolation Predicting within the observed predictor range.
Extrapolation Predicting beyond the observed predictor range.
Exogeneity Condition relating omitted disturbances to included predictors; important for coefficient interpretation.
Homoskedasticity Similar conditional error variance across predictor values.
Multicollinearity Strong relationships among predictors that can undermine coefficient stability; exact collinearity prevents uniqueness.
Leverage How unusual a predictor combination is relative to the fitted design.
Overfitting Learning patterns specific to Training that do not generalize reliably.

Final synthesis. Linear Regression links a numerical prediction to a transparent coefficient model and an explicit optimization objective. In our complete example, the five Training points imply \(\widehat y=2.4+1.2x\); every predicted value, residual, metric and limitation can be checked. The trustworthy workflow does not stop at finding a line: it checks units, holds coefficients fixed during evaluation, examines extrapolation and distinguishes prediction from causation. Those habits transfer directly to more complex Machine Learning methods.
