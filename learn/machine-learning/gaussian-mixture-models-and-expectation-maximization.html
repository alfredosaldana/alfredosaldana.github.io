1. Why soft clustering after k-Means?

Guide 24 introduced algorithms that often assign each observation to one group. But many measurements lie between groups: a machine vibration of 5.5 units may look partly like normal operation and partly like an emerging fault. Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) model that uncertainty by giving each observation a responsibility for every component. The responsibilities add to one; they are not observed class labels.

We will use an intentionally small synthetic example of production-quality sensor measurements. It has two groups, yet membership is unknown to the algorithm. The central questions are: how do we model the complete distribution, infer hidden memberships and update the model parameters without knowing true group labels? The answer is Expectation–Maximization (EM), an iterative method for models with hidden variables.

A GMM describes a probability density and can generate new synthetic observations. That is broader than simply drawing a partition as k-Means does. However, a component is not automatically a real-world fault type, and a density is not a calibrated probability of equipment failure.

2. Learning objectives and necessary prerequisites

After this chapter, you should be able to: distinguish a Gaussian Density from a Probability; compute the posterior probability of a hidden component; complete a numerical E-step and M-step; verify improvement in Log-Likelihood; explain why variances, priors and initialization matter; and compare models without using Test for selection.

We need only a few earlier ideas: mean and variance from Statistics, conditional probability and Bayes' Theorem, logarithms, and the idea of a Loss or Likelihood. We will reintroduce every formula when it becomes necessary. A latent variable is an unobserved variable; in our example it records which Gaussian component generated a reading. We do not pretend to know that value just because we observe the reading.

A useful question to keep in mind is whether the algorithm is discovering a fact about the world or fitting a mathematical description. It is doing the latter. The former requires independent domain evidence.

4. Gaussian density: meaning, formula and units

The Normal (Gaussian) Distribution is specified by a mean and a positive variance. Its density is not the probability of an exact measurement: for a continuous variable, the probability at an exact point is zero. Probability is the area under the curve over an interval. A density can exceed one if the scale is narrow enough, because its units are reciprocal measurement units.

\[\phi(x;\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\![-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}]\]

Here x is a sensor measurement, μ is its component mean, σ² is its component variance and σ its standard deviation. In our initialization, the component means are 2.5 and 8.5, with variance 4 for each, meaning σ = 2 sensor units. The squared deviation in the exponent makes values near μ more plausible under that component.

The figure shows two Gaussian component densities scaled by their mixture weights, plus their sum. The weighted sum integrates to one even though its individual components each integrate to their own weight.

5. A mixture is a weighted distribution

A mixture weight (often called a Prior Mixing Proportion) tells us how often each component is expected to generate observations within the model. For K components the weights are nonnegative and add to one. For two components we initially choose equal weights, 0.5 and 0.5. These are starting values, not observed truths.

\[p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\phi(x;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\]
\[\pi_1=\pi_2=0.5,\qquad\pi_1+\pi_2=1\]

The mixture density evaluated at x=4 equals 0.5 φ(4;2.5,4) + 0.5 φ(4;8.5,4). We will calculate both pieces in the next section. A mixture weight is not the responsibility of a specific observation: π belongs to the model as a whole, while a responsibility changes with x.

Quick check: Would π = (0.8, 0.8) be valid? No: the weights would total 1.6, not one. If a model predicts counts instead of a continuous value, another distribution family may be more appropriate than Gaussian.

6. What does the hidden component variable mean?

Let Z be the unobserved component index and X the observed sensor reading. Our generative story has two steps: (1) draw Z according to the mixture weights; (2) draw X from the Gaussian distribution selected by Z. This is the meaning of a latent-variable generative model. We observe X, not Z.

\[P(Z=k)=\pi_k,\qquad p(x,Z=k)=\pi_k\,\phi(x;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\]

The joint quantity combines a discrete component probability with a continuous density. Summing over all possible Z recovers the mixture density p(x). This is sometimes called marginalizing the hidden variable. Conversely, once an x is observed, Bayes' Theorem can update the probability that Z took a particular value. That is the E-step.

It is incorrect to claim that Z=1 is “normal machinery” and Z=2 is “defective machinery” merely because one component has a lower mean. Those are interpretations to investigate with independent quality labels.

7. E-step: calculate one posterior without skipping operations

For each observation we calculate the responsibility γ: the conditional probability that component k generated that observation under the current fitted parameters. The denominator is crucial: it normalizes the weighted densities so the responsibilities total one.

\[\gamma_{ik}=P(Z_i=k\mid x_i)=\frac{\pi_k\phi(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_j\phi(x_i;\mu_j,\sigma_j^2)}\]

For x=4, the weighted density of component 1 is 0.07528436; component 2 contributes 0.00793491 (units: inverse sensor units). Their sum is 0.08321927. Divide the first contribution by that sum:

\[\gamma_{4,1}=\frac{0.07528436}{0.07528436+0.00793491}=0.904651\]

Component 2 receives 1 − 0.904651 = 0.095349. This is about 90.5% responsibility for component 1, not a 90.5% probability of a fault. The responsibility depends on our choice of priors, component means and variances. Change those assumptions and it changes as well.

9. M-step: why weighted statistics are the solution

After the E-step we treat the fractional responsibilities as weights and re-estimate each Gaussian. Define N_k, the effective number of observations assigned to component k, by adding its responsibilities. It need not be an integer. The new mixture weight is N_k/n, where n is the total number of readings. The new mean is a weighted mean; the new variance is the weighted squared deviation from that newly estimated mean.

\[N_k=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{ik},\qquad\pi_k^{\rm new}=\frac{N_k}{n}\]
\[\mu_k^{\rm new}=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i}{N_k}\]
\[(\sigma_k^2)^{\rm new}=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k^{\rm new})^2}{N_k}\]

Why these operations? They maximize the expected complete-data Gaussian Log-Likelihood with responsibilities fixed. Differentiating with respect to μ produces the weighted-mean equation; differentiating with respect to σ² produces the weighted-variance equation. The weight constraint that all π values add to one yields π=N/n. We will reproduce these results numerically next instead of asking the reader to accept them on faith.

10. Complete M-step: calculate effective sizes and new means

Sum the eight responsibilities from the previous table. By symmetry, N₁=4 and N₂=4. Their total is eight: no observation is missing, even though each one contributes fractionally to both components. Hence both updated mixing weights remain 4/8=0.5.

The weighted sums of measurements are 10.448002 and 33.551998. To see the operation, the first begins with 1(0.998830)+2(0.994780)+3(0.977023)+4(0.904651), then adds contributions from x=7,8,9,10. We must include those small contributions rather than silently discard them.

\[\mu_1^{\rm new}=\frac{10.448002}{4}=2.612000\]
\[\mu_2^{\rm new}=\frac{33.551998}{4}=8.388000\]

Compared with the initialization (2.5,8.5), the two means move slightly toward the center. The model has learned that the originally chosen Gaussians were broader and slightly further apart than what these observations favor. A mean update is not a label assignment and does not require any observed component categories.

12. Complete M-step: variance with every necessary ingredient

To estimate the variance, first form weighted second moments. From all eight readings we obtain Σγᵢ₁xᵢ² = 34.928019 and Σγᵢ₂xᵢ² = 289.071981. An equivalent calculation to the weighted squared deviations is E[x²|component] − (weighted mean)². This computational shortcut is exact in arithmetic and easier to verify with a small table.

\[(\sigma_1^2)^{\rm new}=\frac{34.928019}{4}-(2.612000)^2\]
\[(\sigma_1^2)^{\rm new}=8.7320047-6.8225462\approx1.9094585\]
\[(\sigma_2^2)^{\rm new}=\frac{289.071981}{4}-(8.388000)^2=1.909458\]

We thus changed the variance of each component from 4 to about 1.909458 sensor-units²; their standard deviations become approximately 1.3818 sensor units. This result has the expected direction: readings concentrate fairly closely around 2.5 and 8.5, so a narrower density explains them better.

Note the denominator N_k, not N_k−1: we are using the Maximum Likelihood update for a Gaussian component, not an unbiased sample-variance estimator.

13. Check the updated density and Log-Likelihood

A mathematical derivation should not end at estimated parameters. We now verify that the updated mixture gives greater observed-data Log-Likelihood on the same eight Training readings. The contribution from each reading is the log of its full mixture density, not the average of component logarithms.

\[\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{8}\log\![\sum_{k=1}^{2}\pi_k\phi(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)]\]

Using the original parameters (means 2.5 and 8.5, variances 4, weights 0.5) gives −19.432151. Using the new means (2.612000 and 8.388000), new variances (1.909458 each), and unchanged weights gives −18.106405.

\[\Delta\ell=-18.106405-(-19.432151)=+1.325746\]

Larger is better: the negative number moved closer to zero. This does not mean the sensor values became more probable as exact points; it means the fitted density at the observed measurements increased in aggregate. Later, a much more flexible model could inflate Training Likelihood while generalizing poorly—hence the need for Validation and model selection.

14. Repeat EM until convergence: what really changes?

An EM iteration consists of one E-step followed by one M-step. After an M-step, we recompute the posterior responsibilities using the new parameters. If we continued using the old responsibilities, we would not be performing the next E-step correctly. At the new means and narrower variances, readings 4 and 7 receive responsibilities closer to one for their nearer components.

For this initialized example the observed-data Log-Likelihood progresses approximately as −19.432151, −18.106405, −17.790688, −17.787741 and finally approaches −17.787739. The model converges near means 2.50058 and 8.49942 and variances approximately 1.25348, not the initial values.

A practical stopping rule is a small change in Log-Likelihood between iterations, checked against an appropriate tolerance and maximum number of iterations. Stable Likelihood is not proof of global optimality, and cluster labels can be swapped without changing the fitted density. Always check whether the result makes sense for the application, not only whether the algorithm stopped.

15. Why EM improves the objective: the Jensen argument

The key theoretical question is why an algorithm that alternates between estimating hidden memberships and updating parameters usually does not lower observed-data Log-Likelihood. Start by writing the density as a sum over hidden assignments. Introduce a distribution q over the hidden component, with q positive wherever the corresponding density is positive.

\[\log p(x_i\mid\theta)=\log\sum_k q_{ik}\frac{p(x_i,Z_i=k\mid\theta)}{q_{ik}}\]

Because log is concave, Jensen's Inequality says log of an average is at least the average of the logs. Therefore:

\[\log p(x_i\mid\theta)\geq\sum_k q_{ik}\log\frac{p(x_i,Z_i=k\mid\theta)}{q_{ik}}\]

The right side is an Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) for that observation. In the E-step, choose q as the posterior responsibilities under the current parameters. Indeed, when q equals the posterior, the fraction inside the logarithm is the same constant p(x) for every component, so Jensen's inequality becomes equality: the bound touches the actual Log-Likelihood at the current parameters. In the M-step, maximize the bound with q fixed. Thus the new observed-data Log-Likelihood cannot be smaller under exact EM updates. This is a guarantee of nondecrease, not of finding the globally best solution or of improving Test performance.

Why does this work? The E-step makes the lower bound tight; the M-step raises or maintains it. This explanation justifies the alternating operations rather than treating EM as an arbitrary recipe.

16. Derive the M-step formulas rather than memorize them

To derive the updates more formally, take the part of the expected complete-data Log-Likelihood involving one component and hold responsibilities fixed. Constants independent of parameters can be ignored for the purpose of maximization, but not when computing the observed Log-Likelihood displayed earlier.

\[Q_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}[\log\pi_k-\frac12\log\sigma_k^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_k)^2}{2\sigma_k^2}]+C\]

Differentiate with respect to the mean. The derivative of the quadratic penalty is the weighted sum of deviations, divided by the variance:

\[\frac{\partial Q_k}{\partial\mu_k}=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)}{\sigma_k^2}=0\]

Multiplying by the positive variance and rearranging gives μ_k = Σγx / Σγ. Next let v denote σ²; differentiating Q with respect to v gives:

\[\frac{\partial Q_k}{\partial v}=-\frac{N_k}{2v}+\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)^2}{2v^2}=0\]

Multiplying by 2v², we obtain −N_k v + Σγ(x−μ)²=0, hence v=σ²_k = Σγ(x−μ_k)²/N_k. This identifies every algebraic operation needed for the variance update. Finally, maximize ΣN_k log π_k subject to Σπ_k=1. A Lagrange Multiplier λ gives N_k/π_k−λ=0; summing π_k=N_k/λ across components yields λ=n and thus π_k=N_k/n.

This is the same weighted-mean, weighted-variance and effective-count logic already verified numerically. It is also why zero effective count causes difficulties.

17. Soft responsibilities versus k-Means hard assignments

k-Means alternates between assigning each observation to its nearest centroid and updating each centroid using only its assigned observations. GMM-EM instead weights every observation by a continuous responsibility, and adjusts mixing weights and covariance, not just centers. Both alternate assignment-like and update-like steps, but they optimize different objectives.

For our x=4 observation, k-Means with centers at 2.5 and 8.5 would assign 100% to center 1. GMM initially assigns 90.465% to component 1 and 9.535% to component 2. This difference grows near the overlap boundary and becomes smaller when the fitted Gaussians are well separated.

When do predictions agree? A narrow, equal-variance, well-separated GMM often gives near-zero or near-one responsibilities, which resemble hard assignments. When do they differ? Unequal variances or mixing weights can move a GMM's equal-responsibility boundary away from the midpoint between means. Unlike k-Means, an observation may be more likely under a broader component even if another mean is closer.

18. Initialization, local optima and label switching

EM is an iterative local optimization algorithm. Starting with both means at the same value or one variance extremely small can produce different paths and possibly poor solutions. This is why implementations often use multiple initializations, report the best achieved Training Log-Likelihood and regularize covariance. The multiple starts improve reliability but cannot guarantee the global maximum.

Label switching is harmless for density estimation. If two fitted components are swapped together with their weights, means and variances, the mixture density remains unchanged. Component numbers have no physical meaning without external interpretation. This differs from label leakage: there are no observed labels in our unsupervised example.

An unusually high Training Likelihood could arise from one tiny Gaussian concentrating on a single observation. Such a singularity is not a meaningful discovery of a fault mechanism. We will examine covariance regularization and model selection, which help limit this behavior.

19. From one sensor to multivariate Gaussian mixtures

Industrial systems rarely rely on only one sensor. With d Features, each component has a mean vector of length d and a Covariance Matrix of dimension d by d. The diagonal entries are individual Feature variances; the off-diagonal entries describe how two Features vary together within a component.

\[\phi_d(x;\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{\exp[-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)]}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\]

Here x and μ are d-dimensional column vectors, Σ is a symmetric positive-definite covariance matrix, its determinant |Σ| measures overall volume, and the quadratic form uses Mahalanobis distance rather than ordinary Euclidean distance. An invertible positive-definite covariance is required for this formula.

We may choose full, tied, diagonal or spherical covariance structures in scikit-learn. Full allows orientation and correlation, diagonal suppresses within-component correlations, tied shares a covariance across components, and spherical uses a single variance per component. More flexible covariance can fit complicated shapes, but increases parameters and overfitting risk.

Numerical bridge from PCA to correlated Gaussian components

Guide 25 showed that the direction and scale of variation matter. A full-covariance GMM also uses direction: a component can form a tilted ellipse instead of a circle. The following two-Feature example is synthetic. Both Features have been transformed to dimensionless Training-standardized values; fitting these transformations using Test would be leakage. Neither PCA nor Whitening is required to fit a GMM.

Take two equal-weight components with the same covariance and centers \(\mu_A=(0,0)^T\), \(\mu_B=(2,2)^T\). Evaluate the new reading \(x=(1,0)^T\). First assume a diagonal covariance \(\Sigma_D=I\), the 2×2 identity. The squared distances are \(d_A^2=(1-0)^2+(0-0)^2=1\) and \(d_B^2=(1-2)^2+(0-2)^2=1+4=5\). With equal weights and a common determinant, the normalization factors cancel:

\[P(A\mid x,\Sigma_D)=\frac{e^{-1/2}}{e^{-1/2}+e^{-5/2}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2}}\approx0.880797.\]

Now retain the same observation and centers but assume a positive within-component correlation of 0.6:

\[\Sigma_F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.6\\0.6&1\end{pmatrix},\quad |\Sigma_F|=1-0.36=0.64,\quad \Sigma_F^{-1}=\frac1{0.64}\begin{pmatrix}1&-0.6\\-0.6&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The deviations are \(x-\mu_A=(1,0)^T\) and \(x-\mu_B=(-1,-2)^T\). Explicit multiplication gives \(d_A^2=1/0.64=1.5625\) and \(d_B^2=(1+4-2(0.6)(1)(2))/0.64=2.6/0.64=4.0625\). Therefore:

\[P(A\mid x,\Sigma_F)=\frac{e^{-1.5625/2}}{e^{-1.5625/2}+e^{-4.0625/2}}=\frac1{1+e^{-1.25}}\approx0.777300.\]

20. Why singular covariance is a serious failure mode

Imagine a one-dimensional component centered exactly on a Training observation while its variance tends to zero. The Gaussian density at that observation is inversely proportional to its standard deviation; hence the density can grow without bound. Because Log-Likelihood contains log density, the objective can increase without bound in an unconstrained Gaussian mixture. That means the unconstrained problem may have no finite maximum.

A practical tool such as scikit-learn's GaussianMixture uses reg_covar: a small positive number added to covariance diagonals to improve numerical stability. It is not a cure for every statistical problem and does not replace validation or careful feature measurement. Standardize Features when their scales make covariance estimation unstable, but learn those scaling values on Training only.

What should you diagnose? Watch for components with near-zero weights, tiny covariance eigenvalues, unrealistic density spikes, large sensitivity to random initialization and unexpectedly poor validation density. Each is more informative than merely declaring that the EM algorithm has converged.

21. How many components? Validation and information criteria

A mixture with more components can often fit Training data more tightly, but additional parameters can generalize poorly. One approach is to choose K using Validation mean log density, evaluating each candidate on held-out observations. Another is Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which penalizes parameter count and is computed on Training; lower BIC is preferred among the tested candidates.

\[\mathrm{BIC}=p\log n-2\ell_{\rm train},\qquad p=3K-1\ \text{(1D, full variances)}\]

Why p=3K−1? We estimate K means, K variances and K mixture weights, but the sum-to-one constraint removes one free weight: K+K+(K−1)=3K−1. For more dimensions or another covariance type, count parameters differently.

23. Test evaluation, refitting and avoiding leakage

Once K=2 is chosen using only the development data, we must keep Test untouched during model choice. For the experiment above, the K=2 model fitted on 72 Training observations has a Test mean log density of approximately −2.31579 when evaluated on the reserved 24 observations. We report this value rather than searching for an alternative K after inspecting Test.

In a production workflow, one may refit the selected modeling procedure on Training+Validation before final Test evaluation; if doing so, the refit must be explicitly distinguished from the already reported 72-observation fit, since its Test score will not automatically be the same. The code here deliberately evaluates the fitted Training-only object so that all printed scores match the table.

Data Leakage also applies to unsupervised learning. Computing a scaler, feature selector or PCA using Validation/Test before choosing K contaminates evaluation, even though no labels were used. Fit preprocessing on each Training fold when using Cross-Validation and reuse its frozen settings on the corresponding held-out observations.

24. Interpreting probabilities, anomaly scores and uncertainty

GaussianMixture.predict_proba(X) returns component responsibilities for new readings, based on the already fitted parameters. These values describe membership in the model, not event risk. In contrast, score_samples(X) provides a log probability density for each new reading: low fitted density may flag an unusual observation, but low density depends strongly on units, dimension and the training population.

A model could assign a reading 99% responsibility for component 2 and still give that reading low overall density if it lies far from both component centers. Conversely, a reading near an overlap region can have roughly 50/50 responsibility while having relatively high overall density. Membership uncertainty and anomaly detection are therefore distinct quantities.

For industrial decision-making, calibrated failure probabilities would require observed fault labels or additional modeling. Without them, speaking of two clusters of measurement patterns is defensible; claiming a 99% chance of a mechanical fault is not.

Numerical counterexample: component membership is not typicality

Let us use the updated Training parameters from section 12, rather than invent a second model: \(\pi_A=\pi_B=0.5\), \(\mu_A=2.61200043\), \(\mu_B=8.38799957\), and \(\sigma_A^2=\sigma_B^2=1.90945848\) (sensor-units squared). For each new reading \(x\), compute the two weighted densities \(w_A=0.5\,\phi(x;\mu_A,\sigma_A^2)\), \(w_B=0.5\,\phi(x;\mu_B,\sigma_B^2)\); then calculate \(p(x)=w_A+w_B\) and \(\gamma_B=w_B/p(x)\).

At A, x = 2.61200043: \(w_A\approx0.144352716\), \(w_B\approx0.000023196\), so \(p(x)\approx0.144375912\), \(\gamma_B\approx0.000160665\) and \(\log p(x)\approx-1.935335\). At B, x = 5.5: symmetry gives \(w_A=w_B\approx0.016252597\), so \(\gamma_B=0.5\), \(p(x)\approx0.032505193\), and \(\log p(x)\approx-3.426355\). At C, x = 16: \(w_A\approx5.97\times10^{-22}\) and \(w_B\approx3.71611\times10^{-8}\); therefore \(p(x)\approx3.71611\times10^{-8}\), \(\gamma_B\approx1\) and \(\log p(x)\approx-17.108004\).

25. Python: reproduce EM manually with transparent arrays

This first program uses only NumPy and SciPy. It reconstructs the E-step, the M-step and the observed-data Log-Likelihood, rather than calling a library estimator that conceals the calculation. The logsumexp operation stabilizes sums of exponentials and helps prevent numerical underflow when densities become very small. Try checking the first and fourth rows against our hand calculations.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp

x = np.array([1., 2., 3., 4., 7., 8., 9., 10.])
weights = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
means = np.array([2.5, 8.5])
variances = np.array([4.0, 4.0])

def log_joint(x, weights, means, variances):
    return (np.log(weights)[None, :]
            - 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * variances)[None, :]
            - (x[:, None] - means[None, :])**2 /
              (2 * variances[None, :]))

before = logsumexp(log_joint(x, weights, means, variances),
                   axis=1).sum()
logj = log_joint(x, weights, means, variances)
responsibility = np.exp(logj - logsumexp(logj, axis=1)[:, None])

nk = responsibility.sum(axis=0)
new_weights = nk / len(x)
new_means = (responsibility * x[:, None]).sum(axis=0) / nk
new_variances = (responsibility *
                 (x[:, None] - new_means[None, :])**2).sum(axis=0) / nk

after = logsumexp(log_joint(x, new_weights, new_means,
                            new_variances), axis=1).sum()
print('Means:', new_means)
print('Variances:', new_variances)
print('Log-likelihoods:', before, after)

# Diagnostic: responsibility is not the same as overall density.
from scipy.stats import norm, multivariate_normal
check_x = np.array([new_means[0], 5.5, 16.0])
contributions = new_weights[None, :] * norm.pdf(
    check_x[:, None], loc=new_means[None, :],
    scale=np.sqrt(new_variances)[None, :])
full_density = contributions.sum(axis=1)
p_component_2 = contributions[:, 1] / full_density
for reading, responsibility_b, density in zip(check_x, p_component_2, full_density):
    print(f"Diagnostic x={reading:.6f}: P(B|x)={responsibility_b:.9f}, "
          f"p(x)={density:.10g}, log p(x)={np.log(density):.6f}")

# Covariance assumption: same observation and means, different posteriors.
centers_2d = np.array([[0., 0.], [2., 2.]])
sensor_2d = np.array([1., 0.])
for structure, covariance in (("diagonal", np.eye(2)),
                              ("correlated", np.array([[1., .6], [.6, 1.]]))):
    component_densities = multivariate_normal.pdf(
        sensor_2d, mean=centers_2d[0], cov=covariance), multivariate_normal.pdf(
        sensor_2d, mean=centers_2d[1], cov=covariance)
    posterior_a = component_densities[0] / sum(component_densities)
    print(f"2D {structure}: P(A|x)={posterior_a:.6f}")

Expected results: means approximately [2.612000, 8.388000], variances [1.909458, 1.909458], and Log-Likelihood −19.432151 → −18.106405. logsumexp improves numerical stability; it does not change the mathematical objective.

26. Python: select K with scikit-learn without peeking at Test

This second program simulates a more realistic situation with 120 observations. Unlike the hand example, the data are generated from two overlapping Gaussians with unequal variances. The model does not get the hidden generating component labels. We reserve Test before searching K, use the same Training and Validation splits for every candidate, and do not refit after Test evaluation. The random generator and initialization seed make the results reproducible in the stated environment.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture

rng = np.random.default_rng(26)
z = rng.choice(2, size=120, p=[0.55, 0.45])
readings = rng.normal(np.where(z == 0, 2.5, 8.5),
                      np.where(z == 0, 1.1, 1.5)).reshape(-1, 1)
order = rng.permutation(120)
train = readings[order[:72]]
validation = readings[order[72:96]]
test = readings[order[96:]]

candidates = {}
for k in (1, 2, 3, 4):
    gmm = GaussianMixture(n_components=k, n_init=10,
                          random_state=26, reg_covar=1e-4)
    gmm.fit(train)
    candidates[k] = gmm
    print(k, 'Validation:', round(gmm.score(validation), 5),
          'BIC:', round(gmm.bic(train), 2))

chosen_k = max(candidates, key=lambda k: candidates[k].score(validation))
selected = candidates[chosen_k]
print('Chosen K:', chosen_k)
print('Test mean log density:', round(selected.score(test), 5))

The selected K is 2, and the resulting Test mean log density for the Training-only fit is approximately −2.31579. The score() method returns mean Log Density per observation; bic() returns a criterion using the total Training Log-Likelihood and number of free parameters. They should not be compared numerically as though they used the same scale.

27. Common mistakes and their precise corrections

The following mistakes are not merely terminology issues; each can change the analysis or the conclusion.

  1. Density = probability: incorrect for exact continuous readings. Integrate density over an interval for a probability.
  2. Mixture weight = posterior responsibility: π describes the whole model; γ depends on one observed x.
  3. Responsibility = fault probability: components have no fault meaning without labels and validation.
  4. M-step uses unweighted means: that silently changes soft EM into another procedure.
  5. Variance uses n−1: standard Gaussian MLE updates divide by effective count N_k.
  6. Freeze initial responsibilities forever: every EM iteration requires a fresh E-step.
  7. Compute log of an average log: Log-Likelihood is the sum of logs of full mixture densities.
  8. Convergence = global optimum: EM may settle at a local solution; use different initializations.
  9. Swap component IDs = changed model: label switching changes names, not the summed density.
  10. Use Test to choose K: contaminates the final assessment of predictive density.
  11. Assume more K is always better: Training fit can improve while Validation worsens.
  12. Ignore tiny covariance: density spikes and singularity can invalidate interpretation.
  13. Compare raw densities across incompatible units: change of units changes density numerically.
  14. Treat cluster shapes as business truths: validate any operational interpretation independently.

A good diagnostic is to identify exactly which quantity the code computes—component density, mixture density, responsibility, or a loss—and what its denominator and units mean.

28. Solved exercises: reproduce, interpret and transfer

Use the collapsible answers after trying each problem. Each response explains the calculation or the reason, not just a final number. To test transfer, some exercises modify the assumptions rather than recycling the same observation.

1. Why is a responsibility not a physical label?

It is conditional probability under the fitted mixture parameters, not a verified fault category. Two components can exchange numerical IDs without changing the density; real labels require outside measurements.

2. Check the posterior for x = 4.

Divide 0.07528436 by 0.07528436+0.00793491=0.08321927 to obtain 0.904651. The complement is 0.095349; they sum to one.

3. What is the effective number of observations in component 1?

Sum its eight responsibilities. The result is N1=4. Effective counts need not generally be integers, but here symmetry makes the count exactly four.

4. Compute the updated component 1 mean.

Use weighted sum 10.448002 divided by N1=4. The resulting mean is 2.6120005, approximately 2.612000 sensor units.

5. Compute the component 1 updated variance.

Using unrounded sums, compute 34.92801888/4≈8.73200472 and subtract (2.61200043)^2≈6.82254625. The variance is approximately 1.90945847 sensor-units squared.

6. Why is the variance denominator N1 instead of N1−1?

The M-step maximizes the Gaussian expected complete-data Log-Likelihood. Its Maximum Likelihood variance solution uses total responsibility N1, not the unbiased sample-variance correction.

7. Show improvement in observed Log-Likelihood.

−18.106405−(−19.432151)=+1.325746. Higher observed-data Log-Likelihood on identical Training readings means the fit improved at that iteration; it does not prove better Test generalization.

8. Does 0.95 responsibility imply 95% failure probability?

No. It means the fitted model assigns 95% conditional probability to a latent component. The relationship between that component and genuine failures is unknown unless independently validated.

9. What happens if two component IDs are swapped?

The summed mixture density stays identical provided their means, variances and weights are swapped together. The new numbering is not a new fitted statistical model.

10. What is wrong with using the old responsibilities in the next iteration?

Then the supposed E-step has not recomputed the conditional distribution for the new parameters. Valid EM alternates recomputed responsibilities and updated parameters.

11. Why can Training Log-Likelihood overreward complexity?

Extra components can fit particular Training readings, including tiny high-density peaks. Validation density on unseen observations and complexity penalties help expose unstable fits.

12. Compute the 1D parameter count for K=3.

Three means + three variances + (3−1) independent mixture weights = 8 free parameters. Equivalently 3K−1=8.

13. Compare K=2 with K=4 in the experiment.

K=2 has Validation score −2.48870, better than K=4 at −2.49316 (higher is better). K=4 has slightly higher Training score, illustrating a generalization tradeoff.

14. Does EM convergence guarantee a global maximum?

No. Exact EM gives nondecreasing Training Likelihood, but it may converge to a local stationary point. Multiple starts and diagnostics are useful.

15. What does reg_covar protect against?

Adding a small diagonal covariance term discourages nearly singular components and stabilizes matrix calculations. It cannot guarantee the correct number or meaning of components.

16. How can a point have 99% responsibility yet be anomalous?

Responsibilities normalize component densities relative to one another. Both components may have very low absolute density at the point; being the most likely among them does not make the point typical.

A future Jupyter laboratory could reproduce the manual EM iteration, add a third component, plot the individual densities, vary reg_covar and compare Validation Log Density across several random initializations. The laboratory should record what changed and why, instead of ranking outputs by appearance alone.

17. Calculate the correlated-covariance posterior for x=(1,0).

With common positive-definite covariance Σ=[[1,0.6],[0.6,1]], first obtain det(Σ)=0.64 and Σ⁻¹=[[1,−0.6],[−0.6,1]]/0.64. The squared Mahalanobis distances to (0,0) and (2,2) are 1.5625 and 4.0625. Their difference is 2.5. For equal mixing weights, P(A|x)=1/(1+exp(−2.5/2))=1/(1+exp(−1.25))≈0.777300. For identity covariance the probability was ≈0.880797; covariance structure, not a changed observation, caused the difference.

18. Can a near-certain component assignment still accompany extremely low density?

Yes. With the updated mixture parameters, at x=16 we obtain p(x)≈3.71611×10⁻⁸ and P(B|x) extremely close to one. The posterior answers which of the two components is comparatively more plausible; it does not say the observation is typical or safe. The score log p(x)≈−17.108004 is relative to the model and must be interpreted with the measurement scale.

19. Transfer: what if the new reading is exactly between the fitted means?

At x=(2.61200043+8.38799957)/2=5.5, component variances and weights are equal, and both components have the same squared deviation 2.888². Hence w_A=w_B, P(A|x)=P(B|x)=0.5. However p(5.5)≈0.032505193 density units, not a probability of being “normal” or a 50% probability of a fault. Without external fault labels, component names have no physical risk meaning.

29. Glossary, method selection and interpretation

Gaussian Mixture Model: a weighted sum of Gaussian component densities. Latent Variable: an unobserved quantity such as component membership. Responsibility: posterior probability of a component conditional on a reading and the fitted model. E-step: update responsibilities using current parameters. M-step: update parameters by maximizing expected complete-data Log-Likelihood with responsibilities fixed. Covariance: within-component spread and relationships among Features. Log-Likelihood: log of the model density of observed Training data summed over observations. BIC: Training fit with a parameter-count penalty. Label Switching: equivalent relabeling of latent components. Singularity: an unstable fit with vanishing covariance.

When to consider GMM: overlapping groups of continuous measurements where an interpretable density model and uncertainty about membership are valuable. When to hesitate: heavy tails, strongly non-Gaussian shapes, many dimensions with too little data, components that collapse or shift radically across random starts, and tasks where a density model is not the ultimate goal. Compare against simpler density models and domain baselines.

Connection to the next stage: the same EM idea appears beyond Gaussian mixtures, but its particular E-step and M-step depend on the probabilistic model. Learn to derive the conditional latent-variable distribution and optimize the expected complete-data objective, not merely to memorize the GMM equations.
