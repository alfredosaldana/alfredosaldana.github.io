1. Why data preparation is part of the model—not clerical cleanup

Imagine a hospital wants to estimate whether a patient will be readmitted within 30 days of discharge. Its raw information comes from clinical systems that were built to document care, not to deliver a rectangular table ready for Machine Learning. One record says “Medicine,” another says “MED”; a laboratory result is absent because the test was not ordered; a patient's age is entered in years, while the next system records date of birth. A mathematically sophisticated classifier cannot repair those ambiguities on its own.

Data Preparation establishes trustworthy meaning, types, units, timing and consistent representations. Feature Engineering constructs or transforms variables so a model can learn relevant relationships more effectively. They overlap but are not synonymous: converting an invalid laboratory string into a missing value is preparation; computing a documented visits per 90 days feature from dated encounters is Feature Engineering. Neither guarantees predictive improvement.

Our learning objective: start with an illustrated hospital-discharge dataset, audit its meaning, make defensible preprocessing decisions using Training only, calculate the transformations by hand, and explain how the same transformation must operate on future patients. The result is a reproducible Feature Pipeline—not a medical decision rule.

What makes a Feature useful?

A Feature should be available at the prediction moment, meaningful in the domain, measurable consistently, and appropriate for the intended task. A code indicating an appointment after discharge might correlate remarkably with readmission but could be unavailable on discharge day. Patient identifiers may be strong memorization keys yet generalize poorly and pose privacy concerns. A Feature can therefore be statistically associated with the target while being operationally invalid.

Suppose we use Age, prior visits, hospital length of stay, service and a creatinine laboratory result to illustrate preprocessing. We choose these only as educational variables, not as a validated clinical risk specification. On discharge day, a responsible system would confirm which records had actually been finalized and would involve clinicians in defining each measurement. Our example labels are invented; no result can support real treatment decisions.

The key distinction: fitting versus transforming

Not every data transformation needs to learn from the dataset. A verified height of 170 centimeters becomes 1.70 meters by dividing by 100. That conversion factor is known in advance: we do not need to calculate it from patients' heights.

Other transformations must learn a reference value from Training. For example, our creatinine records include a missing value for patient P5. Among the seven Training patients with observed creatinine, the median is 1.3 mg/dL (the full calculation appears in Section 5). An Imputer must first learn and store that median. The stored number is a fitted preprocessing parameter—it is not an observed laboratory result for P5, nor is it a parameter learned by the final prediction model.

Think of fit() as learning and saving the settings, and transform() as applying those saved settings. We also transform Training after fitting; Validation, Test and future patients use the same settings without fitting again. If we recalculated the median using the complete dataset, it would become 1.25 mg/dL. Then patients reserved for evaluation would have influenced a Training decision, even though we never used their readmission labels. This is a form of Data Leakage; Section 5 shows both median calculations in detail.

Remember the sequence: split the data → fit() the Imputer or Scaler on Training → transform() Training and apply the same fitted transformation to Validation, Test and new observations. A Scaler follows this identical logic, except it stores quantities such as the Training mean and standard deviation instead of a median.

Success will not be “we have no null values.” It will be a documented, reproducible representation whose information was legitimately available at the stated time, supported by appropriate model evaluation and domain review.

Data dictionary. In an actual study, each definition requires a written clinical and temporal specification.

Establish the prediction timestamp

We assume that prediction occurs at discharge (\(t_0\)). Inputs must be available at or before this moment; the Target becomes known only after the following 30 days. If a record was entered two days after discharge, its event date alone does not prove it was accessible at the prediction timestamp. Distinguish event time, entry time, and availability time in real systems.

Why a clean table is not necessarily an unbiased table

Our 12 rows include six positive outcomes, a deliberately unrealistic-looking balance for a general teaching case. That does not establish any real readmission rate. Selection rules matter: repeated discharges, incomplete follow-up, deaths, transfers, and care outside the hospital network can all affect the meaning of a label. Before modeling, determine the eligible population and how missing outcomes differ from confirmed non-readmission. We will use this small table to learn preprocessing, not claim clinical effectiveness.

3. Protect Training, Validation and Test before fitting any transformations

The split is a scientific boundary. Training may estimate preprocessing statistics and model parameters. Validation can help choose alternative pipelines or hyperparameters; after such choices it is no longer an untouched final measure of the entire search. Test must remain reserved for an independent assessment once the analysis plan is frozen. These roles apply to preprocessing as much as to modeling.

Training — P1–P8

Eight January observations determine medians, category vocabulary, scaling parameters, potential Feature Engineering rules and model fitting.

Validation — P9–P10

Two later observations assess candidate preprocessing + modeling combinations. No imputer or scaler is newly fitted on these rows.

Test — P11–P12

Two still-later observations remain untouched during development. At evaluation, apply every learned transformation as already fitted.

Production

A future discharge must pass through the same schema and transformation sequence. New values and categories need explicit policies.

Chronological grouping matches the future-oriented question better than an indiscriminate random split in some settings. But time alone does not solve everything. If the same patient appears repeatedly, related episodes may cross partitions; a group-aware or patient-level strategy might also be necessary. Likewise, label maturation means we cannot train on a February discharge's “no readmission in 30 days” outcome until enough future time has passed.

Numerical demonstration: a change that looks harmless

Consider the missing creatinine for P5. A Training-only imputer will later compute 1.3 mg/dL. If we incorrectly use all observed values in the complete dataset, the median will be 1.25 mg/dL. The two numbers are close, and a few predictions might even be identical. The methodological problem remains: evaluation rows were allowed to inform a learned transformation. With another dataset or nonlinear model, even a small change can alter outcomes.

By contrast, correcting a fixed, verified unit conversion (for example converting mg/dL to the appropriate SI unit with a documented conversion factor) is not itself a statistic estimated from Validation. Distinguishing known domain rules from learned data-dependent rules prevents both needless restrictions and actual leakage.

Operational test: could we run this exact preprocessing step on discharge day without knowing the eventual readmission or any later cohort's statistics? If not, the step should not be used as written for an independent evaluation.

4. Missing data: identify the mechanism before choosing an Imputer

Missing values have different meanings. A test might not be ordered, a sensor might be unavailable, a record might fail during transfer, or an entire subgroup might have incomplete coverage. Converting all blanks to zero erases those distinctions. First count and investigate missingness, then decide whether imputation, an explicit “not measured” category, a Missingness Indicator, or a different data collection strategy is appropriate.

Calculate missingness, preserving the denominator

Two out of 12 creatinine entries are missing: \(2/12=16.67\%\) over the complete illustrative table. But preprocessing is fitted using Training: one of its eight creatinine entries is missing, \(1/8=12.5\%\). Validation has none missing (\(0/2=0\%\)); Test has one missing (\(1/2=50\%\)). The 50% figure is based on only two records and should not be interpreted as a reliable shift estimate.

MCAR, MAR and MNAR are assumptions about the missingness process

Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) means whether a value is missing does not depend on the observed or unobserved data under consideration. Missing At Random (MAR) means that after conditioning on specified observed variables, missingness does not additionally depend on its unobserved value. Missing Not At Random (MNAR) means missingness still depends on unobserved information, possibly the unmeasured value itself. These are modeling assumptions, not labels that can reliably be inferred from two blank cells.

For example, creatinine might be missing more often after shorter stays because fewer tests were ordered. If length of stay is recorded, it may help explain missingness, which is compatible with some MAR formulations. But if testing is triggered by an undocumented clinical concern correlated with the unmeasured creatinine level, an MNAR mechanism may be plausible. Neither statement proves a mechanism for our fabricated table.

Audit missingness without pretending to know its cause

A good audit separates a blank lab result from a documented “test not indicated,” distinguishes truly zero values from absent ones, and inspects whether missingness varies by time, service or hospital system. A simple indicator \(m_i=1\) when creatinine is missing and 0 otherwise can preserve potentially predictive information. However, such an indicator might also encode unequal patterns of access or clinical ordering, so evaluate performance, fairness and stability rather than assuming it is beneficial.

Common mistake: the assumption “there are only two missing observations, so we can delete them” confuses row count with selection bias. Deleting particular patients can shift the represented population; an imputation strategy can also introduce bias. Neither is automatically safe.

5. A complete numerical example of median, mean and indicator imputation

Only seven Training patients have measured creatinine values. In their patient order (excluding P5), the measurements are 0.9, 1.3, 1.8, 0.8, 2.1, 1.0, 1.6 mg/dL. We will compute two candidate imputations using only these seven measured Training values; we will not use the eventual readmission labels to calculate them.

Step 1 — Sort the values and calculate the median

\[0.8,\ 0.9,\ 1.0,\ \boxed{1.3},\ 1.6,\ 1.8,\ 2.1\]

Because there are seven observed values, the middle observation is the fourth: median = 1.3 mg/dL. Therefore P5's missing creatinine becomes 1.3 after transformation. This substitution is not a measured lab result. The dataset should record that it was imputed, especially if results might be interpreted by a person.

Step 2 — Compare the mean, showing the arithmetic

\[\bar{x}_{\mathrm{observed,train}}=\frac{0.9+1.3+1.8+0.8+2.1+1.0+1.6}{7}=\frac{9.5}{7}\approx1.3571\]

Mean imputation would instead replace P5 with approximately 1.3571 mg/dL. Neither number is inherently more clinically appropriate. Median imputation is less sensitive to extreme observations, but neither method restores the information that was never observed. A clinically informed analysis might avoid imputing certain results altogether.

Step 3 — Add an indicator without confusing it with the outcome

Step 4 — Prove why fitting on the entire table is incorrect

Across all 12 rows there are ten observed creatinine values. Sorted, they are 0.8, 0.9, 0.9, 1.0, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.6, 1.8, 2.1. The median would be \((1.2+1.3)/2=1.25\), rather than Training's 1.3. Replacing a held-out missing result using 1.25 would let information from February and March modify a Training-fitted preprocessing choice.

Key rule: `fit` estimates the median on Training; `transform` applies that same median to Training, Validation, Test and future discharges. The correctness of the workflow depends on where the number was learned, not just on whether it looks reasonable.

When should we use something more sophisticated?

k-nearest-neighbor and iterative imputers can use relationships between other variables, but they introduce additional assumptions, complexity and potential instability. They must be learned within Training and evaluated as part of a Pipeline. On a tiny clinical teaching dataset, such methods cannot be validated convincingly. The correct choice is empirical, domain-dependent, and subject to model evaluation—not a rule that one Imputer always wins.

6. Categorical Encoding: represent information without inventing an order

A categorical Feature takes values such as Medicine, Surgery or Emergency. These are names, not measured quantities. Replacing them with 0, 1 and 2 can inadvertently tell a model that Emergency is “twice Surgery” or that a meaningful numerical distance exists between departments. Ordinal Encoding is appropriate when categories have a justified order (such as documented severity levels), not merely because numbers are easier to store.

One-Hot Encoding from the Training vocabulary

Among P1–P8, the service vocabulary contains only Medicine and Surgery. One-Hot Encoding (OHE) constructs binary columns to indicate membership. P1's Medicine becomes (1, 0), and P2's Surgery becomes (0, 1). Notice that zero in a dummy column means “not this known category,” not “medical severity zero.”

Why unknown categories require an explicit policy

Our February patient P10 belongs to Emergency, which did not occur in the January Training cohort. With scikit-learn's OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), the two fitted indicator columns become 0 and 0. This keeps the input shape consistent, but it is not a positive, explicit representation of Emergency and may behave differently from a known category. In particular, if we use a drop-first Encoding scheme, an all-zero vector could be confounded with the dropped reference category; choosing a suitable encoder policy requires care.

Alternatives include a carefully specified “Other/Unknown” group supported by Training, rethinking whether the category is stable, or fitting a new version of the pipeline when service codes genuinely change. Do not refit the vocabulary on Validation merely to make Emergency appear as a third learned column: that changes the Feature space used by the fitted model.

High cardinality and the hazard of Target Encoding

Service has low cardinality; physician ID or diagnosis code may have thousands of levels. One-Hot Encoding can become wide, sparse and unstable for rare categories. Frequency grouping, hashing or other representations may be useful, but each has information and interpretability trade-offs. Target Encoding replaces categories with statistics involving the outcome, so computing category averages on all rows is direct leakage. Even within Training, using each row's own label to encode itself can produce severe overfitting. Proper cross-fitting or out-of-fold procedures are important and will receive deeper treatment in the validation guide.

Clinical nuance: a department label can reflect administrative practices rather than clinical severity, and the label's meaning may vary by hospital. Encoding preserves the recorded category; it does not establish that categories are comparable across institutions.

When is a numeric code legitimate?

If severity is explicitly ordered as low, medium and high, encoding as 0, 1 and 2 may be defensible for models that use rank, but equal spacing remains an assumption. Trees can use categorical handling differently from linear models. Use the representation that matches domain meaning and algorithm behavior, and verify it on held-out data.

7. Feature Scaling: Standardization, Min–Max and units

Length of stay is measured in days, creatinine in mg/dL, and age in years. Their raw numerical magnitudes are not comparable measures of importance. Some methods, including distance-based models and regularized linear algorithms, respond strongly to relative feature scale. Scaling changes numerical representation; it does not change what a variable measures or make a variable more clinically important.

Standardization: compute the mean and standard deviation using Training

The eight Training ages are 45, 62, 70, 39, 55, 81, 50 and 68, which sum to 470. Therefore the fitted mean is \(\mu_{\mathrm{train}}=470/8=58.75\) years. The population-style standard deviation used by StandardScaler is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{train}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{8}(x_i-58.75)^2/8}\approx13.1696\) years. The divisor is 8 here; a sample descriptive standard deviation with divisor 7 would not be the same scaling convention.

\[z=\frac{x-\mu_{\mathrm{train}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{train}}};\qquad z_{\mathrm{P9}}=\frac{60-58.75}{13.1696}\approx0.0949\]

Interpret P9's standardized Age of approximately 0.095: P9 is about 0.095 Training standard deviations above the Training mean. The result is not a 9.5% higher medical risk. The value zero denotes the fitted mean, not the absence of age, and a negative standardized age does not mean a negative biological age.

See why fitting again on Validation changes the answer

Suppose we incorrectly recompute the Scaler using the eight Training ages plus both Validation ages (P9 = 60, P10 = 47). The combined mean would be \((470+60+47)/10=57.7\), and the new population-style standard deviation would be approximately 12.3130. Patient P9 has not changed age, but its transformed value would change:

Min–Max Scaling: compute minimum and maximum from Training

Training ages range from 39 to 81 years, so the Training range is \(81-39=42\) years. For P9, the transformed value is \((60-39)/42=21/42=0.50\). For Test patient P11, Age 76 becomes \((76-39)/42=37/42\approx0.881\). Both transformations use January's fitted bounds.

\[x_{\mathrm{minmax}}=\frac{x-39}{81-39}\]

A future patient aged 90 would map to \((90-39)/42=51/42\approx1.214\), above 1. This is not a software error: the training range was [39,81], and future observations need not lie inside it. Some implementations can clip values by explicit choice, but clipping removes information about values beyond the fitted range.

When would we choose which method?

Standardization centers and rescales using the Training mean and standard deviation. Min–Max uses Training extrema and is sensitive to extreme values; neither is inherently “normalization of every dataset into a normal distribution.” RobustScaler uses the median and interquartile range, which can be preferable when extreme observations distort classical scaling. Tree splits are often relatively insensitive to monotone rescaling, while nearest-neighbor distances and many gradient-based methods are not. Scaling must occur inside the cross-validation or train/validation pipeline.

8. Outliers, impossible values and robust statistics

An Outlier is an observation that is unusual under a stated reference distribution or domain rule. Unusual is not the same as erroneous. A 20-day hospital stay can be clinically valid; a length of stay of −20 days is incompatible with our definition. Removing both simply because they are far from the average would damage the population we wish to study.

Numerical comparison: a recording error versus a legitimate extreme

Our eight Training stays are 2, 5, 7, 1, 4, 9, 3, 6 days. Their sum is 37 and the mean is \(37/8=4.625\) days. Imagine that a transcription error changes the nine-day stay into 90 days. The sum becomes \(37-9+90=118\), and the mean jumps to \(118/8=14.75\) days. The median, however, remains 4.5 days if we sort the original eight values as 1,2,3,4,5,6,7,9 and the corrupted set as 1,2,3,4,5,6,7,90.

This explains why the mean and the standard deviation are sensitive to extreme values, while the median can remain stable. It does not imply that every large observation should be replaced by the median. First check the original record, the timestamp, units, patient history and whether the data came from a plausible distribution.

Interquartile Range as a descriptive screening rule

One common convention uses the medians of the lower and upper halves. For the sorted Training stays, the lower half is 1,2,3,4 so \(Q_1=(2+3)/2=2.5\); the upper half is 5,6,7,9 so \(Q_3=(6+7)/2=6.5\). Thus \(IQR=6.5-2.5=4\) days. Under the heuristic 1.5×IQR fences, the lower and upper cutoffs are \(2.5-6=-3.5\) and \(6.5+6=12.5\). A 90-day value would be flagged; a 9-day value would not. Software packages use different quantile conventions, so document the definition.

What does a flag mean? It requests investigation, not automatic deletion. The cause might be a data error, an unusual but real patient, a different clinical population, or a shift in recording policy. Deleting rare severe cases may make the model perform worse on patients most in need of reliable predictions.

Why clipping is not a free solution

Winsorization caps values at chosen bounds, potentially reducing influence from extremes, but it can also collapse important distinctions and may need tuning. If the bounds are empirical, fit them on Training only. Record original measurements and a flag for audited corrections where appropriate. A reproducible preprocessing rule states exactly whether values are rejected, corrected from source, clipped, or retained.

9. Skewed Features, transformations and interpreting units

Many real Features are asymmetric: a few patients may have many previous visits, while most have none. A right-skewed variable has a long upper tail. A transformation may make a relationship easier for some models to learn, but it can change interpretation. It does not automatically remove outliers, causal confounding or missing-data bias.

Why log(1+x) is useful for nonnegative counts

Consider hypothetical prior-visit counts of 0, 1, 3 and 15, chosen to emphasize skew. The natural-log transformation \(g(x)=\ln(1+x)\) maps them to 0, 0.6931, 1.3863 and 2.7726. The raw difference between 3 and 15 is 12 visits, but the transformed difference is approximately \(2.7726-1.3863=1.3863\). Large counts receive less incremental weight, and zero remains representable.

\[g(0)=\ln 1=0,\quad g(1)=\ln 2\approx0.6931,\quad g(3)=\ln4\approx1.3863\]

For a linear model with a log-transformed Feature, a unit increase in \(\ln(1+x)\) no longer corresponds to one additional visit; interpretations must refer to the transformed scale. An exponential transform in the inverse direction may be required to display results on the original scale. Never use a log on negative or zero values without checking the definition and mathematical domain.

Different transformations address different problems

Square-root transforms may be used for nonnegative counts; Box–Cox requires positive inputs; Yeo–Johnson allows nonpositive values under its defined implementation. A nonlinear transformation changes relative distances, unlike simple Standardization. Its usefulness must be validated for the chosen algorithm and objective. If a transformation estimates distributional parameters, fit those on Training only.

Check whether the Feature has the right mathematical meaning

A lab concentration, a count of visits and a percentage of missed appointments are not interchangeable. Percentages require defensible denominators; rates depend on time-at-risk; counts over unequal observation windows can be misleading. For our main dataset, previous visits are explicitly counted over the same 90-day interval. If an individual had been enrolled for only 20 of those days, the identical count would mean a different exposure pattern and might justify a separate feature for observation time.

Model-centered decision: use transformations when they correct a well-defined representation problem or improve a validated modeling objective. Do not transform every numerical column merely because a recipe says to. Preserve enough metadata to interpret the original units.

10. Temporal Feature Engineering: construct the predictor that can exist in practice

Feature Engineering is especially valuable when raw systems record events rather than ready-made predictors. Imagine an encounter table with timestamps for each patient's previous visits. To calculate visits in the preceding 90 days at discharge, we count only qualifying visits whose event times precede the discharge snapshot and fall inside the defined lookback. The resulting number is a Feature we can reproduce for historical and future patients.

A complete calculation of a 90-day event count

Suppose a discharge occurs on April 30. The patient has qualifying earlier encounters on February 10, March 15 and April 12, plus a visit scheduled for May 4. The first three occur inside the preceding 90-day window and are already known on April 30, so \(\mathrm{visits}_{90d}=1+1+1=3\). The May 4 event contributes zero to the day-of-discharge feature: whether it happens is future information. The same counting rule must be used when constructing Training rows and Production rows.

For a true event-stream implementation, define whether the discharge encounter itself is excluded, whether transfers count separately, how time zones work, whether a late-entered encounter was accessible by the prediction time, and how duplicates are resolved. These are part of the mathematical definition of the feature, not merely engineering details.

Rates, windows and denominator checks

Suppose two patients each have three visits, but one was observable for 90 days and the other only 30 days. Rates per 30 observed days are \(3/90\times30=1\) and \(3/30\times30=3\), respectively. These calculations describe exposure-adjusted visit frequency under a simple assumption of comparable observation, not clinically validated risk estimates. If observable time is zero, the rate is undefined; a deliberate missing-data policy is needed.

Other useful, cautiously defined temporal Features

Time since last hospitalization, number of medication changes before discharge, and rolling averages of laboratory results are candidate features only if their precise definitions and availability are defensible. A “30-day post-discharge follow-up completed” indicator cannot be used as a discharge-day predictor of readmission during those same 30 days. When future labels involve a 30-day horizon, make sure that training snapshots have sufficient follow-up and that repeated patient episodes do not create unintended overlap.

Prediction is not intervention: a Feature may associate with readmission because it proxies severity, hospital policy or accessibility. Statistical usefulness is not evidence that changing the Feature would cause better health outcomes.

11. Interaction Features and nonlinear relationships

A standard linear score assumes additive contributions unless we explicitly model interactions or transformations. Sometimes the relationship between one Feature and the Target depends on another Feature. Interaction Features let a model represent such conditional effects, but a larger Feature space also increases opportunities for overfitting.

Construct a complete interaction with two patients

For a deliberately hypothetical linear risk score, let \(v\) = prior-visit count and \(l\) = length of stay in days. Define an interaction Feature \(h=vl\), with units visit-days. P2 has three prior visits and a five-day stay, so \(h_{P2}=3\times5=15\). P3 has four prior visits and a seven-day stay, so \(h_{P3}=4\times7=28\).

\[s=\beta_0+\beta_1v+\beta_2l+\beta_3(vl)\]

If, solely for illustration, \(\beta_0=-1,\beta_1=0.2,\beta_2=0.1,\beta_3=0.05\), then P2's score is \(-1+0.2(3)+0.1(5)+0.05(15)=0.85\). P3's score is \(-1+0.2(4)+0.1(7)+0.05(28)=1.90\). These are arbitrary scores from invented coefficients, not estimated readmission probabilities or a validated model. A Logistic Regression could transform a fitted score through a Sigmoid to produce a probability, subject to evaluation and Calibration.

Interpret what changes when an interaction is present

Holding length of stay at \(l\), a one-visit increase changes the linear score by \(\beta_1+\beta_3l\), not just \(\beta_1\). With the illustrative values and \(l=5\), this is \(0.2+0.05(5)=0.45\) score units. If \(l=7\), it is 0.55. Thus the interpretation of the visit coefficient depends on the stay length. This is why feature interactions require more careful explanation than “multiply two columns.”

Polynomial Features can add squared terms such as \(l^2\), enabling smooth curvature in linear models. Trees may already capture some nonlinearities and interactions without explicitly creating each product. Use domain knowledge and honest Validation results to decide whether complexity improves generalization. Avoid constructing hundreds of features from twelve observations.

Collinearity warning: interaction terms can correlate strongly with their components; centering variables can make some coefficient interpretations easier, but it does not change the substantive need for correct validation.

12. Feature Selection: useful signal, redundancy and unstable shortcuts

Adding Features is not always beneficial. A variable can increase training fit while hurting future generalization; a high-cardinality identifier can memorize past patients; and two almost-duplicate measurements can complicate coefficient interpretation. Feature Selection seeks a defensible subset of predictors using a criterion that belongs inside Training or the authorized cross-validation procedure.

Filter, wrapper and embedded approaches

Filter methods screen predictors using statistics such as Training-only variance or association, without fitting every candidate predictive model. Wrapper methods repeatedly fit and compare models using a controlled internal validation procedure. Embedded methods select or shrink predictors as part of fitting, as Lasso can do under appropriate conditions. None is automatically superior. Association-based screening involving the Target must remain inside Training folds, not be computed once on the full dataset before Cross-Validation.

Variance and correlation are not importance by themselves

If a column called Hospital country is exactly the same for every Training patient, it has zero Training variance, so it cannot distinguish those training rows by itself. Yet automatically dropping a column that is constant in Training can be problematic when new hospitals appear later, and software handling must be planned. A strongly correlated pair, such as length of stay measured in days and hours, carries largely duplicated information. This can destabilize individual linear-model coefficients; it does not prove that either predictor is causally irrelevant.

Conversely, a Feature with low marginal correlation can be valuable through interactions. An example is an XOR pattern: if a target changes only when two binary values differ, each binary value separately can have little apparent association with the Target while the pair jointly predicts it. Screening based exclusively on univariate correlations could discard both. Evaluate models, not isolated coefficients, when claiming usefulness.

Why missingness and fairness make selection harder

A recorded hospital or service code may proxy access or treatment policy. Removing direct demographic fields does not automatically eliminate discriminatory proxy information from other variables. Conversely, excluding potentially relevant information can reduce performance for certain groups. There is no single statistical filter that resolves these concerns. Feature selection requires purpose, privacy review, subgroup evaluation and a clear human governance process.

Correct sequence: establish the split → choose and fit candidate Feature Selection using only allowed Training information → validate the entire preprocessing-and-modeling Pipeline → compare the selected approach with a baseline → reserve untouched Test data for final evaluation.

13. Data Leakage: work through three failure modes rather than memorizing a warning

Data Leakage can enter through features, transformations, model selection, or repeated patient identities. It creates a mismatch between what a model sees during development and what it could genuinely see when asked to make a new prediction. Sometimes the leaked input is obvious; more often it is disguised as an ordinary administrative field or an innocent-looking preprocessing statistic.

Failure A — a future outcome masquerading as an input

Suppose a column called 30-day follow-up status is filled in two weeks after discharge. If it reveals that a patient was rehospitalized, a classifier predicting 30-day readmission would effectively be reading part of its future target. The model might achieve high historical accuracy without learning useful discharge-day risk. The remedy is to reconstruct the Feature table as of discharge and audit event and availability timestamps.

Failure B — numeric preprocessing fitted on all observations

Our seven measured Training creatinine values have median 1.3 mg/dL. Including later measured values changes the median to 1.25. That produces a subtle difference in P5's imputed value, and if other modeling components use that imputation the estimated model can change. The key question is not whether 0.05 mg/dL seems small: the transformation would have been informed by observations supposedly held back for evaluation.

Failure C — outcome-guided Encoding or Feature Selection before splitting

If we calculate a service-specific readmission rate using all 12 outcomes and encode each patient with that number, the Test labels contribute to their own prediction inputs. Similarly, ranking all Features by their correlations with all outcomes before Cross-Validation lets the evaluation target influence the selected feature subset. The correct procedure nests any target-dependent preprocessing and selection inside Training or the relevant cross-validation fold.

Patient duplication adds another dimension. When multiple stays for the same person are distributed across Training and Test, a model may exploit persistent person-specific information. Depending on the scientific objective, group splits, temporal gaps and careful definition of the future target may be required. Neither a date split nor a random split automatically solves all forms of leakage.

Audit questions that actually prevent failure

For every Feature ask: Was its value known by discharge? What system recorded it? Was the calculation trained on Training only? Was the definition identical across cohorts? Could the same patient or event appear in multiple partitions? Was the preprocessing option chosen by looking at Test results? An auditable Feature lineage document answers these questions with timestamps, transformations, code versions and source definitions—not with an unsupported statement that leakage was “handled.”

Independent transfer case: cold-chain shipments, without borrowing hospital statistics

Its temperature excursion feature is the maximum number of degrees Celsius (°C) by which a sensor exceeded the required range during the completed shipment. 14. A reproducible scikit-learn ColumnTransformer and Pipeline

We now assemble the theory into a small code example. This is an illustration of preprocessing mechanics using our synthetic dataset—not a clinical prediction system. In real applications, validation of patient identities, timestamps, governance, and larger independent samples would be essential. We will fit preprocessing parameters and a classifier on January Training rows only, then transform February and March using that fitted object.

Reproduce the input table and create a fixed model schema

The following code rebuilds all twelve rows, explicitly identifies the chronological groups, and connects different preprocessing rules to numeric and categorical columns. Numeric missing values are filled with a Training median, optionally preserving missingness indicators. Age and other numeric features are then standardized. Service categories are imputed and One-Hot Encoded, with unknown categories handled according to a documented encoder setting.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rows = [
    ("P1", 45, 1, 2, "Medicine", 0.9, 0),
    ("P2", 62, 3, 5, "Surgery", 1.3, 1),
    ("P3", 70, 4, 7, "Medicine", 1.8, 1),
    ("P4", 39, 0, 1, "Surgery", 0.8, 0),
    ("P5", 55, 2, 4, "Medicine", np.nan, 0),
    ("P6", 81, 5, 9, "Medicine", 2.1, 1),
    ("P7", 50, 1, 3, "Surgery", 1.0, 0),
    ("P8", 68, 3, 6, "Surgery", 1.6, 1),
    ("P9", 60, 2, 5, "Medicine", 1.4, 1),
    ("P10", 47, 1, 2, "Emergency", 0.9, 0),
    ("P11", 76, 4, 8, "Medicine", np.nan, 1),
    ("P12", 58, 2, 4, "Surgery", 1.2, 0),
]
columns = ["id", "age", "visits90", "stay", "service", "creatinine", "readmit"]
df = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=columns)
features = ["age", "visits90", "stay", "service", "creatinine"]
train, validation, test = df.iloc[:8], df.iloc[8:10], df.iloc[10:]

numeric = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True)),
    ("scale", StandardScaler()),
])
categorical = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")),
    ("onehot", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")),
])
preprocess = ColumnTransformer([
    ("numeric", numeric, ["age", "visits90", "stay", "creatinine"]),
    ("category", categorical, ["service"]),
])
model = Pipeline([
    ("preprocess", preprocess),
    ("classifier", LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
])
model.fit(train[features], train["readmit"])
val_pred = model.predict(validation[features])
held_out = model.predict(test[features])
print("Validation predictions:", val_pred.tolist())
print("Test predictions:", held_out.tolist())
print("Training median creatinine:",
      model.named_steps["preprocess"]
           .named_transformers_["numeric"]
           .named_steps["impute"].statistics_[-1])
# Audit the fitted schema without fitting on held-out observations.
transformer = model.named_steps["preprocess"]
feature_names = transformer.get_feature_names_out().tolist()
p10_matrix = transformer.transform(validation.loc[validation["id"] == "P10", features])
p11_matrix = transformer.transform(test.loc[test["id"] == "P11", features])
print("Transformed columns:", feature_names)
print("Validation P10:", np.round(p10_matrix[0], 4).tolist())
print("Test P11:", np.round(p11_matrix[0], 4).tolist())
assert len(feature_names) == 7
assert p10_matrix.shape == (1, 7) and p11_matrix.shape == (1, 7)
assert np.allclose(p10_matrix[0][-2:], [0.0, 0.0])
assert np.isclose(p11_matrix[0][4], 2.64575131, atol=1e-7)

The median print should be 1.3. The model is deliberately fitted on only eight cases, so its probability estimates and apparent accuracy are not reliable. We include its predictions only to illustrate a reproducible software workflow. The test labels are not used by fit, but in a real investigation Test predictions would be examined only after choosing the final procedure using development data.

Understand what each line accomplishes

ColumnTransformer applies different operations to different feature types. SimpleImputer computes training statistics; StandardScaler subsequently learns moments from the imputed Training matrix; OneHotEncoder learns only Training categories. The outer Pipeline fits the whole sequence on Training, then applies the fitted preprocessing before model predictions. Merely writing a Pipeline is not enough if the data have already been contaminated before entering it.

Connect this idea to the code: model.fit(train[features], train["readmit"]) fits the Imputer, Scaler, Encoder and classifier using Training. Later, model.predict(validation[features]) applies their stored transformations and produces predictions; it does not fit them again. The same applies to model.predict(test[features]). Because these steps are inside the Pipeline, predict() handles transformation internally.

Important coding nuance: the numeric imputer with add_indicator=True adds indicators for columns that had missing values during fitting. With our Training data, creatinine is such a column. The appended indicator is also passed through StandardScaler here, so it may not remain literally 0/1 after scaling. If binary interpretability matters, use a carefully designed separate transformer branch.

Verification task: inspect preprocess.get_feature_names_out() on the fitted step, compare the transformed shapes for Training and Validation, and confirm that P10's new Emergency category does not change column count. Also verify the input order and ensure IDs or future Labels never enter the model Features.

Audit the transformed matrix: inspect actual column names and values

The fully runnable code prints seven Feature names. For P11, the transformed row is approximately [1.3098, 1.0310, 1.3517, −0.1170, 2.6458, 1, 0], in the exact order reported by get_feature_names_out(). The number 2.6458 is not a creatinine measurement; it is the original missingness flag (1) after StandardScaler. Exactly one of eight Training patients has missing creatinine, so the indicator mean is 0.125 and its population SD is \(\sqrt{0.125(1-0.125)}pprox0.33072\). The standardized value is therefore \((1-0.125)/0.33072pprox2.6458\).

For P10, the unseen Emergency service becomes [0,0], while the transformed matrix keeps exactly seven columns. The appended assert statements check the schema and numerical behavior rather than asking the learner to trust a prose claim. To retain an interpretable 0/1 missingness indicator, one can route it through a separate, unscaled branch—but that constitutes a different candidate Pipeline that must be validated.

15. Evaluate the complete preprocessing choice—not a single impressive metric

Preprocessing is part of the hypothesis tested by a Machine Learning experiment. Median versus mean imputation, an additional missingness indicator, two alternative encoders, or different scaling methods can lead to different generalization patterns. The valid unit of comparison is the complete Pipeline trained and evaluated under the same splitting protocol, not a preprocessing statistic examined independently of downstream consequences.

Use comparisons that answer a real question

Imagine three candidate pipelines: A uses numeric median imputation and One-Hot Encoding; B adds a missingness indicator; C uses a more elaborate multivariate Imputer. We would fit each on the designated Training portion and compare them with an appropriate Validation strategy. If a model predicts readmission, we might consider Recall, Precision, calibration, workload, subgroup performance and decision consequences, not Accuracy alone. In this 12-row teaching set, such comparisons would have extremely high sampling uncertainty and should not be presented as empirical evidence for clinical deployment.

Document the feature schema and transformation lineage

A reproducible training artifact should store column names and types, unit definitions, patient/discharge eligibility rules, feature windows and snapshots, category-vocabulary policy, missing-value rules, fitted medians, scaler moments, source versions and the approved model configuration. A model trained on creatinine in mg/dL may receive clinically different values if production sends μmol/L without conversion. The code might still execute, making the failure more dangerous than an obvious error.

Validation also concerns people and deployment

Lab measurements are shaped by medical ordering practices; lengths of stay reflect access and institutional policy. A feature could improve the average score while producing inequitable errors in patients whose care differs systematically. The proper response is to investigate source processes, assess meaningful subgroups with sufficient data, respect privacy, and involve healthcare professionals. One cannot infer fairness, transportability or clinical usefulness from our synthetic calculations.

A credible deliverable: a signed-off feature dictionary, fitted Pipeline, documented split protocol, unit tests, independent metrics with uncertainty, and a statement of the intended and prohibited uses. This is much stronger than reporting “all missing values were filled and accuracy increased.”

16. Common mistakes: identify the mechanism and the remedy

1. Imputing every blank as zero

Zero is a meaningful measured value for some Features; a blank often means no reliable measurement. Record missingness and apply a justified policy.

P5 is the teaching counterexample: its creatinine is absent, whereas an age or visit count of zero can be meaningful under some definitions.

2. Calculating the median using all rows

Our valid Training median is 1.3 mg/dL; 1.25 from all observed rows leaks held-out information.

P11 must receive 1.3 from January even though the full-table median is 1.25; which rows supplied the parameter matters more than the difference between numbers.

3. Assuming missingness must be random

MCAR/MAR/MNAR describe different mechanisms and assumptions; two missing cells cannot identify the mechanism.

Short stays might receive fewer laboratory orders, yet our tiny table cannot demonstrate MAR. Mechanism assumptions demand substantive justification.

4. Using numeric labels for unordered services

Medicine=0 and Surgery=1 may suggest a nonexistent numeric ordering to some algorithms; One-Hot Encoding avoids that interpretation.

A linear score could treat 2−1 and 1−0 as comparable distances despite the labels being nominal. Fit dummy columns from Training instead.

5. Refitting the encoder when Emergency appears

The vocabulary must remain the one learned from Training; unknown categories need a documented transform-time policy.

P10 Emergency is not a reason to add a fresh column in Validation: the trained model would then receive an unexpected input dimension.

6. Assuming standardization makes data normally distributed

Standardization rescales mean and spread; it does not turn an arbitrary skewed distribution into a Gaussian.

Age z-scores preserve the skew and rank order of raw Age under an affine transformation; they do not reshape it into a bell curve.

7. Recomputing scalers for each cohort

P9 and P11 must use January’s age mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum.

Standardizing P9 with a February mean would put it in a different coordinate system from the eight training patients.

8. Clipping every outlier automatically

A clinically valid extreme can matter; investigate provenance and the intended population before altering it.

A nine-day stay is high but possible. A ninety-day stay could also be possible in another population; audit each with clinical provenance.

9. Building post-discharge Features

A follow-up event in the next 30 days cannot predict an outcome from the discharge snapshot.

The May 4 event in our timeline cannot count as an encounter already completed by April 30.

10. Selecting Features before Cross-Validation

Outcome-guided selection outside the folds lets held-out labels influence the Feature subset.

For example, selecting the variable with the strongest correlation using all twelve Target labels contaminates the independent Test rows.

11. Treating identifiers as predictors

IDs can memorize hospitals or patients and create leakage and privacy problems rather than generalizable signal.

Patient IDs should be retained for joins and audit trails, not silently entered as model features.

12. Forgetting units and denominators

A concentration in mg/dL differs from μmol/L, and visits per 30 days differ from raw counts over unequal exposure.

A value of creatinine expressed in a different unit can be numerically far larger even when it describes the same blood sample.

13. Adding interactions without interpreting them

With an interaction, the effect of a Feature depends on another Feature’s value, and the extra complexity may overfit.

With the illustrative score, the increase associated with one extra visit is 0.45 at a five-day stay, but 0.55 at seven days.

14. Believing a Pipeline guarantees validity

Pipelines help prevent statistical preprocessing leakage; they do not repair future-derived Features, mislabeled outcomes or unfair sampling.

A correctly implemented Pipeline can still contain a post-discharge variable illegally constructed upstream.

15. Treating a synthetic metric as clinical evidence

Our twelve rows are invented and too small for stable inference; a real model needs independent clinical validation and governance.

Predicting invented readmissions is not the same as demonstrating safer discharge planning with actual patient outcomes.

These errors are interconnected. For example, forgetting source units can create apparent outliers, which a careless trimming rule may delete; the remaining dataset is then biased and can make the subsequent model look better than it is. The most defensible workflow begins with definitions, provenance and timing—not with a universal cleanup function.

17. Knowledge checks: calculate, interpret and explain each answer

Try each question independently before opening the solution. Every numerical answer refers to the same complete synthetic dataset, unless the question explicitly introduces a separate hypothetical scenario.

1. Why is P5 creatinine considered missing rather than zero?

The original record does not contain a measured lab value. Zero would assert a physical measurement that was never observed. Treat it as missing and document the operational reason if known.

In this hospital example, turning a blank into zero would also distort summary statistics and scaling parameters. Keeping an explicit indicator lets later analysis distinguish imputed observations from measured ones.

2. Calculate the Training creatinine missingness percentage.

One of P1–P8 has missing creatinine, so 1/8=12.5%. The complete dataset has 2/12=16.67%, but that full-table percentage is not the statistic used to fit an imputer.

The different denominators matter: the overall rate describes the full teaching table, whereas the Training rate describes the data from which preprocessing parameters may legitimately be learned. Neither rate establishes a hospital-wide missingness mechanism.

3. Derive the Training median creatinine.

The seven observed Training results sorted are 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.3, 1.6, 1.8, 2.1. The fourth is 1.3 mg/dL.

The median is resistant to changing the largest observed value without crossing the center of the ordered sample. It is nevertheless an estimated statistic, not a substitute for a clinically measured result.

4. Compute the mean of those seven measured values.

Their sum is 9.5 mg/dL; dividing by 7 produces 1.357142857 mg/dL, or about 1.3571 mg/dL.

The mean exceeds the median partly because the upper values pull it upward. Both summaries describe the measured Training observations; neither estimates the actual missing result without additional assumptions.

5. What value imputes P11 creatinine and why?

1.3 mg/dL because the imputer was fitted on January Training. P11 is a held-out Test case, so its own cohort must not refit the median.

The imputing procedure should also keep an indicator of absence and the same feature names and units. Recomputing the median on March could change the representation just before model evaluation.

6. Why does an all-data median of 1.25 create leakage?

The all-data median includes observed creatinine from Validation and Test; a fitted training-time preprocessing parameter would depend on future evaluation cases.

The two medians can produce identical predictions in some models, but identical predictions do not vindicate the information flow. The evaluation protocol is invalidated by the forbidden fitting step, regardless of the size of its numerical impact.

7. One-Hot Encode Medicine and Surgery, then explain P10.

With columns [Medicine,Surgery], Medicine maps to [1,0] and Surgery to [0,1]. P10 Emergency is unknown to the Training vocabulary; ignore-unknown handling maps it to [0,0], not a learned explicit Emergency column.

The all-zero representation is not a claim that the Emergency service has no clinical significance. It signals that the encoder has no fitted indicator for this unseen category, motivating an explicit unknown-category policy.

8. Compute P9 age as a Training Min–Max value.

Training minimum 39 and maximum 81 yield (60−39)/(81−39)=21/42=0.50.

Interpret 0.50 as the midpoint of the Training age range, not the midpoint of medical risk. Min–Max Scaling does not estimate a probability and cannot make an old or young patient safer.

9. Compute P9 standardized age and interpret it.

Training mean 58.75 and population-style standard deviation about 13.1696 yield (60−58.75)/13.1696≈0.0949. It is 0.095 Training SD above mean, not a 9.5% risk increase.

The SD convention in StandardScaler uses the Training population-style divisor n, not the sample descriptive divisor n−1. Changing that convention changes numerical outputs without creating new predictive information.

10. A future patient is 90 years old. Is a Min–Max result >1 necessarily an error?

No. (90−39)/42=51/42≈1.214. The fitted Training range is [39,81], and future ages can be outside it. Clipping is an explicit optional decision with information loss.

If the production workflow requires strict [0,1] inputs, clipping or validation rules must be chosen and tested explicitly. Silently refitting the bounds with that future patient would create inconsistent training and inference transformations.

11. Calculate the original Training mean length of stay.

The eight stays sum to 2+5+7+1+4+9+3+6=37, so mean=37/8=4.625 days.

This is a summary of eight synthetic Training stays. It is not a population estimate for real hospital length of stay, and it would be sensitive to even a single corrupted extreme entry.

12. How does replacing 9 by 90 change the mean stay?

The new sum is 37−9+90=118, so the mean is 118/8=14.75 days. Investigate the outlier instead of automatically deleting it.

A screening rule may flag 90 as extreme, but only source review can establish whether it is erroneous. The median remaining at 4.5 in both sets provides a second robust diagnostic, not a definitive correction.

13. Construct an interaction for P2 and P3.

Visits×stay gives P2: 3×5=15 visit-days, and P3: 4×7=28 visit-days. The interaction units differ from either original Feature.

If an estimated model assigns a coefficient to visits×stay, that coefficient refers to one visit-day of the interaction. It does not independently mean that length of stay or earlier visits cause readmission.

14. Calculate the April 30 preceding-90-day visit Feature.

For visits on Feb 10, Mar 15, Apr 12 and future May 4, only the first three qualify as prior known encounters; count=3.

A scheduled May visit is future information even if its appointment was visible in April; whether to include scheduled appointments as a distinct feature requires a separate, time-appropriate definition. The example is counting actual past encounters.

15. What is the difference between a Missing Indicator and a Target?

A Missing Indicator records whether a Feature is absent, e.g., creatinine_missing=1 for P5. The Target is subsequent readmission, which must remain separate and future-known.

P5 has a missingness indicator of 1 even though the observed readmission Target is 0. They encode different events; using the future Target to compute the indicator would be leakage.

16. Why must Feature Selection happen inside a fold?

If target-guided Feature Selection uses all labels before Cross-Validation, the held-out fold influences which predictors are selected, biasing performance evaluation.

The Feature Selector itself learns from labels, so its choices must be recomputed for each Training fold. The evaluation fold should receive only the selected transformation, not participate in choosing it.

17. Does high Test Accuracy on twelve synthetic rows establish clinical utility?

No. The rows are invented, the sample tiny, and accuracy alone ignores prevalence, error costs, calibration, sampling, fairness and external validity.

Even independent accuracy on genuine data would not, by itself, show that a treatment decision is beneficial. Clinical effects, harm, calibration and patient outcomes require separate evidence.

18. What should be saved with a fitted Pipeline?

Feature definitions, source and availability times, units, allowed categories, learned imputation and scaling parameters, code versions, model configuration, evaluation protocol and audit trail.

A saved model file alone is insufficient if its inputs cannot be reconstructed with identical units, timestamps, transformations and software versions. Reproducibility is a property of the whole system.

21. Distinguish transformation consistency from a valid scientific conclusion.

S7 has a new carrier and missing temperature excursion. The fitted Pipeline returns a same-width row; S8 has a z-score of 3.0984. Has preprocessing succeeded? Does this demonstrate safety or predictive quality? Describe the next two checks.

Worked solution:

Software consistency: yes, the saved Training median, scaling constants and two carrier columns can transform both rows without refitting. Scientific validation: not yet. First audit whether excursions were actually available by the prediction timestamp, units and sensor quality; investigate S8 as a potential Distribution Shift and S7 as a changed carrier taxonomy. Then evaluate a separately trained model on genuinely held-out outcomes with confidence/uncertainty, costs and subgroup checks where justified. A same-width matrix proves schema compatibility—not prediction accuracy, calibration, fairness or clinical/logistics benefit.

18. Glossary, synthesis and technical reading

The central lesson is not “fill missing values, convert strings to numbers, and scale everything.” It is to make every transformation meaningful, fitted only on permitted information, reproducible and useful under independent evaluation. We have traced this principle from a raw hospital record to a structured input matrix and executable pipeline.

Data Preparation
Rules that make raw records valid, consistent and fit for an explicitly defined analysis.
Feature Engineering
Construction or transformation of predictor variables based on validated information.
Feature
An input variable available at the authorized prediction moment.
Target / Label
The outcome the model attempts to predict; here, future 30-day readmission.
Data Dictionary
Documentation of each field’s meaning, units, type, timing and valid values.
Missing Value
An absent or unavailable measurement, not necessarily a true zero.
MCAR
Missing Completely At Random: missingness independent of observed and unobserved variables under the model.
MAR
Missing At Random: missingness conditionally independent of the missing value given specified observed variables.
MNAR
Missing Not At Random: remaining dependence on unobserved values or information after conditioning.
Imputation
Replacement of missing entries according to a documented statistical or domain rule.
Missingness Indicator
Binary Feature recording whether an original measurement was absent.
One-Hot Encoding
Binary indicator representation of a categorical vocabulary learned from Training.
High Cardinality
A categorical Feature with many distinct possible values.
Standardization
Rescaling with a fitted mean and standard deviation.
Min–Max Scaling
Rescaling with fitted minimum and maximum values.
RobustScaler
A preprocessing method based on median and interquartile range.
Outlier
An unusual value relative to a defined reference; not automatically erroneous.
IQR
Interquartile Range: difference between the chosen upper and lower quartiles.
Interaction Feature
A constructed term that lets one Feature’s contribution depend on another.
Data Leakage
Improper use of future or evaluation information in development or prediction.
As-of Time
The moment at which a system must actually be able to compute its Features.
ColumnTransformer
scikit-learn utility to apply different preprocessing to specified columns.
Pipeline
An ordered, fitted sequence of preprocessing and estimation steps.
Cross-Fitting
Learning a target-dependent transform from folds other than the one being encoded.
Distribution Shift
A change in data patterns between development and future use.

A final connected walkthrough

For P11, the Test patient, we first confirm that Age=76, four prior visits, eight stay-days and Medicine were known at discharge; missing creatinine is marked missing, not zero. We then apply the January Training median 1.3 mg/dL and the fitted numeric scaler; Medicine is encoded with the January service vocabulary. The resulting row has the same Feature schema as Training. Only then can a model that was fitted and selected through authorized development steps produce a prediction. Whether that prediction is trustworthy requires held-out evaluation; the transformation itself cannot establish clinical benefit.
