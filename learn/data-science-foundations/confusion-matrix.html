Understanding the Confusion Matrix

True Positives, True Negatives, False Positives, and False Negatives — explained visually from first principles.

BeginnerBinary ClassificationModel EvaluationPrecision & Recall

Why do we need a Confusion Matrix?

Suppose we build a machine learning model that predicts whether an observation belongs to one of two classes. Examples include whether a customer will churn, whether a transaction is fraudulent, whether an email is spam, or whether a borrower will experience payment difficulties.

“How many predictions did the model get right?”

That question leads us to Accuracy, but Accuracy by itself does not tell us what kinds of mistakes the model made. A Confusion Matrix separates predictions into four possible outcomes so that we can understand those errors.

1. Start with Actual vs. Predicted

Actual classWhat actually happened in the observed data.
Predicted classWhat the fitted model predicted would happen.

For a binary problem, define the observed outcome as

\[ Y=\begin{cases}1,&\text{positive class},\\[4pt]0,&\text{negative class}.\end{cases} \]

\(Y\) represents the actual observed outcome.

The model produces a predicted class:

\[ \widehat{Y}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{positive prediction},\\[4pt]0,&\text{negative prediction}.\end{cases} \]

\(\widehat{Y}\), pronounced “Y-hat,” is the model prediction.

Important: “positive” does not mean “good,” and “negative” does not mean “bad.” Positive simply means the class designated as the event of interest, often coded as 1.

2. The four possible outcomes

For every observation, the model can be correct or incorrect, and it can predict positive or negative. That produces exactly four possibilities.

3. Understand each cell in plain language

True Positive (TP)

The model predicts positive, and the observation is actually positive. The model correctly detects the event of interest.

True Negative (TN)

The model predicts negative, and the observation is actually negative. The model correctly recognizes the absence of the positive event.

False Positive (FP)

The model predicts positive, but the observation is actually negative. This is often described as a false alarm.

False Negative (FN)

The model predicts negative, but the observation is actually positive. This is a missed positive case.

Read the names from right to left

A useful beginner trick is to interpret the second word first. The word Positive or Negative tells you what the model predicted. Then the word True or False tells you whether that prediction was correct.

Mini example: suppose “positive” means customer churn. If the model predicts that Maria will churn and Maria actually churns, that observation is a True Positive. If the model predicts that David will churn but David stays, that is a False Positive. If the model predicts that Ana will stay but Ana actually churns, that is a False Negative.

4. Build a Confusion Matrix from data

Suppose we evaluate a classifier on 100 observations. In reality, 20 are positive and 80 are negative.

\[20+80=100\]

Of the 20 actual positives, the model correctly identifies 15 and misses 5:

\[TP=15,\qquad FN=5\]

Consistency check: \(TP+FN=15+5=20\).

Of the 80 actual negatives, the model correctly identifies 70 and incorrectly classifies 10 as positive:

\[TN=70,\qquad FP=10\]

Consistency check: \(TN+FP=70+10=80\).

Two different directions through the matrix

The rows and columns of the Confusion Matrix answer different questions. This distinction is the key to understanding why Precision and Recall are different.

Start from predicted positivesThere are \(TP+FP=15+10=25\) observations that the model classified as positive. This is the denominator used by Precision.
Start from actual positivesThere are \(TP+FN=15+5=20\) observations that are actually positive. This is the denominator used by Recall.

Notice that the numerator is the same in both cases—\(TP=15\)—but the denominator changes because the question changes.

5. Derive the evaluation metrics

Instead of memorizing formulas, first ask what question each metric answers.

Accuracy

How often was the classifier correct overall?

\[\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}\]
\[\frac{15+70}{100}=\boxed{85\%}\]

Precision

Of everything predicted as positive, how many were actually positive?

\[\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}\]
\[\frac{15}{15+10}=\boxed{60\%}\]

Recall / Sensitivity

Of all actual positives, how many did the model find?

\[\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}\]
\[\frac{15}{15+5}=\boxed{75\%}\]

Specificity

Of all actual negatives, how many did the model correctly identify?

\[\mathrm{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}\]
\[\frac{70}{70+10}=\boxed{87.5\%}\]

F1 Score — balancing Precision and Recall

\[ F_1=2\frac{\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}} \]

With Precision \(=0.60\) and Recall \(=0.75\):

\[ F_1=2\frac{(0.60)(0.75)}{0.60+0.75}=\frac{0.90}{1.35}\approx0.667=\boxed{66.7\%} \]
MetricQuestion it answersResult

There is no single “Confusion Matrix metric.” Each metric answers a different question. Which metric matters most depends on the problem and on the consequences of different errors.

Precision and Recall side by side

This is one of the most important distinctions in classification:

Precision looks at predictions

The model predicted positive 25 times. Of those 25 positive predictions, 15 were correct.

\[\frac{15}{25}=0.60\]

Interpretation: when the model says “positive,” it is correct 60% of the time in this example.

Recall looks at reality

There were 20 observations that were actually positive. The model correctly found 15 of them.

\[\frac{15}{20}=0.75\]

Interpretation: the model detects 75% of the positive cases that actually exist.

Two additional rates you will see often

The same four cells also produce the False Positive Rate (FPR) and False Negative Rate (FNR).

False Positive Rate

\[\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}=\frac{10}{80}=12.5\%\]

Among actual negatives, 12.5% were incorrectly classified as positive. Notice that \(\mathrm{FPR}=1-\mathrm{Specificity}\).

False Negative Rate

\[\mathrm{FNR}=\frac{FN}{FN+TP}=\frac{5}{20}=25\%\]

Among actual positives, 25% were missed. Notice that \(\mathrm{FNR}=1-\mathrm{Recall}\).

6. Why Accuracy can be misleading

Suppose we have 1,000 observations: 950 are negative and only 50 are positive. Imagine a useless classifier that predicts negative for everyone.

OutcomeCount
TP0

\[\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{950}{1000}=\boxed{95\%}\]

But Recall is

\[\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{0}{0+50}=\boxed{0\%}\]
The lesson: 95% Accuracy does not necessarily mean that we have a useful classifier. With imbalanced classes, a model can achieve high Accuracy simply by predicting the majority class.

7. False Positive vs. False Negative: which is worse?

There is no universal answer. The importance of each error depends on the application and on how the prediction will be used.

ApplicationPositive classExample error
Fraud detectionFraudA False Negative means fraud was present, but the model failed to detect it.
Spam filteringSpamA False Positive can send a legitimate email to the spam folder.
Customer churnChurnA False Negative means the model predicted that a customer would stay when the customer actually churned.
Credit risk modelingDefined risk eventFalse Positives and False Negatives have different consequences depending on the decision process.
The Confusion Matrix is mathematical; the cost of each error depends on context. The matrix tells us what happened. Domain knowledge tells us how much each type of error matters.

8. Where does the Confusion Matrix come from?

Many classifiers first produce an estimated probability for the positive class:

\[ \widehat{p}(\mathbf{x})=\widehat{P}\!\left(Y=1\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\right) \]

Suppose the model produces \(\widehat{p}(\mathbf{x})=0.72\). To convert that probability into a class, choose a threshold \(t\):

\[ \widehat{Y} = \begin{cases} 1, & \widehat{p}(\mathbf{x}) \ge t,\\[6pt] 0, & \widehat{p}(\mathbf{x}) < t. \end{cases} \]

If \(t=0.50\), then \(0.72\ge0.50\), so the observation is classified as positive.

Change the threshold → change the Confusion Matrix

Lowering the threshold generally causes more observations to be classified as positive. This often increases Recall because more actual positives are detected, but it can also create more False Positives and reduce Precision. Raising the threshold generally makes the classifier more selective.

Important connection: TP, TN, FP, and FN are not necessarily fixed properties of a probabilistic model. They depend on the classification threshold used to convert probabilities into class predictions.

A numerical threshold example

Suppose three observations receive the following predicted probabilities:

ObservationPredicted probabilityPrediction if \(t=0.50\)Prediction if \(t=0.30\)
A0.72PositivePositive
B0.44NegativePositive
C0.18NegativeNegative

Observation B did not change its model score: it remained 0.44. What changed was the decision rule. With a threshold of 0.50, 0.44 is classified as negative; with a threshold of 0.30, the same observation is classified as positive.

Therefore, changing the threshold can change TP, FP, TN, and FN and, as a result, change Precision, Recall, Specificity, and other threshold-dependent metrics.

Thresholds create trade-offs

Threshold changeTypical effectWhy
Lower the thresholdMore predicted positivesThe model requires a lower probability to assign the positive class.
Lower the thresholdRecall usually increasesMore actual positives can be captured.
Lower the thresholdFalse Positives may increaseMore actual negatives can also be labeled as positive.
Raise the thresholdFewer predicted positivesThe model becomes more selective before assigning the positive class.
Important nuance: these are typical directional effects, not a guarantee that every metric will change by the same amount. The exact result depends on the distribution of model scores and on the data.

9. Common mistakes to avoid

  1. Thinking that “positive” means good. Positive simply identifies the class of interest.
  2. Confusing FP and FN. The second word tells you the predicted class; True or False tells you whether that prediction was correct.
  3. Looking only at Accuracy. Accuracy can hide poor performance on the minority class.
  4. Treating Precision and Recall as interchangeable. Precision starts from predicted positives; Recall starts from actual positives.
  5. Forgetting the threshold. Changing the threshold can change every cell of the Confusion Matrix.

What should you learn next?

The Confusion Matrix is a fundamental foundation. Once these four cells feel intuitive, the natural next topics are Precision versus Recall, class imbalance, classification thresholds, ROC curves, AUC, and probability calibration.

Final conclusion

The Confusion Matrix is more than a \(2\times2\) table. It is the foundation for understanding how a classification model succeeds and how it fails.

Instead of asking only “How accurate is my model?”, ask:

What did it get right? What did it get wrong? Which errors matter? And how do those errors change when the decision threshold changes?

Once TP, TN, FP, and FN are understood, Precision, Recall, Specificity, and F1 stop being formulas to memorize. They become natural answers to different questions about model behavior.
