01. The question that time dependence answers

A meter reading today may resemble the reading an hour earlier, even if both readings have the same average level across a year. That resemblance is neither an ordinary cross-sectional correlation nor necessarily an effect of one hour causing the next. Autocorrelation asks how values of a single series are associated across time separations. If past observations contain information about later observations, a forecasting procedure should at least examine that dependence before choosing a model.

This guide studies three related but different objects: autocovariance measures lagged co-movement in squared measurement units; autocorrelation function (ACF) rescales it to a dimensionless number; and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) isolates a lag after accounting for intervening lags in a linear projection. They are descriptive and diagnostic tools. A large spike is not automatically a causal relationship or an approved forecasting model.

Our recurring example is a deliberately synthetic, fully disclosed sequence of twelve equally spaced hourly deviations of electricity demand. We use a small dataset so every product in an autocovariance sum can be reconstructed, not because twelve points suffice for reliable operational inference. Later examples distinguish theoretical processes from the quirks of a single observed path. Readers should already understand a sample mean and variance; we reconstruct covariance, conditioning, and hypothesis testing when needed.

02. Learning objectives, prerequisites and boundary

After studying both the conceptual explanations and complete solutions, you should be able to: calculate the first two sample autocovariances under a stated convention; derive an ACF and a second-order PACF; distinguish theoretical population quantities from sample estimates; assess a white-noise reference band; compute a Ljung–Box statistic; and communicate what these diagnostics do not establish. The most demanding step is understanding why a second-lag ACF can remain large while its PACF is small, or the reverse.

Essential bridges from earlier guides are covariance and correlation (introductory moments), tests with a null hypothesis, regression adjustment, and stationarity. We restate each locally. Algebra and simple summation notation are sufficient for the worked calculations; matrix algebra is explained only where it clarifies higher-order PACF. No programming is required to follow the reasoning.

The guide intentionally stops short of fitting and validating a complete ARIMA forecasting system. An ACF/PACF pattern can suggest plausible candidate autoregressive or moving-average orders, but model identification requires stationarity checks, residual assessment, temporal validation and sensitivity to finite samples. The next guide moves from dependence diagnostics to forecast baselines and evaluation. Keep three separate questions in mind: What dependence is observed? What generating mechanism is plausible? What future performance can be demonstrated? The answer to one is not necessarily the answer to all three.

03. The observed sequence and its measurement contract

Suppose an electricity analyst records deviations from a reference demand of 100 MW, once at the end of each hour. The synthetic deviations are shown in the table. Actual illustrative demand is reference plus deviation: a deviation of 5 MW means 105 MW, and 8 MW means 108 MW. The units are MW, a power measurement, not MWh of energy consumed in an interval. We do not silently interchange those units.

There are twelve equally spaced timestamps, no missing rows, no duplicated hours, and one consistently defined measure. The sequence was deliberately constructed to demonstrate positive first-lag association and a negative second-lag association; it is not a random extract from an actual power system and is not evidence for a specific grid. At time 12, 4 MW is simply the observed deviation. If the analyst were issuing a forecast at time 9, values for times 10–12 would not yet be available and must not enter the forecasting procedure.

We call the deviation at time \(t\) by \(X_t\), where \(t=1,\ldots,12\). The raw demand is \(100+X_t\) MW. Adding the same constant to all observations does not change covariance, ACF, or PACF, since centering subtracts the mean. By contrast, replacing MW by kW multiplies covariance by one million but leaves correlations unchanged. These invariances are useful checks against arithmetic and unit errors.

04. Why a lag is a time interval, not merely a row

Lag \(k\) means a separation of \(k\) observation intervals. In an hourly series, lag 1 is one hour, lag 2 is two hours, and lag 24 is one day; in a monthly series, lag 12 is a year. The lag is not a universal unit. The expression \(X_t\) paired with \(X_{t-k}\) is meaningful only when the timestamps represent a consistent temporal separation.

Our first-lag pairs are \((X_2,X_1)\) through \((X_{12},X_{11})\): eleven pairs. Our second-lag pairs are \((X_3,X_1)\) through \((X_{12},X_{10})\): ten pairs. Observe that the number of paired terms changes with \(k\). In an irregularly sampled dataset, blindly shifting the spreadsheet by one row might pair one-hour differences, three-hour differences, and a weekend gap. A numerical function can produce a coefficient, but that coefficient will not represent a well-defined one-hour lag.

Do not confuse contemporaneous correlation between demand and temperature, which involves two variables measured at a common time, with autocorrelation of demand against its own past. Cross-correlation is a separate diagnostic with additional alignment and causal-interpretation complications. When we discuss a lag, always attach its physical interval, check whether timestamps were sorted, and ask what information was available at the intended decision time.

05. Population autocovariance begins with stationarity

For a stochastic process \(\{X_t\}\), covariance between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-k}\) is \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-k})=E[(X_t-\mu_t)(X_{t-k}-\mu_{t-k})]\). Here \(E\) means the probability-weighted expectation across possible realizations, not the average of a single observed table. If the process is weakly stationary, its mean \(\mu\) and finite variance are constant over time and its covariance depends on separation \(k\), rather than on the specific calendar hour. We can then write a single function \(\gamma(k)\).

The units of \(\gamma(k)\) are MW squared. Positive \(\gamma(k)\) means above-average values \(k\) hours apart tend to occur together in a linear covariance sense; negative covariance means an above-average value tends to pair with a below-average one. A zero value means no linear lag association, not necessarily statistical independence. It is a population statement involving repeated realizations of the process under its assumptions.

The identities \(\gamma(0)=\operatorname{Var}(X_t)\ge 0\) and \(\gamma(-k)=\gamma(k)\) follow directly: zero separation is variance, and swapping covariance arguments does not change covariance. A process with a changing mean or variance may still have calculable sample correlations, but an ordinary stationary-process ACF interpretation can be misleading. Thus inspection of trend, seasonal change and structural breaks precedes interpretation of spikes.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\gamma(k)=\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-k})=E[(X_t-\mu)(X_{t-k}-\mu)]\]

06. Population autocorrelation rescales the units

The autocorrelation function is \(\rho(k)=\gamma(k)/\gamma(0)\) when \(\gamma(0)>0\). Dividing by variance removes squared units. At lag zero, \(\rho(0)=1\); by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \(-1\le\rho(k)\le1\). This is a correlation between measurements of the same process separated in time, not between arbitrarily chosen columns of a dataset.

The ratio tells us the direction and strength of linear association at each lag. For example, \(\rho(1)=0.6\) would mean a positive first-hour association at the process level, not that demand grows 60%, not a 60% probability of another high reading, and not that 60% of future observations can be forecast correctly. Negative values describe a tendency to alternate above and below the mean. If variance is zero, autocorrelation is undefined: a constant sequence has no standardized variability to relate.

A useful theoretical check is a stationary AR(1) process \(X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) with \(|\phi|<1\), zero-mean uncorrelated innovations and finite innovation variance. Multiplying the model by a past centered value and taking expectations gives \(\gamma(k)=\phi\gamma(k-1)\) for positive \(k\). Thus \(\rho(k)=\phi^k\). We derive the resulting PACF later. This identity is a result under specified model assumptions, not a claim that our illustrative twelve-point table follows an AR(1) process.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\rho(k)=\frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)},\qquad-1\le\rho(k)\le1\]

07. Sample conventions: state the denominator

Given \(n\) recorded values, compute the sample mean \(\bar x=\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_t/n\) and centered deviations \(d_t=x_t-\bar x\). One common biased sample autocovariance convention is \(\widehat\gamma_k=(1/n)\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}d_td_{t-k}\) for \(k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\). Despite the name, this convention is standard in ACF implementations because it yields a coherent normalization and useful algebraic properties. The adjective biased is about finite-sample expectation, not a numerical mistake.

Other books divide the lag-\(k\) numerator by \(n-k\), sometimes calling the result an adjusted or unbiased-denominator estimate. These are different estimators and can yield noticeably different high-lag values. The \(n-k\) divisor does not magically make every statistic unbiased when the mean is estimated. A student's first responsibility is to say which convention is used, rather than quote a number without a definition.

For all calculations and ACF graphics here we use the common \(1/n\) autocovariance convention and define \(\widehat\rho_k=\widehat\gamma_k/\widehat\gamma_0\). The factors \(1/n\) cancel, so the sample ACF is simply the lag-\(k\) sum of centered cross-products divided by the total centered sum of squares. At larger lags there are fewer pairs and much greater uncertainty. A visually striking late spike based on only two or three pairs deserves especially careful scrutiny.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat\gamma_k=\frac1n\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)(x_{t-k}-\bar x)\]

08. Work the observed mean and variance first

The complete synthetic sequence is \(5,6,8,7,5,4,6,7,8,6,6,4\) MW. Its sum is 72 MW and its mean is \(\bar x=72/12=6\) MW. Subtracting 6 from each observation gives centered deviations \((-1,0,2,1,-1,-2,0,1,2,0,0,-2)\) MW. Square each deviation and add: \(1+0+4+1+1+4+0+1+4+0+0+4=20\) MW².

Under our declared autocovariance convention, lag-zero autocovariance is \(\widehat\gamma_0=20/12=1.666667\) MW². Notice this is not the conventional unbiased sample variance \(20/(12-1)=1.818182\) MW². Both calculations can be correct for their distinct purposes. We use the \(n\) denominator because it corresponds to our chosen sample ACF definition and makes its ratio transparent.

Three checks prevent silent error: the centered deviations sum to zero; the sum of squared deviations is positive; and \(\widehat\rho_0=\widehat\gamma_0/\widehat\gamma_0=1\). If a centered sum is not zero, either the mean was miscomputed or a row was omitted. The table includes each of the twelve observations and centered deviations so that a student can reproduce these checks with paper and pencil.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\bar x=\frac{72}{12}=6,\qquad\widehat\gamma_0=\frac{20}{12}=1.6667\ \mathrm{MW}^2\]

09. Compute the first-lag autocovariance by hand

To find lag 1, multiply each centered deviation by the deviation one hour earlier. Using our disclosed vector, the eleven products \(d_2d_1,\ldots,d_{12}d_{11}\) are \(0,0,2,-1,2,0,0,2,0,0,0\). Their sum is \(5\) MW². The calculation is not the covariance of two independently sampled variables: it is built from overlapping lagged versions of the same sequence.

The lag-one sample autocovariance is \(\widehat\gamma_1=5/12=0.416667\) MW²; the standardized sample correlation is \(\widehat\rho_1=(5/12)/(20/12)=5/20=0.25\). Interpreted descriptively, a one-hour separation in this small constructed sample shows modest positive linear association. It does not guarantee \(\rho(1)=0.25\) in any population, and it does not mean the next reading will be 25% larger.

Why did we divide by 20 rather than by 11 pairs? Because the ACF uses the sample autocovariance convention already chosen. A common error is to divide the cross-product sum by 11 while keeping lag-zero variance divided by 12, without acknowledging the changed convention. That mixed formula would answer a different numerical question. A clear calculation should display its numerator, denominator, units and estimator definition before making an interpretation.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat\rho_1=\frac{\widehat\gamma_1}{\widehat\gamma_0}=\frac{5/12}{20/12}=0.25\]

10. Repeat the calculation at lag two

At lag 2, pair time 3 with time 1, time 4 with time 2, and continue through time 12 with time 10. There are ten centered cross-products: \(-2,0,-2,-2,0,-2,0,0,0,0\) MW². Their sum is \(-8\) MW². Consequently \(\widehat\gamma_2=-8/12=-0.666667\) MW² and \(\widehat\rho_2=-8/20=-0.40\).

The signs now contrast: lag 1 has a positive correlation, while lag 2 has a negative one. This could reflect alternation, a transient pattern, or simply construction and sampling variability. Without more observations we should not label it a reliable seasonal cycle. The sign also does not by itself tell us whether an autoregressive order of two is appropriate. The PACF calculation, developed later, clarifies why an ACF at lag 2 can include indirect association transmitted through lag 1.

For a checking exercise, verify lag 3 by forming nine products. Their sum is \(-14\) MW² and \(\widehat\rho_3=-14/20=-0.70\). That visually larger spike would exceed a common white-noise reference band for \(n=12\), but such a reference is approximate, pointwise, and weak in small samples. We will use it to practice careful language, not to assert discovery of a generating mechanism.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat\rho_2=\frac{-8/12}{20/12}=-0.40\]

11. Read an ACF plot without inventing a model

An ACF plot puts the lag \(k\) on the horizontal axis and the estimated autocorrelation \(\widehat\rho_k\) on the vertical axis. Each spike is a separate covariance-based calculation with fewer paired observations at higher \(k\). Lag zero is identically 1 by definition and is often omitted from the display to keep focus on time dependence. Our plot shows lags 1–5, with checked estimates \(0.25,-0.40,-0.70,-0.10,0.30\).

Positive spikes indicate same-direction lagged movement around the mean; negative spikes indicate opposite-direction movement. A gradually decaying sequence of spikes is a clue worth investigating, not proof of a unique model. Peaks at multiples of a known period could indicate seasonal dependence, but should be checked against calendar alignment and outliers. A sample with a trend can generate persistent autocorrelations even when the detrended process has much weaker dependence.

An ACF is calculated using observations available within the sample. It is not an out-of-sample forecasting score. It can also conceal nonlinear dependence because covariance tests only a linear association. Interpret a plot by naming the reference frequency, numerical lags, uncertainty, sample size, and alternative explanations. Avoid phrases such as 'the ACF proves the future depends on the past' or 'there is a clear AR(3)' based merely on the appearance of one small-sample chart.

12. Partial correlation asks an adjusted question

Suppose a three-hour pattern makes \(X_t\) associated with \(X_{t-2}\) because both are connected through \(X_{t-1}\). The ordinary ACF at lag 2 includes both indirect paths and any additional linear association. Partial autocorrelation at lag 2 asks a narrower question: after accounting for the one-hour intervening value, is there still linear association between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-2}\)? 'Accounting for' means linear projection, not necessarily physically holding temperature or operating conditions constant.

A useful mental model is regression. Fit one linear prediction for \(X_t\) using \(X_{t-1}\), another for \(X_{t-2}\) using the same intermediate \(X_{t-1}\), and correlate the two sets of prediction errors. That residual correlation expresses what remains after the common linear influence of the intermediate lag has been removed. With stationary autocorrelation functions and population projections, its value is the second-order PACF.

At lag 1 there is no intervening lag, so PACF(1) equals ACF(1). At lag 2 the two can differ. At lag 3, the procedure adjusts for lags 1 and 2; at lag \(k\), for all intervening lags 1 through \(k-1\). This definition is not the same as taking the original ACF and subtracting the previous spike. Nor does it mean removing every nonlinear or unobserved influence. Its 'partial' qualification is specifically about linear adjustment to intermediate values of the series.

13. Derive PACF at lag two algebraically

Write \(a=\rho(1)\) and \(b=\rho(2)\) for a stationary process with nonzero variance. Standardize \(X_t\) to mean zero and variance one. Regress the standardized current value on standardized lag 1, obtaining a linear-projection residual \(U=X_t-aX_{t-1}\). Similarly, regress standardized lag 2 on the same middle value, giving \(V=X_{t-2}-aX_{t-1}\). Direct covariance expansion produces \(\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)=b-a^2\), since the two cross-covariances involving the middle observation each equal \(a\), and its variance is one.

Both residual variances equal \(1-a^2\). Their standardized correlation is therefore \(\operatorname{Corr}(U,V)=(b-a^2)/(1-a^2)\). This is the population lag-two PACF when \(|a|<1\). We have derived the formula, not merely cited it. If \(|a|=1\), the middle value predicts the endpoints perfectly in the linear sense and the residual variance vanishes; ordinary partial correlation is undefined, so the denominator correctly warns us.

The crucial term \(a^2\) is the association at lag 2 that can be explained by two sequential first-lag links under this projection algebra. A lag-two ACF of \(0.36\) with lag-one ACF \(0.6\) gives PACF(2)\(=(0.36-0.36)/(1-0.36)=0\). A nonzero ACF is therefore entirely compatible with a zero second partial autocorrelation. That is why the two graphs can tell substantially different stories.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\phi_{22}=\frac{\rho_2-\rho_1^2}{1-\rho_1^2}\]

14. Calculate the second partial correlation for our table

We have already checked \(\widehat\rho_1=0.25\) and \(\widehat\rho_2=-0.40\) for the synthetic twelve-hour series. Insert these numbers in the order-two Yule–Walker/Levinson projection formula: numerator \(-0.40-(0.25)^2=-0.4625\); denominator \(1-(0.25)^2=0.9375\); quotient \(-0.4625/0.9375=-0.493333\ldots\). Thus the lag-two Yule–Walker PACF estimate is approximately −0.4933.

The difference between \(-0.40\) for the ACF and \(-0.4933\) for the PACF is substantive in meaning. The first number correlates observations separated by two hours without adjustment; the second represents a standardized direct linear association conditional on the intervening hour under the estimated Toeplitz covariance structure. The estimated first-lag pathway contributes \(0.25^2=0.0625\) to the ordinary two-lag correlation, so removing it makes the remaining association more negative.

An important computational detail: this plug-in calculation uses the stationary Yule–Walker autocorrelation convention. An ordinary least-squares regression run on the available aligned triplets \((X_t,X_{t-1},X_{t-2})\) may produce a somewhat different finite-sample PACF because it uses different effective rows and estimators. Neither method is 'the same number by definition' in a finite sample. To compare software results fairly, explicitly select the PACF algorithm and lag convention instead of treating implementation differences as mathematical contradictions.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat\phi_{22}=\frac{-0.40-(0.25)^2}{1-(0.25)^2}=\frac{-0.4625}{0.9375}=-0.493333\ldots\]

15. PACF of higher order: the recursive idea

For lag \(k>2\), the PACF can be obtained as the coefficient of the \(k\)th lag in the linear projection of \(X_t\) onto \(X_{t-1},\ldots,X_{t-k}\). The usual stationary covariance structure leads to the Yule–Walker equations, which form a Toeplitz system: each matrix entry depends on a lag difference rather than on a calendar position. Solving the system yields the coefficient associated with the furthest included lag, written \(\phi_{kk}\). That coefficient equals the population PACF at lag \(k\) under the relevant linear-projection setup.

An accessible way to interpret the recursion is to start with lag one, add lag two and ask whether it contributes beyond lag one, then add lag three and ask whether it contributes beyond lags one and two. Each stage changes the collection of variables being controlled. The numbers do not form a sequence of ordinary pairwise correlations; they are coefficients of successively larger conditional linear prediction problems.

A higher-order PACF estimate can be numerically unstable if few observations remain for a large lag. Different implementations use Yule–Walker, ordinary least squares, Burg estimation, or alternative bias adjustments; results can differ at the ends of short sequences. For reproducibility, report algorithm, sample, number of lags, detrending and missing-value handling. Our accompanying diagnostic plot uses the Yule–Walker approach consistently with the low-order calculation, but none of its bars should be read as a certified structural order.

16. White noise: a precise reference process

A weak white-noise process has constant mean (often zero), constant finite variance and zero autocovariance at all nonzero lags. In symbols, for \(k\ne0\), \(\gamma(k)=0\). It need not be normally distributed; Gaussian white noise is an additional special case. The name describes an absence of linear serial correlation, not a guarantee that every observation is independent or that every possible dependence measure vanishes.

If the innovations are independent and identically distributed (IID) with finite variance, then they are white noise, but the reverse implication need not hold. Independence implies zero covariance under finite moments; zero covariance is a weaker condition. Distinguishing these levels of assumption matters for forecasting uncertainty, conditional variance models and causal interpretation. A Ljung–Box test is fundamentally sensitive to serial autocorrelations, not to every form of nonlinear dependence.

White noise is often a residual diagnostic target after fitting a forecasting model. An acceptable model should not leave easily exploitable linear serial structure in its errors, because that suggests remaining predictability. Yet residual white-noise behavior alone does not prove parameter consistency, calibration, normality, independence from predictors or practical forecast accuracy. Conversely, a time plot that looks irregular to the eye may still contain serial dependence, especially at a seasonal lag that is not readily visible.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\gamma_W(k)=0\ (k\ne0)\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad W_t\ \text{independent over time}\]

17. A concrete counterexample: uncorrelated is not independent

Let \(Z_t\) be independent standard Normal variables and define \(W_t=Z_tZ_{t-1}\). Each \(W_t\) has mean zero and variance one. For nonzero lag 1, \(E(W_tW_{t-1})=E(Z_tZ_{t-1}^2Z_{t-2})=0\) because independent zero-mean factors appear; nonoverlapping lags also have zero covariance. Thus \(W_t\) is weak white noise in the covariance sense.

But \(W_t\) and \(W_{t-1}\) share the hidden factor \(Z_{t-1}\). Their squares are dependent. Calculate \(E(W_t^2W_{t-1}^2)=E(Z_t^2)E(Z_{t-1}^4)E(Z_{t-2}^2)=1\cdot3\cdot1=3\). Meanwhile \(E(W_t^2)E(W_{t-1}^2)=1\cdot1=1\). The covariance of their squares is therefore \(3-1=2>0\). This explicit calculation demonstrates dependence that an ordinary ACF cannot see, even though all nonzero-lag linear autocovariances are zero.

The construction relies on the known fourth moment of a standard Normal, \(E(Z^4)=3\), a cited standard result rather than an assumption that every distribution has this fourth moment. The key lesson is transferable: when analyzing financial volatility, intermittent demand or sensor extremes, examine residual squares and conditional variation when justified. Never write 'the Ljung–Box test found independence' merely because a p-value was large.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\operatorname{Cov}(W_t^2,W_{t-1}^2)=3-1=2>0\]

18. A white-noise reference band and its assumptions

Under a simple white-noise null, with appropriate regularity assumptions and sufficiently large \(n\), the standard deviation of a sample autocorrelation at a fixed nonzero lag is approximately \(1/\sqrt n\). The familiar pointwise 95% reference band is therefore \(\pm1.96/\sqrt n\). For our \(n=12\) observations the numeric bounds are \(\pm1.96/\sqrt{12}\approx\pm0.566\). These values describe an approximate repeated-sampling reference under a null, not the probability that a particular true autocorrelation lies inside an interval.

Our ACF spikes are 0.25 at lag 1, −0.40 at lag 2 and −0.70 at lag 3. The third spike lies outside the approximate band. That is a useful prompt to inspect this lag, but twelve observations are very few for the asymptotic approximation. The data were constructed rather than randomly sampled from an established stationary model, so any formal inference is mainly illustrative. Using the same band at high lags despite fewer observed pairs is another reason for care.

If we inspect 20 nominal 95% pointwise intervals, we should expect some false alarms under a null simply because we examine many lags. Simultaneous bounds would require a different multiplicity-aware procedure. Moreover, confidence intervals for the ACF of a genuinely autocorrelated process may use Bartlett-type standard errors rather than white-noise bands. Always state whether the plotted limits are a white-noise reference, a lag-specific uncertainty interval, or a multiplicity-adjusted decision rule.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\text{White-noise reference:}\qquad\pm\frac{1.96}{\sqrt{n}}=\pm0.5658\quad(n=12)\]

19. Do not confuse PACF bands with ACF proof

A PACF plot typically adds visually similar approximate confidence bands. The common \(\pm1.96/\sqrt n\) heuristic is used under appropriate white-noise assumptions, but the sampling behavior of estimated partial autocorrelations depends on the method, lag, fitted model and effective sample. A package may draw reference bands that look identical to an ACF plot even though the underlying computation is fundamentally different.

Consider our second-lag PACF estimate of approximately −0.4933. It lies inside the crude ±0.566 boundary, whereas the ACF at lag 3 is −0.70 and lies outside. This does not authorize the statement that PACF is insignificant at every order, that lag 3 is causally important, or that we found a correct AR/MA order. It means that, under a rough small-sample reference, these individual diagnostics give different signals that deserve larger-sample checking and predictive validation.

Reliable interpretation also requires respecting preprocessing. PACF of a level with a deterministic trend is not the PACF of the detrended residual; PACF after differencing may describe a different stochastic process. Model residuals are another object altogether. A scientific report labels which series is plotted, its sampling frequency, transformations, missing-value handling and the maximum lag selected before seeing striking bars whenever possible.

20. Why a joint test complements individual bars

Looking at ten separate ACF spikes invites a multiple-testing problem. The Ljung–Box test combines sample autocorrelations up to a preselected maximum lag \(h\). Its null hypothesis is that the underlying autocorrelations through lags 1,…,\(h\) are zero under the conditions used by the test. The statistic is \(Q=n(n+2)\sum_{k=1}^{h}\widehat\rho_k^2/(n-k)\). Squaring means that positive and negative autocorrelations both contribute; the denominator compensates for smaller paired samples at larger lags.

The familiar large-sample approximation compares \(Q\) to a Chi-Square distribution, with degrees of freedom \(h\) for raw observations under the simple null. For fitted model residuals, software may use \(h-p-q\) or a user-specified model_df adjustment for estimated dynamic parameters, if appropriate. This adjustment is not universally equal to the number of every parameter in every model, and can be misleading when a procedure or test's assumptions are inappropriate.

A p-value is the probability, under the null and reference approximation, of a statistic at least as extreme as the observed value. A small p-value is evidence against joint zero-autocorrelation at the selected lags; a large one is not proof that all lags equal zero or that the observations are independent. Sensible lag choices, sample size, preprocessing and whether parameters were fitted are as important to report as the p-value itself.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[Q_h=n(n+2)\sum_{k=1}^{h}\frac{\widehat\rho_k^2}{n-k}\]

21. Calculate Ljung–Box with the first two lags

Using \(n=12\), \(\widehat\rho_1=0.25\), \(\widehat\rho_2=-0.40\) and \(h=2\), calculate \(Q=12(14)[0.25^2/(12-1)+(-0.40)^2/(12-2)]\). First, \(0.25^2=0.0625\) and \((-0.40)^2=0.16\). Next, \(0.0625/11\approx0.00568182\) and \(0.16/10=0.016\). Their sum is \(0.02168182\). Multiplication by 168 gives \(Q\approx3.642545\).

For a raw series and simple large-sample chi-square reference with 2 degrees of freedom, the tail probability is approximately 0.16182. At conventional \(\alpha=0.05\), we do not reject the joint zero-autocorrelation null through lag 2 under this approximate calculation. This is not proof that the process is white noise. The use of only 12 points makes the large-sample reference questionable, and the generating process has not been established as stationary.

Now choose \(h=3\) and add the squared third-lag correlation: \(168\times0.70^2/(12-3)=168\times0.49/9\approx9.146667\). Hence \(Q_3\approx3.642545+9.146667=12.789212\); the Chi-Square(3) reference tail is approximately 0.00512. The conclusion changes with the chosen lag maximum. This is precisely why the lag horizon should be motivated by the scientific question or selected in advance, not tuned until a preferred p-value appears.

\[Q_2=168\left(\frac{0.25^2}{11}+\frac{(-0.40)^2}{10}\right)=3.642545\]

22. Short samples, lag choice and power

Our twelve-hour teaching sequence is useful for arithmetic, not strong hypothesis testing. At lag 5 there are only seven paired centered products; at lag 10 there are merely two. The chi-square approximation used for Ljung–Box is an asymptotic result under modeling assumptions; it can behave poorly when \(h\) is a substantial fraction of \(n\). Choosing \(h\) too small can miss a seasonal or delayed association, whereas choosing it too large can dilute power or make the reference questionable.

The forecast horizon and domain should help determine lags. For hourly electricity demand, one-hour and 24-hour separations may be substantively important. Yet a 12-hour dataset cannot contain an empirical lag-24 pair: reporting a value would be fabrication. For monthly sales with several years of data, lag 12 may be meaningful. An analyst should state whether the lags were prespecified or selected after inspecting the chart, since post-hoc selection changes the interpretation of nominal p-values.

A test with a large p-value could reflect truly small autocorrelation, small sample size, competing patterns of opposite signs, or a poorly chosen lag set. Power is the probability of rejecting a false null under a specified alternative; it is not guaranteed by a formula for \(Q\). If a model-selection decision is consequential, obtain more observations, report effect sizes with uncertainty and validate prospective predictions. Technical significance without operational relevance is not enough.

23. Population AR(1): an ACF that tails off

Consider \(X_t=0.6X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) for a weakly stationary AR(1), with innovations uncorrelated with earlier values, constant variance and zero mean. The coefficient \(0.6\) is dimensionless. Under those assumptions \(\rho(1)=0.6\), \(\rho(2)=0.6^2=0.36\), \(\rho(3)=0.216\), and so on. The ACF decreases geometrically rather than becoming exactly zero after lag one.

But using our derived formula, PACF(2)\(=(0.36-0.6^2)/(1-0.6^2)=0\), and the higher-order population PACF values vanish as well. This is the sense in which the PACF of an ideal AR(1) cuts off after lag one, while its ACF tails off. 'Cuts off' describes a theoretical population shape, not a demand that every sample PACF bar after lag one is exactly zero. Finite data exhibit stochastic estimation error and may have nonzero spikes.

A process with coefficient \(-0.6\) would have an ACF alternating in sign: \(-0.6,+0.36,-0.216,\ldots\), while its PACF still truncates after lag one. This comparison shows why a negative first-lag spike alone does not necessarily mean seasonality. Before selecting an AR(1) model, assess stationarity, residual serial correlation and forecasting performance against simple baselines. The theoretical shape merely generates a candidate.

24. Population MA(1): the complementary pattern

Now consider the stationary moving-average process \(X_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\), where innovations are uncorrelated across time and share variance \(\sigma^2\). Expand the variance: \(\gamma(0)=\sigma^2(1+\theta^2)\). At lag one the only shared innovation is \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\), so \(\gamma(1)=\theta\sigma^2\). For all \(k\ge2\), no common innovation remains and \(\gamma(k)=0\). Thus \(\rho(1)=\theta/(1+\theta^2)\), and the population ACF cuts off after lag one.

For \(\theta=0.8\), calculate \(\rho(1)=0.8/1.64\approx0.487805\); lag 2 and higher ACF values equal zero. However, PACF(2)\(=(0-0.487805^2)/(1-0.487805^2)\approx-0.31226\), not zero. Its PACF generally tails off. This is the classic complementary shape to the AR(1) example and illustrates why the two graphs are read together rather than separately.

The mathematical existence of an MA(1) formula does not prove the fitted data follow one. Moreover, interpreting parameters often assumes invertibility, typically \(|\theta|<1\) for this convention, to ensure a unique stable innovation representation. Invertibility is separate from stationarity; a finite moving average of white noise is stationary under the stated conditions. The purpose here is to use simple theoretical models to build intuition, not to perform parameter fitting reserved for a later curriculum stage.

\[\rho_{\mathrm{AR(1)}}(k)=\phi^k,\qquad\operatorname{PACF}(k)=0\ (k>1)\]

25. Seasonal spikes, trend and differencing

An hourly series with daily repetition might have noticeable ACF at lags 24, 48 and 72, provided enough observations are available. A monthly series with annual seasonality may display peaks at lags 12, 24 and 36. Yet a sequence of spikes at regular multiples does not establish stable seasonality by itself: calendar changes, institutional schedules, a few extreme events or a changing mean can produce similar visual features.

If a series has deterministic trend, many adjacent points tend to be above or below the global mean together simply because the global level changes over time. Sample ACF values can stay high across many lags even if the short-term deviations behave differently. Detrending changes the object analyzed. Differencing, \(\Delta X_t=X_t-X_{t-1}\), can reduce a stochastic trend, but it also changes covariance structure and can introduce negative lag-one dependence if performed unnecessarily. Review the transformations in Guide 64 rather than applying differencing mechanically because an ACF 'looks persistent'.

A disciplined comparison plots the raw series, a justified transformed series, and their separate ACF/PACF estimates; it documents all transformations. For genuine forecasting, fit transformations using the training period only and avoid centering on statistics from future observations. ACF of a retrospectively transformed series can be useful diagnostically, but it does not automatically represent the information available at an earlier forecast origin.

26. Residual diagnostics are not raw-series diagnostics

Suppose a forecasting model predicts demand \(\widehat X_t\) using information available just before time \(t\). The residual is \(e_t=X_t-\widehat X_t\) in MW. An ACF calculated from \(X_t\) describes temporal structure in demand; an ACF calculated from \(e_t\) describes structure left unexplained by the fitted model. The two quantities can be very different even on the same timestamps. A model can account for a strong first-lag association yet leave a seasonal lag in its errors.

When using Ljung–Box on residuals, the reference distribution may require adjustment for dynamic parameters fitted from those same data. For an ARMA(\(p,q\)) model, a common adjustment is model_df=p+q, and the resulting degrees of freedom at lag \(h\) are \(h-p-q\) when positive, under appropriate assumptions. This is an illustration, not a rule for arbitrarily counting every fitted feature in every forecasting system. When \(h\le p+q\), the usual adjusted reference is unavailable or unhelpful.

Checking training residuals alone can miss overfitting. A model might whiten its in-sample residuals while producing poor future forecasts after a regime change. Always report in-sample residual diagnostics alongside rolling-origin or holdout forecast errors, errors by horizon and forecast uncertainty. White-noise residuals are evidence about remaining serial linear structure, not a complete deployment certificate.

27. Unit scaling and transformed autocorrelation

If power is converted from MW to kW, each observed value becomes \(1000X_t\). Deviations become 1000 times larger, and sample autocovariances become \(1000^2=1{,}000{,}000\) times larger. However \(\widehat\rho_k\) remains unchanged because both the numerator and lag-zero denominator are multiplied by the same factor. Additive changes likewise cancel after centering. Therefore the first-lag ACF is 0.25 whether we report deviations in MW or kW.

This invariance does not extend automatically to nonlinear transformations. Replacing \(X_t\) by \(X_t^2\), taking logarithms of positive demand, truncating extreme observations or differencing the series changes its statistical dependence structure. For example, values in MW relative to a 100 MW reference can be negative deviations even though actual demand is positive; a logarithm of negative deviations is undefined. To use log demand, we must transform the actual positive demand \(100+X_t\), not the deviations themselves.

A reproducible report says exactly which variable and scale the ACF describes. 'ACF of electricity' is incomplete if one analyst uses raw MW, another uses hourly energy totals in MWh, a third differences levels, and a fourth analyzes squared residuals. Numerical comparisons are only meaningful once sampling frequency, units, transformation and normalization are aligned.

\[e_t=X_t-\widehat X_t,\qquad \operatorname{ACF}(e_t)\ne\operatorname{ACF}(X_t)\ \text{in general}\]

28. Missing hours, duplicated timestamps and boundaries

Before calculating ACF on operational data, verify that timestamps correspond to a fixed interval, that daylight-saving changes are handled explicitly, and that duplicate or missing timestamps have not silently changed the meaning of a row shift. If the clock moves backward and an hour is repeated, two identical-looking local labels can refer to distinct instants. A time-zone-aware index and a clearly defined observation interval prevent some avoidable errors.

A missing hour cannot simply be deleted if the analyst then pretends the two surrounding observations are one hour apart. Deletion can convert a two-hour gap into a false lag-one pair. Interpolation may preserve an evenly spaced table but can introduce artificial serial dependence, especially when missingness is patterned. Every imputation should be documented, sensitivity-checked and restricted to information available at the intended analysis or forecast time.

The boundaries are equally important: at lag \(k\), there are only \(n-k\) within-sample pairs. There is no wraparound from the first observation to the last unless a circular process and circular estimator have been explicitly justified. Correlating time 1 with time 12 as 'lag 1' in our twelve-hour demonstration would be wrong. A student should be able to reconstruct the paired indices before calculating any products, especially for seasonal or irregular data.

29. Correlation does not make a causal arrow

Temporal precedence is necessary for many causal explanations, but it is not sufficient. If high electricity demand at time \(t-1\) correlates with demand at time \(t\), both could reflect temperature, occupancy, a production schedule or a common seasonal driver. ACF is symmetric in lag sign for a stationary process, \(\rho(-k)=\rho(k)\); that symmetry alone warns against reading its plot as a directed causal diagram.

PACF controls linear intermediate lags of the measured series, not every confounder. Its 'direct' association is direct only relative to that linear conditioning set. It does not generally estimate the effect of changing a past meter reading while holding external conditions constant. In most physical systems one cannot intervene on a historical observation itself. Forecast usefulness and causal identifiability are distinct objectives.

If a decision requires causal attribution, define the intervention, treatment assignment or structural model and evaluate confounding and assumptions. For forecasting, it may be enough to exploit stable predictive dependence and compare future errors, provided leakage is avoided. For scientific communication, replace 'lag 2 causes a decrease' with 'lag 2 is negatively correlated after linear adjustment for lag 1 under the chosen estimator'. Precise wording is part of statistical competence, not a merely stylistic preference.

30. A defensible reporting workflow

A reproducible dependence analysis follows an order designed to prevent silent changes after inspecting attractive spikes. First define the scientific question: short-term hour-to-hour predictability, a daily recurrence, or residual adequacy. Next declare the measurement unit, frequency, time zone, sample window, missingness rules and forecast origin. Examine the time plot and nonstationarity before calculating ACF/PACF. Document all transformations, their rationale, and whether they were estimated without looking ahead.

Then choose a reasonable maximum lag from the research question and available sample size. Show the ACF and PACF with numerical scales and explicitly labeled approximate bands if used. Reproduce at least one autocovariance and a low-order PACF by hand or an independent computation. If applying Ljung–Box, report whether observations are raw or fitted residuals, maximum lag, any model degrees-of-freedom adjustment, test statistic, reference distribution and p-value. Explain that an approximation is weakest in small samples such as ours.

Finally, separate descriptive evidence, structural hypotheses, and deployment decisions. ACF/PACF alone cannot prove the process model, independence, calibration or causality. Validate candidate models on future periods, review errors by horizon and repeat diagnostics after plausible preprocessing variations. The result should be a clear, bounded claim: which lags show what estimated linear association in which series, how uncertain those values are, and what additional evidence is needed before action.

31. Independent software checking without hiding the mathematics

Software can check our arithmetic but should not replace an explanation of estimators. For example, statsmodels.tsa.stattools.acf(x, nlags=5, adjusted=False, fft=False) uses the same standard \(1/n\) normalization as our worked ACF. Its output includes lag zero, which should be exactly one up to numerical precision. Calling a PACF routine requires a method argument to match our Yule–Walker choice; pacf_yw(x, nlags=5, method="mle") uses the lag-zero-normalized autocovariance rather than the alternative sample-size adjustment.

For Ljung–Box, statsmodels.stats.diagnostic.acorr_ljungbox(x, lags=[2,3], model_df=0, return_df=True) should reproduce the unadjusted illustrative statistics. Different defaults across package versions can change output and confidence intervals. Document software version, adjusted, nlags, method and model_df. Because students use Windows and Jupyter in other project contexts, the static guide does not assert execution on their machine.

The conceptual verification remains mathematical: \(\sum d_t=0\), \(\sum d_t^2=20\), lag cross-products are 5 and −8, second-order PACF equals −0.4625/0.9375, and Ljung–Box totals are known. A script that runs while displaying the wrong estimator convention is not a valid independent confirmation. Compare formula, intermediate sums and interpretation, rather than relying solely on a success message from a library.

32. Misconceptions and checks before the exercises

One frequent error is to treat autocorrelation as the correlation between two unrelated variables. Another is to infer independence from zero autocorrelation. A third is to confuse PACF with a simple difference between ACF bars. The correct remedies are to identify time alignment, inspect covariance definitions, and explicitly derive a partial correlation from residual projections. Repeating definitions without doing the arithmetic does not solve these conceptual obstacles.

A second family of errors concerns the inferential reference: using \(\pm1.96/\sqrt n\) as a universal confidence region, calling a pointwise band a simultaneous guarantee, or reporting a chi-square p-value from \(n=12\) as definitive. The appropriate response is to state null assumptions, sample size, lag set, asymptotic nature and the difference between failure to reject and proof of absence. For model residuals, report the appropriate degree-of-freedom adjustment rather than automatically using \(h\).

A final family concerns decision-making. Neither a visually beautiful ACF/PACF pattern nor a significant Ljung–Box test demonstrates useful forecast improvement or causality. An educationally sound explanation must connect the numbers to the task, compare alternative explanations and describe what remains unverified. The exercises deliberately cover hand computations, a theoretical AR/MA contrast, a nonlinear white-noise counterexample, an irregular-index problem, and a final transfer decision. Their solutions include intermediate operations and interpretations rather than answer-only shortcuts.

33. Exercise 1 — Rebuild lag-zero autocovariance

Problem. An auditor is given the twelve synthetic demand deviations but not the mean or variance. Reconstruct the zero-lag sample autocovariance and explain why it differs from the familiar sample variance.

Solution. Add all twelve values: \(5+6+8+7+5+4+6+7+8+6+6+4=72\) MW. Divide by twelve to obtain \(\bar x=6\) MW. Subtract 6 from each of the twelve entries, obtaining \((-1,0,2,1,-1,-2,0,1,2,0,0,-2)\). Square and add to obtain 20 MW². Because the guide defines lag-\(k\) autocovariance with divisor \(n\), calculate \(\widehat\gamma_0=20/12=1.666667\) MW². The usual unbiased sample variance uses \(n-1=11\) and is \(20/11\approx1.818182\) MW².

Interpretation and check. The two numbers answer different estimator questions and must not be mixed without justification. The centered deviations sum to zero. If the measured deviations were instead recorded in kW, \(\widehat\gamma_0\) would be 1,666,667 kW², preserving dimensional consistency. The exercise tests the choice of normalization, not just the ability to divide.

34. Exercise 2 — Verify lag-one products

Problem. A spreadsheet reports lag-one ACF equal to 0.25. Verify the number without accepting the spreadsheet as a source of authority, and state its units.

Solution. Take the centered deviations \(d_t\) from Exercise 1. Pair \(d_2\) with \(d_1\), \(d_3\) with \(d_2\), through \(d_{12}\) with \(d_{11}\). The products are \(0,0,2,-1,2,0,0,2,0,0,0\). Summing gives 5 MW². Dividing by twelve yields \(\widehat\gamma_1=5/12\approx0.416667\) MW². The lag-zero autocovariance is \(20/12\) MW², so \(\widehat\rho_1=(5/12)/(20/12)=0.25\). The ACF itself has no units.

Check and meaning. The coefficient is within [−1,1] and positive, as expected from the sum of cross-products. It describes modest linear association at a one-hour separation in an illustrative finite sample. The number is not a slope in MW per hour and cannot be converted to a 25% growth claim. Before making an operational forecast, one would still inspect stationarity, uncertainty and out-of-sample errors.

35. Exercise 3 — Calculate lag-two ACF and compare signs

Problem. The second-lag cross-product sum is not given. Compute it from the complete data and explain what a negative result does and does not mean.

Solution. There are ten legitimate pairs because \(12-2=10\). With the already centered deviations, list \(d_3d_1,\ldots,d_{12}d_{10}\): \(-2,0,-2,-2,0,-2,0,0,0,0\). Their total is −8 MW². With the \(1/n\) denominator, \(\widehat\gamma_2=-8/12=-0.666667\) MW². Standardize using \(\widehat\gamma_0=20/12\) to obtain \(\widehat\rho_2=-8/20=-0.40\). It is more negative than the lag-one coefficient is positive.

Check and meaning. The sign indicates opposite centered deviations tended to pair two hours apart under our linear estimator. It does not say that a high reading must be followed by a low reading after precisely two hours. The pairs overlap and are not independent experimental trials. The apparent sign change could be construction, a transient cycle, or sampling variation; it cannot establish a physical mechanism or an AR(2) model by itself.

36. Exercise 4 — Derive PACF(2) rather than guessing

Problem. Using the two checked ACF values, compute the lag-two PACF with the stated Yule–Walker convention. Explain why it differs from the lag-two ACF.

Solution. Let \(a=0.25\) and \(b=-0.40\). From the residual-projection derivation, \(\phi_{22}=(b-a^2)/(1-a^2)\). Square \(a\): \(0.25^2=0.0625\). Subtract in the numerator: \(-0.40-0.0625=-0.4625\). Compute denominator \(1-0.0625=0.9375\). Divide to obtain \(\phi_{22}=-0.4625/0.9375=-0.493333\ldots\). It is a dimensionless partial correlation.

Interpretation. The ordinary −0.40 correlation includes an estimated \(a^2=0.0625\) contribution involving two adjacent-lag links. After removing that linear component, the remaining association is more negative. This is not a calculation of causal influence, and another finite-sample PACF algorithm can yield a different estimate. State the estimator and assumptions before comparing to software or claiming any lag is significant.

37. Exercise 5 — ACF persists, PACF disappears

Problem. A hypothetical stationary process has \(\rho(1)=0.7\) and \(\rho(2)=0.49\). Compute PACF(2) and decide whether a nonzero ACF at lag two implies an additional direct linear association.

Solution. Square \(0.7\) to obtain \(0.49\). Substitute into \(\phi_{22}=[0.49-0.49]/[1-0.49]=0/0.51=0\). Thus its second-lag partial association vanishes even though its ordinary second-lag ACF is 0.49. The second-lag association can be completely explained by two first-lag links in this projection structure. This is consistent with a theoretical stationary AR(1) having coefficient 0.7.

Limit. Consistency does not mean the two ACF numbers identify a unique generating process; higher-order behavior, data quality and forecast validity remain unknown. A real sample gives estimated coefficients with uncertainty, not exact theoretical values. The example teaches why PACF adds information and why we must never derive model order solely from the existence of a positive second-lag ACF.

38. Exercise 6 — Rebuild a white-noise reference band

Problem. Compute the common 95% pointwise white-noise ACF band for \(n=12\) and for a larger sample of \(n=100\). Would the numerical threshold have the same interpretation in both samples?

Solution. Use \(\pm1.96/\sqrt n\). With twelve points \(\sqrt{12}\approx3.4641\), so the band is approximately \(\pm0.5658\). With one hundred, \(\sqrt{100}=10\) and the band is \(\pm0.196\). As sample size grows, the approximate null fluctuation around zero becomes smaller for a fixed lag under the reference assumptions. The lag-three sample ACF of −0.70 lies beyond the first band, but this is not a robust small-sample test.

Interpretation. The band is pointwise, not a guarantee for all inspected lags. It assumes an appropriate white-noise reference and sufficiently large sample for its derivation. If the underlying process has substantive autocorrelation, a different uncertainty formula may be appropriate. More observations improve potential precision but do not solve data leakage, structural breaks or model misspecification automatically.

39. Exercise 7 — Calculate Ljung–Box at lag two

Problem. Reproduce the exact arithmetic of the Ljung–Box statistic with twelve observations, maximum lag two and the checked sample ACF values.

Solution. Write \(Q=12(14)\left[0.25^2/11+(-0.40)^2/10\right]\). Compute \(0.25^2/11=0.0625/11=0.005681818\ldots\) and \(0.16/10=0.016\). Add to obtain \(0.021681818\ldots\). Multiply by 168: \(Q=3.642545\ldots\). Under the large-sample Chi-Square(2) reference the upper-tail probability is about 0.16182. With a conventional 5% rejection rule, this does not reject the joint null through lag two.

Interpretation. The result does not prove no autocorrelation and does not establish independence. Only two lags were tested and the reference is dubious with \(n=12\). The analyst must document the test horizon before exploring the data whenever possible. In the next exercise we will see that including lag three produces a very different result.

40. Exercise 8 — Change the test horizon responsibly

Problem. Extend Exercise 7 to maximum lag three using \(\widehat\rho_3=-0.70\), and explain why selecting this horizon after seeing a significant result creates a problem.

Solution. Its incremental term is \(12(14)\times(-0.70)^2/(12-3)=168\times0.49/9=9.146667\). Add the previous \(3.642545\) to obtain \(Q_3=12.789212\). The illustrative Chi-Square(3) upper-tail probability is approximately 0.005115. Numerically this would reject a simple joint zero-ACF null at 5%, unlike the lag-two version. Both computations use the same observations but different hypotheses about which lags are included.

Interpretation and caution. A researcher who tests every possible horizon and reports only the smallest p-value has performed unreported selection. The nominal 5% comparison is no longer a transparent single prespecified test. The sample is tiny and the model reference is approximate. The appropriate report gives both horizons, explains the substantive reason to inspect the third lag and requests longer independent records for a reliable operational conclusion.

41. Exercise 9 — AR versus MA theoretical patterns

Problem. Compare a stationary AR(1) with coefficient 0.6 and an MA(1) with coefficient 0.8. Report ACF at lags one and two and the second PACF in each case.

Solution. AR(1): \(\rho_1=0.6\), \(\rho_2=0.6^2=0.36\); \(\phi_{22}=(0.36-0.36)/(1-0.36)=0\). MA(1): \(\rho_1=0.8/(1+0.8^2)=0.8/1.64\approx0.487805\), \(\rho_2=0\); \(\phi_{22}=[0-(0.487805)^2]/[1-(0.487805)^2]\approx-0.31226\). Thus in the ideal AR(1) the PACF cuts off while ACF tails off, and in the ideal MA(1) the ACF cuts off while PACF need not.

Interpretation. These are population patterns derived from assumptions, not sample significance statements. A real sample may be consistent with several candidate models, or with neither. Choice requires diagnostics, information criteria where appropriate and rolling prediction comparisons. A student's answer should include those limitations rather than treating two spikes as a perfect model-selection oracle.

42. Exercise 10 — Detect dependence invisible to ACF

Problem. For IID standard Normal \(Z_t\), let \(W_t=Z_tZ_{t-1}\). Explain how lag-one covariance of \(W_t\) can be zero while the squared process has positive lag-one covariance.

Solution. Because the \(Z\) variables are independent with zero mean, \(E(W_tW_{t-1})=E(Z_tZ_{t-1}^2Z_{t-2})=E(Z_t)E(Z_{t-1}^2)E(Z_{t-2})=0\). Thus the ordinary lag-one covariance is zero. But \(W_t^2W_{t-1}^2=Z_t^2Z_{t-1}^4Z_{t-2}^2\), whose expectation is \(1\cdot3\cdot1=3\). Each \(W_t^2\) has mean one, so \(\operatorname{Cov}(W_t^2,W_{t-1}^2)=3-1=2>0\).

Interpretation. The series is uncorrelated in levels but dependent in squared values. The fourth moment 3 pertains to the Normal distribution under the stated hypothesis; it is not assigned to arbitrary data. Ljung–Box on levels cannot certify independence. A volatility investigation could examine squared errors or specialized conditional-variance diagnostics, but that is a distinct question requiring its own assumptions.

43. Exercise 11 — Correct a damaged time index

Problem. A meter file contains readings at 09:00, 10:00, 12:00 and 13:00, with no reading at 11:00. A colleague deletes the missing row and computes lag-one ACF by shifting one row. Identify the defect and propose two defensible alternatives.

Solution. The pairs 09:00–10:00 and 12:00–13:00 are one hour apart, but the shifted pair 10:00–12:00 is two hours apart. Therefore the row-based lag-one statistic mixes different physical intervals and has no consistent one-hour meaning. First alternative: preserve the explicit hourly index, mark 11:00 missing, and use an estimation method that handles incomplete lag pairs while documenting the convention and number of usable pairs. Second alternative: impute 11:00 using an expressly justified method, report that imputation, and check how the ACF changes.

Interpretation. Neither option makes the missing value an observed truth. Interpolation can create extra smoothness and artificial dependence; skipping invalid pairs changes uncertainty. For an historical forecasting experiment, an imputation using 12:00 when issuing a prediction at 10:00 would leak future information. Timestamps and information availability are part of the statistical model, not mere formatting.

44. Exercise 12 — Final transfer: advise an energy analyst

Problem. A team wants to deploy a one-hour demand forecast based only on a twelve-hour sample showing ACF(1)=0.25, ACF(2)=−0.40, PACF(2)≈−0.4933 and Ljung–Box p-values 0.16182 (two lags) and 0.00512 (three lags). The manager says: 'The three-lag test proved dependence, so fit AR(3) and launch tomorrow.' Write an evidence-based response.

Complete solution. First restate the data contract: 12 hourly synthetic deviations in MW, no missing timestamps; model is not trained or prospectively validated. Reproduce at least one calculation: lag-one centered cross-products sum to 5 and total squared deviations to 20, giving \(r_1=0.25\). The conditional second-lag association is \((-0.40-0.25^2)/(1-0.25^2)=-0.4933\). The two Ljung–Box p-values differ because they test different lag sets, with extremely questionable small-sample chi-square calibration. Neither identifies AR(3), a causal mechanism or forecast improvement.

Decision and next checks. Do not authorize deployment. Gather additional real hourly observations across weekdays, weekends and weather regimes; validate timestamps, gaps and operating changes; inspect stationarity and seasonal lags; choose model candidates before testing many patterns; estimate using training data only; examine residual ACF/PACF with appropriate degrees-of-freedom corrections; and compare one-hour-ahead predictions against naive and seasonal-naive baselines in rolling time windows. Report MAE in MW, uncertainty by forecast horizon and costs of large errors. The defensible current conclusion is that this small teaching sample illustrates nontrivial estimated linear lag structure, but supplies insufficient evidence for operational model selection. That distinction is the transferable reasoning the course expects.
