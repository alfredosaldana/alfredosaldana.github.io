01. The question comes before the plot

A time series is a sequence of measurements indexed by when they refer to, not merely a table that happens to contain a date column. An hourly electricity meter, a daily hospital census, monthly sales, and annual inflation are time series, but the meaning of their numbers differs. A timestamp might identify when a reading was recorded, the end of an interval, or the beginning of an accounting period. Before drawing a line, ask four questions: what is measured, for which unit, over what interval, and when was the information genuinely available? A wrong answer can make an apparently correct forecast unusable.

Consider an electricity utility that must anticipate tomorrow's hourly energy demand. The decision is operational: how much electricity to arrange in advance. The outcome is energy consumed during each hour, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), not instantaneous power measured in kilowatts (kW). We shall construct a transparent synthetic illustration of two complete calendar days of hourly slots, one of which contains a missing observation. No observations were collected from an actual utility. The small dataset exists so you can reproduce every operation and see where conclusions stop.

A useful forecast question is: At 18:00 on the first day, what can we predict about consumption from 19:00 to 23:00? Notice that this is more precise than "predict energy." It identifies the issue time, the target time range, the unit and implicitly the permissible information. A different question—at midnight predict all 24 hours of the following day—has a different information set and error structure even when it uses the same dataset.

02. Learning contract and prerequisites

You should know arithmetic, percentages, means, elementary probability, and graphs. We will remind you how a mean, a weighted sum, a variance and correlation work. This guide does not assume programming, differential equations, or advanced time-series modeling. Its objective is not to fit ARIMA or seasonal decomposition models. Those belong to later guides. The task here is to construct a trustworthy time-indexed dataset and to reason correctly about what can and cannot be inferred from temporal order.

By the end you should be able to state an observational unit, build an unambiguous time index, distinguish frequency from sampling interval and forecasting horizon, recognize serial dependence, compute and interpret a lag, aggregate energy safely, diagnose missing times, and detect information that leaks from the future into a historical forecast. A final transfer case asks you to redesign a flawed demand-forecast table rather than imitate a calculation.

Terms that need care are event time, when a phenomenon occurred; recording time, when it entered a system; and availability time, when a user of the forecasting model could have accessed the final value. These times may differ by minutes, days, or months. A forecast is only as historically honest as its availability assumptions.

03. Observation, unit, and index

Write a time series as \(Y_t\), where \(Y\) is the variable of interest and \(t\) identifies a time slot. If \(t\) means the one-hour interval 08:00–09:00 on a specific date, then \(Y_t\) can mean kWh consumed during that hour. The subscript is an index, not multiplication. Distinguish a calendar timestamp such as 2026-10-05 08:00 from an integer index \(t=8\). An integer may indicate position in an array; it does not establish whether a row is on Monday, is a holiday, or spans a daylight-saving transition.

A complete observation needs an entity, a precise temporal definition, a value, and a unit. For multiple meters, use the pair (meter_id, interval_start) as the key; timestamp alone is not unique across meters. If both a local timestamp and a zone are needed, store either an offset-aware datetime or UTC instant plus the original local display. Do not pretend that identical clock labels represent the same instant during the autumn daylight-saving transition.

The unit of analysis is also a scientific choice. A one-hour reading from a single meter is not an independent participant in an experiment. Twenty-four hours from one meter share physical infrastructure, weather, and behavior. Treating all 24 as independent homes would confuse repeated measurement with replication. Likewise, an aggregate national series and a series for a single building do not describe the same statistical population.

04. Establish an hourly calendar without ambiguity

Imagine the first synthetic day is Monday, 2026-10-05, and the second is Tuesday, 2026-10-06, in a fixed-offset time zone. Each displayed hour \(h=0,1,\ldots,23\) denotes the half-open interval [h:00, (h+1):00), so midnight's consumption belongs to the following calendar date. A half-open interval includes its starting instant and excludes its ending instant. That convention prevents an event exactly at 09:00 from being counted both in 08:00–09:00 and 09:00–10:00.

For this teaching example there is no clock change on the two dates. In production, a timezone should be identified by a reliable timezone name and the corresponding UTC instant. The local day when clocks spring forward can have 23 elapsed hours; the autumn clock change can produce 25. A data pipeline that assumes every local calendar day contains exactly 24 hourly timestamps can silently shift or duplicate measurements.

A fixed interval grid is a claim about how data ought to occur, not proof that every observation exists. We must construct the expected calendar first, then compare observed keys with expected keys. We also require a unique index. If two records appear for the same meter and interval, decide whether they represent duplicated ingestion, revisions, or separate channels before combining them.

05. Full synthetic dataset: two days, one missing slot

This illustrative dataset contains 48 planned hourly slots, one for each hour on October 5 and October 6. There are 47 observed values and one missing observation at Tuesday 14:00–15:00. A dash means unknown, not zero. Every number is a synthetic kWh value made for teaching; do not interpret the values as actual company observations or calibrated forecasts.

Read a row horizontally: Monday at 18:00–19:00 has 30 kWh, Tuesday at the same wall-clock interval has 31 kWh. Read a column vertically to see how consumption evolves across hours. The display is deliberately compact, but the complete raw observations are present. No additional hidden rows or external dataset are needed to reconstruct the calculations.

The data seem to rise during daytime and fall overnight, but two days do not establish a stable daily seasonal pattern. One can describe a shape without proving that it will recur. The presence of a missing Tuesday value is scientifically important: a smooth line joining 13:00 and 15:00 conceals the gap and suggests a measured trend where no observation exists. Any figure must represent the missing slot explicitly.

06. A time-series graph is evidence, not decoration

A line plot respects the ordering of observations and can reveal persistent rises, abrupt changes, cycles, flat intervals and gaps. It is often a useful first view, but its interpretation depends on axes and cadence. The horizontal axis should display actual time or honestly labeled hourly slots; the vertical axis should state the outcome and unit. Equal horizontal distances imply equal elapsed intervals only when that is how the index was constructed.

For the two days, the visual difference between midnight demand and evening demand is much larger than the difference between corresponding hours on consecutive days. This observation motivates looking at within-day patterns, but cannot justify a seasonal forecasting model from two observations per clock hour. Ask whether any apparent extreme point might be a transcription error, a special event, or a valid peak; the plot alone cannot decide.

Joining observations across missing intervals is a design choice, not an innocent cosmetic step. A forecasting system that marks a gap as zero may invent an artificial crash; one that interpolates may invent a measured value. Both changes alter statistical summaries. The right display distinguishes real measured points from estimated points, states what has been imputed, and retains a record of missingness for sensitivity checks.

07. Frequency, cadence, and horizon are different concepts

Observation frequency refers to the nominal temporal density: hourly, daily, weekly, monthly, or irregular event-driven. Sampling interval is the time elapsed between consecutive samples when samples are regular: 60 minutes for the hourly grid. Forecast horizon is the distance between the issue time and target time; a forecast issued at 18:00 for the hour 21:00–22:00 covers a later interval than one for 19:00–20:00. Frequency is a property of observation design; horizon is a property of a forecast question.

We can express a horizon as \(h\) periods if the grid has a known fixed spacing: \(\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}\) means a forecast for \(t+h\) based on information at origin \(t\). The hat indicates a prediction, and the vertical bar reads "given information through \(t\)." The index \(h=3\) is only meaningful as "three hours ahead" if every period is genuinely one hour. With irregular medical visits, three observations ahead is not necessarily three days or three months ahead.

A forecasting origin is the point at which a model would have to make its prediction. A target period is the interval whose outcome will later be judged. A lead time includes practical delays in acting on a forecast. If the grid is hourly but the dispatch must be scheduled two hours in advance, a one-hour-ahead statistical forecast may arrive too late for the decision. The operational clock matters as much as the spreadsheet clock.

08. Calendar time versus elapsed time

Calendar labels capture weekday, season, holidays and human schedules, while elapsed time measures objective duration. They usually coincide on an ordinary day but diverge near daylight-saving changes, leap days, irregular observations, and calendar months of different lengths. A "one month" interval does not have a fixed number of hours. A continuous elapsed-time variable cannot by itself encode weekend status.

Suppose one file timestamps each hourly consumption total at the interval's beginning and another at its end. Joining their rows on identical timestamps shifts one series by an hour. That can create apparently strong lag correlations and a fake lead–lag relation. Before every join, check whether each timestamp is an interval start, interval end, event time, or publication time. Write that choice in the data dictionary.

The appropriate representation depends on the question. For energy billing, local calendar days and the timezone matter. For equipment vibration sampled every 0.1 second, elapsed seconds may dominate. For a sensor that reports only when triggered, no fixed hourly cadence is implied. Converting all timestamps into sequential integers can conceal these distinctions. Convert units deliberately, and preserve enough information to reconstruct the original instants.

09. Energy is a flow; installed capacity is a stock

Energy consumption in kWh over an hour is a flow accumulated across a period; it adds over disjoint intervals. Instantaneous power in kW is a rate, and a single snapshot cannot be summed as if it were accumulated energy. A stock, such as water stored in a reservoir at 08:00, is a state observed at an instant; adding stocks across hours usually has no direct physical meaning.

For a constant 5 kW load over two hours, energy is \(5\,\mathrm{kW}\times 2\,\mathrm{h}=10\,\mathrm{kWh}\). If power varies, energy is the time integral of power: \(E=\int_{a}^{b}P(s)\,ds\). The integral is an accumulation operation; the units confirm why it works: kW multiplied by hours gives kWh. This is a Calculus I idea, not a complicated stochastic model. At discrete resolution, we approximate the integral by a sum of power readings multiplied by the time each represents.

For the case dataset, hourly entries are already accumulated kWh. Therefore daily energy is the sum of hourly values, provided the day's coverage is complete. Daily average hourly energy is that sum divided by the number of expected hourly intervals, but only if missing values are treated appropriately. The number obtained by summing a single power reading at each hour would not be the same quantity unless measurement conventions justified the approximation.

10. Aggregation: reconstruct the complete day

Monday has 24 observed hourly values. Adding the values gives 480 kWh for Monday. Dividing by 24 yields 20 kWh per hour, an average hourly energy amount. Notice the denominator: it represents 24 equally sized one-hour slots, not 24 independent households. As an alternative check, the first six values sum to 65 kWh; hours 06–11 sum to 122 kWh; hours 12–17 sum to 157 kWh; and hours 18–23 sum to 136 kWh. The four six-hour subtotals sum to \(65+122+157+136=480\) kWh.

For Tuesday, the sum of observed hourly values is 477 kWh across 23 known slots, and the observed-slot mean is \(477/23\approx20.7391\) kWh. This 477 is not the total energy of the complete Tuesday calendar day: an entire one-hour contribution is unavailable. Dividing 477 by 24 and reporting 19.875 as if it were the measured daily hourly mean would silently insert zero at the missing hour. Multiplying 477 by 24/23 would estimate a full day under an explicit representativeness assumption, but it would still be an estimate rather than a measurement.

A safer reporting table records date, sum over observed hours, expected slots, observed slots and completeness. It may show "Tuesday daily total: unavailable; observed subtotal: 477 kWh; coverage 23/24 = 95.83%." This preserves information without pretending that completeness is unimportant.

11. Group by calendar day without changing the scientific quantity

The aggregation operator must match the variable. For daily energy, sum the 24 hourly energy increments. For daily peak power, take the maximum of measured power over the day if that is the intended peak estimator. For a daily mean temperature, use a time-weighted average if sampling intervals differ; a simple arithmetic mean of unevenly spaced observations overweights periods with dense sampling.

If one sensor records power at 08:00 and 08:05 but another only at 08:00, blindly averaging all available readings does not give equal exposure by hour or by sensor. Define whether the target is "mean of recorded values," "time average," or "mean across devices." These are different estimands. A database aggregation function cannot choose the right scientific quantity for you.

Daily totals also hide intraday peaks. Monday's 480 kWh total does not tell the operator whether 30 kWh occurred at 18:00–19:00. Two days can have equal daily totals but very different peak timing and capacity requirements. If the decision is hourly dispatch, a daily summary is informative for context, not a substitute for hourly forecasts. Preserve granular measurements when possible and aggregate explicitly for a particular decision.

12. Missing values are absent measurements, not measured zeros

A true zero means the process produced no energy consumption in that interval. A missing observation means its value is unknown. The two states must not share an encoding. A third state is not applicable: for example, a site that was not yet operating. Yet another is provisional: a value exists, but a later meter correction may revise it. Those states can produce very different modeling implications and should be described separately.

One missing hour among 48 expected observations gives 47/48 = 97.9167% observed-slot completeness across the two-day demonstration. On Tuesday alone coverage is 23/24 = 95.8333%. Report both the missing count and coverage denominator. In a real system, first ask whether the gap is sensor failure, transmission delay, scheduled downtime, ingestion error or an actual zero erroneously coded as missing. That investigation may matter more than any imputation technique.

If the missing Tuesday 14:00 reading truly equals Monday's same-hour value of 26 kWh, a same-hour imputation would produce 503 kWh Tuesday total. This is an illustrative what-if, not a recovered observation. A lower/upper sensitivity range of 20–32 kWh for the missing hour would imply a daily total between 497 and 509 kWh. The range is useful only if its endpoints have an intelligible operational basis, not because they were chosen to produce a desired answer.

13. Imputation is a modeling assumption

Common gap-filling methods include constant imputation, last observation carried forward, linear interpolation, same-hour historical averages and model-based predictions. Each changes the data-generating story. A forward fill assumes the last known value remains reasonable; linear interpolation assumes a smooth path between neighboring observations; same-hour averaging assumes comparable daily cycles. These assumptions may fail during a power outage, an extreme weather event or a deliberate operating change.

For the missing Tuesday 14:00–15:00 value, adjacent Tuesday hours are 26 kWh at 13:00 and 27 kWh at 15:00. Linear interpolation gives \((26+27)/2=26.5\) kWh, hence an imputed daily total of \(477+26.5=503.5\) kWh. A same-hour previous-day method gives 26 kWh and total 503 kWh. The 0.5 kWh difference is not the entire uncertainty: neither method tells us how wrong it might be during an unexpected afternoon event.

Crucially, interpolation uses the future 15:00 value. It might be acceptable for retrospective reporting after the entire Tuesday is known, but is not available for a forecast issued at 14:00. The exact same procedure can be sensible for data repair and invalid for a real-time prediction experiment. Always record the as-of time of an imputation and whether the method can run without knowledge of the period being predicted.

14. Temporal dependence from first principles

If consecutive observations tend to resemble each other, the series exhibits serial dependence. A high-demand hour may be followed by another high-demand hour because households, weather and operations evolve gradually. Dependence concerns the joint behavior of observations at different times. It is not the same as the mean changing over a day, although both can appear in the same series.

The formal lag-\(k\) autocovariance of a stationary process is \(\gamma(k)=\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-k})\). The corresponding autocorrelation is \(\rho(k)=\gamma(k)/\gamma(0)\) when variance is positive and constant. These formulas assume that relevant population moments do not depend on the calendar origin. Our 48-point teaching series cannot establish this assumption. Calculating an empirical lag statistic from it is a descriptive exercise, not proof of stationary dependence or a reliable long-run autocorrelation.

Under independence across time, covariances at nonzero lags are zero when moments exist. The converse does not generally hold: zero covariance does not guarantee independence for nonlinear relationships. A pattern may arise because the mean changes by hour of day rather than because random deviations persist. This is why later time-series analysis separates recurring calendar structure from dependence remaining after that structure is accounted for.

Equation · symbols, units, and assumptions explained in the text
\[\rho(k)=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-k})}{\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)}\]

15. Lags: what information is shifted?

A lag-1 value at time \(t\) is \(Y_{t-1}\), the observation from the preceding hourly interval. At Tuesday 18:00, the lag-1 value is Tuesday 17:00's 29 kWh; the lag-24 value is Monday 18:00's 30 kWh. A lag is a candidate predictor, not automatically a causal driver. The value of \(Y_{t-24}\) means the same wall-clock hour yesterday only if the grid is hourly, timezones and calendar alignment are handled correctly, and no index rows have been silently deleted.

Suppose a dataset drops Tuesday 14:00 because it is missing, then creates lags by shifting rows. At Tuesday 15:00 the preceding row would be Tuesday 13:00, two elapsed hours earlier, not one. Row shift and time shift are no longer equivalent. The solution is to complete the hourly index, preserve a missing value at 14:00, and shift against the correctly spaced grid. The resulting lag-1 at 15:00 is missing, which accurately represents that information gap.

Lags should be labeled with origin and target. When predicting Tuesday 18:00 from a forecast issued at Tuesday 16:00, the actual Tuesday 17:00 outcome is not yet observed at issue time, although it is one row before the target. A target-relative lag can still be future information relative to the forecast origin. Availability, not just row order, determines whether a feature is legal.

16. Compute a lag without losing the calendar meaning

The Monday 18:00 hour has 30 kWh, Monday 19:00 has 29 kWh, and Monday 20:00 has 25 kWh. For Monday at 19:00, lag-1 is 30 kWh; at 20:00, lag-1 is 29 kWh. If we use a persistence forecast \(\widehat Y_{t\mid t-1}=Y_{t-1}\), we predict Monday 20:00 consumption as 29 kWh while the observed value is 25 kWh. Forecast error, defined as actual minus predicted, equals \(25-29=-4\) kWh. Its absolute error is 4 kWh.

This single error is an illustration, not evidence that persistence is a good forecasting algorithm. One error cannot estimate average performance. Nor is persistence a claim that energy has been physically carried forward. It simply uses the last observed interval as a predictor for the next one. If measurements arrive with a two-hour delay, even that lag may not be available in real time.

For an hour-of-day comparison, Tuesday 18:00 consumption is 31 kWh versus Monday 18:00's 30 kWh, a +1 kWh day-over-day difference. But only one comparable pair is available, so we should not call that a dependable seasonal growth estimate. The lesson is to distinguish a mechanically computable change from a reproducible statistical pattern.

17. Why time-series rows are not iid samples

Independent and identically distributed (iid) observations are independent draws from the same probability distribution. Many standard introductory standard-error formulas rely on iid sampling or a carefully specified weaker assumption. Hourly electricity demand does not naturally satisfy iid: the distributions change by hour, temperatures evolve, and adjacent disturbances may be correlated.

To understand the consequence, let the sample mean of \(n\) time-indexed observations be \(\overline Y=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}Y_t\). Its variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y)=n^{-2}\sum_t\sum_s\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_s)\). Under independence, off-diagonal covariances vanish, leaving the familiar variance of the mean. Under positive serial dependence, the extra covariance terms can make uncertainty considerably larger. This derivation follows from the variance-of-a-sum identity and does not require advanced time-series machinery.

A commonly cited effective-sample-size heuristic is \(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx n(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)\) for a long stationary AR(1)-type process with positive lag-1 correlation \(\rho\). This is a conditional approximation, not a universally valid conversion. We do not estimate \(\rho\) from our tiny two-day synthetic example and use that number to claim an inferential confidence interval. Instead, identify temporal dependence as a reason to question a default iid analysis.

Equation · symbols, units, and assumptions explained in the text
\[\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_s)\]

18. Forecast origin and horizon: a reproducible protocol

Fix an origin at Monday 18:00, just after the measurement for 17:00–18:00 becomes available. A prediction issued then can use readings ending no later than 18:00, provided they have been delivered and validated. If the target is 20:00–21:00, the forecast must be generated before that interval begins. Any recorded consumption from Monday 19:00–20:00 could be known at 20:00 but is not yet known at the 18:00 origin. A two-hour-ahead forecast is therefore not just a shifted one-step prediction.

The notation \(\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}\) should be read as the prediction of an outcome indexed by target \(t+h\) constructed using data available at origin \(t\). The conditioning bar is a reminder of an information set, not a guarantee of statistical correctness. Operational availability may be stricter than event time: a meter reading could be recorded at 18:00 but not delivered until 18:07.

A reproducible experiment records the exact origin timestamp, all model and feature versions, the intended target interval, the issue time and the actual outcome once finalized. Without that provenance, a later analyst might unknowingly use revised values or newly published weather observations while pretending to reconstruct a forecast that occurred in the past.

\[\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}=f_h(\mathcal{F}_t)\]

19. The as-of information set

Define \(\mathcal F_t\) as the set of information genuinely available to the forecaster by origin time \(t\). It may include past finalized energy values, a weather forecast issued earlier, the known calendar and planned operating schedules. It may not include tomorrow's realized temperature, later revisions to today's readings, or sales totals published after the decision. A valid forecast \(\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}\) must be constructed from \(\mathcal F_t\).

A weather forecast for tomorrow is permissible if it was actually issued by the current origin. Tomorrow's measured temperature is not. Both columns could be labeled "temperature for tomorrow" in an analytics file, which is why metadata must include issue and availability timestamps. The calendar holiday flag is generally known in advance, though unexpected closure orders are not. Similarly, a product price scheduled for tomorrow is known only if the plan was committed and shared before the origin.

Target leakage is the introduction of information that would not have been available when the prediction had to be made. Leakage can also occur without an explicit future column: a scaling parameter, imputation average or feature selection procedure estimated on all historical and future evaluation rows can bring future distributional information into the training process. The core question is not "is this feature correlated with the target?" but "could this exact value have been computed then?"

20. Three leakage traps, with repairs

First, using the true Tuesday 15:00 energy to linearly interpolate missing Tuesday 14:00 energy is valid for some retrospective reports but invalid for a prediction issued at 14:00. Repair: either preserve the missingness or use a forward-looking method with only earlier observations and record its uncertainty.

Second, training a model for Tuesday's energy with Tuesday's realized outside temperature gives the model information from the same future interval. Repair: use archived weather forecasts with their issue timestamps; compare the forecast as actually known at each origin. If only realized temperature is archived, clearly label the analysis as explanatory or conditional-on-realized-weather, not a realistic forecast backtest.

Third, normalizing a series using the mean and standard deviation computed over the full dataset before splitting can leak properties of the evaluation period into training. Repair: fit preprocessing on the historical training segment, then apply those frozen parameters to later times. Repeat this procedure at every rolling evaluation origin. None of these repairs guarantees forecast skill; they make the evaluation defensible.

21. Chronological splitting is not random row splitting

Suppose data span 100 consecutive days and the objective is to forecast future demand. Randomly allocating rows to training and test sets can place day 90 in training while testing day 30. Such an evaluation lets the training process indirectly learn about the future distribution. The resulting test accuracy does not answer the operational question. A chronological split trains on earlier dates and evaluates on later dates.

A simple design trains on days 1–70, validates choices on days 71–85 and reserves days 86–100 for final evaluation. The numbers are hypothetical and illustrate a protocol, not a universally recommended split ratio. A rolling-origin design is more informative: fit on data through day 70 and forecast day 71; then fit on data through day 71 and forecast day 72; continue while preserving the information set at each origin.

Be precise about the forecast horizon. A rolling one-step evaluation that updates with yesterday's observed value is different from a 30-day forecast issued once at day 70. Do not use the former's error rate to promise the latter's performance. If preprocessing uses target values from evaluation dates, a chronological split alone is not enough to prevent leakage.

22. Irregular series and exposure adjustments

Some series are not naturally hourly. Emergency department arrivals can be aggregated by day, while failures of industrial equipment occur at irregular event times. An observed count per day depends partly on exposure: a clinic open for 6 hours is not comparable to one open for 24 hours if the underlying event rate is similar. Reporting only the count may make shorter operating hours look like lower intensity.

A simple rate is \(\text{rate}=\text{events}/\text{exposure}\). If a clinic records 12 visits in 6 operating hours, the observed rate is \(12/6=2\) visits per hour; another records 20 visits across 10 hours, also \(20/10=2\) visits per hour. Their counts differ, but their time-normalized rates are equal. This arithmetic does not establish that future arrivals are independent or Poisson, only that the denominator matters.

If the series records monthly revenues, months differ in length, selling days and seasonal context. Dividing by exposure might produce revenue per open day when that matches the business question; it would be wrong if the decision concerns the total cash collected per calendar month. The measure and its denominator must follow the decision rather than an automatic normalization convention.

Equation · symbols, units, and assumptions explained in the text
\[\text{observed rate}=\frac{\text{event count}}{\text{exposure}}\]

23. Stocks, flows and aligned comparisons

Imagine a warehouse reports 100 units of inventory at Monday 08:00 and 90 at 09:00. Summing 100+90=190 does not give "inventory during two hours." A stock snapshot is a state; one may report the last value, the average level, or the change of −10 units, depending on the question. In contrast, shipments of 8 and 12 units during the two hours are flows and can be summed to 20 shipped units.

A stock–flow identity can link them: ending stock = opening stock + arrivals − departures, assuming no loss, reclassification or measurement error. This identity is a reconciliation check, not an assumption that every imbalance is fraud. If inventory at 09:00 is 90, starting inventory is 100, and recorded arrivals are 5, then recorded departures would need to be 15 units for the simple balance to hold: \(90=100+5-15\).

Align intervals before relating stock changes to flows. A departure timestamp at 09:00 might belong to the interval ending then or beginning then depending on system definitions. Misalignment can manufacture apparently predictive relationships where the flow is actually recorded after the stock snapshot.

24. Dependence is not trend, and trend is not causality

A trend is a broad change in level; seasonality is recurring variation tied to a calendar cycle; serial dependence is a relationship between observations at different lags. They can coexist but are not synonyms. In the synthetic two-day energy example, the within-day shape is visible, yet there are not enough repeated days to establish a durable seasonal component or estimate a trend across months.

Two trending series can exhibit high sample correlation because both move with time, even without one causing the other. For example, suppose cumulative training hours and cumulative electricity spending both rise across a year. Their association may reflect common passage of time and growth, not a direct mechanism. Conversely, a lag relationship does not identify causal direction. A leading variable might be a proxy for a shared calendar driver.

Our goal is disciplined description. More elaborate decomposition, differencing, AR/MA models and forecasting uncertainty are taught later in the curriculum. This guide prepares the ground by defining what values exist, how times align, what is unknown, and why statistical dependence requires methods that respect time.

25. A quality-control checklist for an incoming series

Before modeling, reconcile expected timestamps with observed timestamps; validate ordering, duplicates and timezone handling; inspect numerical units and whether values are stocks, flows or rates. A daily total that accidentally mixes kWh, MWh and kW has a unit problem that no forecasting algorithm can repair. A file sorted lexicographically on unpadded date strings may not be in chronological order. A daylight-saving hour repeated with the same unqualified local label is not a duplicate by definition.

Then audit availability: for each candidate feature, record event time, recording time, revision time and historical availability. Ask whether the output should be a point forecast, an interval, a classification of capacity risk, or a scheduling decision. Define how missing intervals will be represented and what sensitivity checks will be reported. State the prediction origin and target before examining future accuracy.

Finally, separate three statements. "The measured Monday hourly total is 480 kWh" is a description supported by complete observations. "The unknown Tuesday total might be near 503 kWh if the missing hour resembles yesterday" is an assumption-dependent estimate. "Tomorrow's total will be 503 kWh" is a forecast requiring a prediction method and proper prospective evaluation. None should be substituted for the others.

25A. Time indexes and database integrity: a complete worked protocol

A practical analyst should be able to reconstruct an hour-by-hour grid from raw records. First, specify one meter, its timezone and the intended interval boundaries. Second, generate the expected 48 intervals from Monday 00:00 inclusive to Wednesday 00:00 exclusive. Third, match each received meter record to its exact interval key. Fourth, count keys that are absent and keys that appear more than once. Fifth, identify whether the value is raw, corrected or provisional; do not automatically overwrite an earlier value without tracking its availability time. Finally, calculate the coverage percentage using expected slots as denominator.

This protocol is stronger than merely sorting the received 47 records. Sorting cannot reveal the time that is missing unless we know the calendar that ought to exist. Nor does sorting settle the ambiguity of two readings labeled 01:00 during a daylight-saving fallback: each may be valid if its UTC offset differs. Conversely, two fully identical records with the same meter, UTC instant and revision ID may be duplicate ingestion. The distinction is testable only if the key contains the right information.

To audit a completed dataset, reconcile the sum of interval counts: 47 observed values plus one missing placeholder equals 48 expected slots. This is a data-coverage identity, not a statistical hypothesis test. If the counts disagree, investigate the cause before plotting or modeling. A forecast built on a subtly shifted time index may fit beautiful lines while learning the wrong hour.

25B. A decision table for time-series quantities

A useful classification asks what aggregation answers each decision. Energy per hour is an interval accumulation; for daily usage, add complete intervals. Power sampled at an instant is a rate; for energy, integrate over time or apply a documented numerical approximation. Inventory is a stock; to report current availability, use a specified snapshot. Number of admissions is an event count aggregated by non-overlapping admission times. Number of patients present is a census snapshot. Visits per open hour is a rate with an exposure denominator. The same time index can contain all these columns without making their values interchangeable.

Suppose a plant produces 120, 150 and 130 items in three consecutive one-hour slots. Production is a flow, so the three-hour total is \(120+150+130=400\) items. The corresponding inventories at the three hour ends might be 50, 40 and 55 items. Their sum, 145, is not production, deliveries, or a closing stock. A valid closing stock is 55 items; a change between first and third snapshots is \(55-50=5\) items, over the span those snapshots define. A production rate would require exposure, such as items per operating hour.

This distinction matters when missingness arises. A missing one-hour production count means a total is incomplete; a missing inventory snapshot may still leave earlier and later levels available but does not reveal the path or maximum. The correct repair and decision statement follow the physical meaning of the measurement. If the time-indexed record cannot answer what the quantity represents, pause the analysis rather than imposing an aggregate automatically.

25C. Point-in-time correctness and revised information

Data often have more than one relevant timestamp. A purchase may occur at 09:00, be received by an analytics service at 09:02, and be marked fraudulent or refunded three days later. If a historical experiment uses the eventual refund status to predict decisions made at 09:01, it has hidden later knowledge inside a seemingly harmless historical field. A valid reconstruction uses the version that existed at the decision time, even if the final version would be more accurate today.

A robust feature record can store event_time, recorded_at, available_at, revision_number and value. For each forecast origin, choose the latest permissible version whose available_at is not later than the origin. This is often called a point-in-time join or as-of join. It is a conceptual requirement; a specific database implementation may need additional checks for equal timestamps, timezones and duplicate revisions.

Consider predicting demand Monday at 18:00. A meter value for 17:00–18:00 may have event_time=18:00, available_at=18:07 and final corrected value published the next morning. Neither the preliminary reading nor the corrected final reading is legitimately available at exactly 18:00 if the system sees it only at 18:07. A model that uses it at the 18:00 origin has an optimistic backtest, despite using a timestamp that appears to refer to the past. Historical availability is thus an independent design axis beyond temporal ordering.

25D. What a time-aware evaluation should report

A responsible evaluation specifies the forecast origin, horizon, target unit, observation frequency, validation calendar, preprocessing scope and the timing of external information. The reported error metric also needs units. A Mean Absolute Error (MAE) computed on hourly energy has units of kWh: \(\operatorname{MAE}=n^{-1}\sum_i|Y_i-\widehat Y_i|\). If two forecasts have absolute errors 4 and 6 kWh, MAE is \((4+6)/2=5\) kWh. This example is a demonstration of the formula, not a measured performance estimate for our two-day series.

A Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) divides absolute error by the observed outcome. It becomes undefined at an observed zero and unstable near zero. Therefore one should not declare it the universal metric for electricity, transaction counts or intermittent demand. A metric chosen after examining which one favors the model produces biased reporting. If peak underprediction carries a larger operational cost than peak overprediction, the decision may require a weighted loss or asymmetric cost analysis beyond a simple MAE.

Errors from overlapping forecast horizons may share target periods, and evaluation observations can be serially correlated. Consequently a confidence interval treating every error as iid can be misleading. In later guides, models and intervals will be built to account for such structure. At this stage the essential discipline is a reproducible evaluation table: one row per issued forecast with issue timestamp, target interval, horizon, frozen predictors and finalized outcome.

25E. Counterexamples that expose hidden assumptions

Counterexample one: a spreadsheet has exactly 24 rows labeled hourly, but one hour occurs twice and another hour is absent. Row count equals 24, yet the calendar is incomplete and its daily total might double-count one interval. Verify unique timestamps against an expected grid; never equate row count with coverage.

Counterexample two: the hourly electricity total and the daily total are both correctly calculated, but the daily report is used to dispatch power at 18:00. The aggregation destroyed peak timing, so a correct sum can still support an incorrect operational decision. The forecasting target must match the action's resolution.

Counterexample three: an engineered lag feature is computed by taking the preceding row from a sorted table and appears historically valid. A missing interval or late revision changes which row was truly available. For prediction, reconstruct actual elapsed-time lags and origin-specific availability; row ordering is not enough.

Counterexample four: a team measures high lag-1 correlation in a two-day hourly series and reports that demand is stationary. Repeated daily rhythms and limited observation length can produce apparent correlations without establishing constant population moments. Descriptive statistics require explicit assumptions before they become inferential statements. These examples show why the trustworthy questions are not only "can I calculate it?" but also "does its index, unit, information set and scientific interpretation match my decision?"

26. Worked exercise 1 — Identify the observational unit

Problem. A platform records one row per click, including user ID and event timestamp. A manager asks for the number of unique users active each hour. Is the raw click log already an hourly time series of unique users?

Solution. No. The click log is event-level data: a single user may click repeatedly in one hour. First define a timezone and half-open hourly boundaries; then group by hour and count distinct user IDs within each interval. The resulting value is an hourly count of distinct users, not a click count. A user present in two different hours may be counted once in each hour; this is intentional for hourly active users. Missing hours should appear as zero only if the logging system was functioning and absence of events really means no activity. Otherwise they are unknown. The transformation changes both unit of analysis and denominator of the business question. It does not create independent hourly observations.

27. Worked exercise 2 — Rebuild the day total

Problem. Reproduce Monday's daily energy total from the four six-hour blocks. What is the average hourly energy?

Solution. Hours 00–05 contribute \(12+11+11+10+10+11=65\) kWh. Hours 06–11 contribute \(14+18+21+22+23+24=122\) kWh. Hours 12–17 contribute \(25+25+26+26+27+28=157\) kWh. Hours 18–23 contribute \(30+29+25+21+17+14=136\) kWh. Summing gives \(65+122+157+136=480\) kWh. Because all 24 one-hour slots are observed, mean hourly energy is \(480/24=20\) kWh per hour-long interval. As a unit check, the daily sum is kWh, while the averaged interval energy is kWh per hourly slot. The calculation does not use iid assumptions and it does not measure peak instantaneous kW.

28. Worked exercise 3 — Missingness and sensitivity

Problem. Tuesday's observed subtotal is 477 kWh with 23 of 24 slots. Calculate coverage and totals under a 26-kWh same-hour imputation, a 26.5-kWh interpolation and a 20–32-kWh sensitivity range.

Solution. Coverage is \(23/24\times100=95.8333\%\). Under the same-hour assumption, the total is \(477+26=503\) kWh. Under interpolation using adjacent measured hours, \((26+27)/2=26.5\) kWh and total \(503.5\) kWh. The range gives \(477+20=497\) to \(477+32=509\) kWh. Every resulting full-day total is estimated, not observed. Interpolation requires a later hour and is invalid for a forecast issued before that later value was available. The correct choice depends on the purpose and assumptions; if no estimate is justified, retain the observed subtotal and incomplete coverage.

29. Worked exercise 4 — Lag and forecast error

Problem. At Monday 19:00, observed hourly energy is 29 kWh; at 20:00 it is 25 kWh. Use a lag-1 persistence forecast for 20:00 and compute signed and absolute errors.

Solution. Forecast 20:00 with the preceding interval's observed value: \(\widehat Y_{20\mid19}=29\) kWh. Actual is \(Y_{20}=25\) kWh. Signed error using actual minus forecast is \(25-29=-4\) kWh; absolute error is \(|-4|=4\) kWh. A negative signed error under this convention means overprediction. This is one example and cannot quantify long-run model skill. If the preceding interval was delayed and unavailable at issue time, even this simple forecast would violate the availability requirement.

30. Worked exercise 5 — Row shift versus hour shift

Problem. A Tuesday table omits the 14:00 row entirely. A program shifts a numerical column down by one row to form lag_1. What happens to the 15:00 record?

Solution. Its preceding stored row is 13:00, so the naive operation assigns \(Y_{13}=26\) kWh as the 15:00 lag. But the preceding hour is 14:00, whose outcome is unknown. The correct lag-1 value is missing. Insert the expected 14:00 timestamp with a missing value before shifting, and use the true time grid. The 15:00 lag stays unknown instead of falsely looking like a valid predictor. This is a data-integrity problem, not a request to fill an empty cell with any available number.

31. Worked exercise 6 — Stock versus flow

Problem. A storage tank holds 100 liters at 08:00 and 90 liters at 09:00. Five liters arrive between readings. Infer outflow under a simple balance and decide whether stocks should be summed.

Solution. The balance is \(90=100+5-O\), where \(O\) is outflow. Rearranging gives \(O=100+5-90=15\) liters during the interval. Adding 100+90=190 liters does not describe accumulated throughput; it adds snapshots of one tank at two times. A valid summary could be the stock change −10 liters, the closing stock 90 liters, or an average stock if the averaging method is defined. The calculation assumes no leakage or unrecorded transfers; without that assumption, the residual is not automatically explained by outflow.

32. Worked exercise 7 — Exposure normalization

Problem. Clinic A observes 12 arrivals in six open hours; B observes 20 in ten open hours. Compare counts and simple hourly rates.

Solution. Clinic B has eight more visits, \(20-12=8\), but A's rate is \(12/6=2\) visits per open hour and B's rate is \(20/10=2\) visits per open hour. The raw count comparison reflects different exposure duration. The normalized rates are equal for these observed periods. This does not prove arrival processes or workloads are identical: peak concentration, staffing, case mix and repeat visits may differ. If the decision is total daily staffing, both count and hourly shape remain relevant.

33. Worked exercise 8 — Availability audit

Problem. At 09:00 Monday, a forecast of Tuesday 14:00–15:00 demand is requested. Candidate features are Monday 08:00 energy (finalized by 08:10), Tuesday observed 14:00 temperature, Monday's holiday flag, and a temperature forecast issued Monday 06:00 for Tuesday 14:00. Classify them.

Solution. Monday 08:00 energy is permitted because it was finalized before Monday 09:00. The historical calendar holiday flag is permitted if defined and known by the origin. Tuesday's realized temperature is prohibited because it has not occurred at Monday 09:00. The archived temperature forecast issued at 06:00 Monday is permitted, provided its exact vintage is available; it represents an uncertain expectation, not the realized weather. All four may appear in the same modern database, but only three belong to the historical information set. Keep origin, issue timestamps and finalized fields to enforce the classification.

34. Worked exercise 9 — Irregular timestamps

Problem. Readings occur at 08:00, 08:05, 08:40 and 09:00, each with a temperature. Explain why their simple mean need not equal the average temperature during the hour.

Solution. A simple mean gives every reading weight 1/4, although the first two readings are only five minutes apart and there are 35 minutes before the third. If the process is assumed constant between readings (step-function approximation), the 08:00 value covers five minutes, the 08:05 value 35 minutes and the 08:40 value 20 minutes, while 09:00 begins the next interval. The time average over 08:00–09:00 is thus \((5T_{08:00}+35T_{08:05}+20T_{08:40})/60\), with degrees Celsius as the unit. This is an explicit interpolation convention, not a measured integral. Other conventions give different approximations.

35. Worked exercise 10 — Chronological evaluation

Problem. A team randomly samples 20% of days for testing a model that will forecast tomorrow's demand. Why might this be misleading, and what is a defensible alternative?

Solution. Random splitting may train on dates later than some test dates and can expose future regimes or preprocessing summaries. Use an earlier training window followed by later validation and test windows; at every evaluation origin, construct features only from data and external forecasts available then. For example, train on days 1–70, validate on 71–85 and test on 86–100, while refitting any scalers using only the training window. If the production task is a fixed 14-day forecast, test that same horizon instead of reporting only refreshed one-day-ahead errors. Chronology is necessary for this question but does not alone prevent feature leakage.

36. Worked exercise 11 — Independence misconception

Problem. An analyst uses all 48 planned hourly slots as 48 independent customer observations to compute a standard error. Identify two errors.

Solution. First, only 47 hourly energy values were observed; the missing Tuesday 14:00 value is not a measured zero or a valid independent outcome. Second, hourly values from one meter are temporally ordered repeated measurements, not 48 independent customers. Their means may differ by clock hour and residuals may be correlated. The usual iid standard error can therefore understate uncertainty or answer the wrong sampling question. The proper approach starts by defining the inferential population and dependence structure, then choosing a time-aware analysis when justified. Nothing in our two-day example supports a precise confidence interval for long-run demand.

37. Worked exercise 12 — Transfer to a hospital series

Problem. A hospital tracks patients present at 23:59 each day and new admissions during each day. It proposes adding both columns across seven dates to calculate "weekly patients" and using final discharge diagnoses for forecasts issued at admission. Repair the analysis.

Solution. Patients present at 23:59 is a stock measured at an instant; summing daily census snapshots double-counts patients across days and is not a unique-patient total. New daily admissions are a flow and can be summed to weekly admissions if dates and coverage do not overlap. Unique weekly patients require patient identifiers and a distinct-person definition, not an arithmetic sum of census snapshots. A discharge diagnosis assigned after the encounter is not available at admission and would leak outcome information into an admission-time forecast. Define a timestamped admission information set, the exact forecast target, hospital timezone, late data and missingness before evaluating any model. A useful final report distinguishes observed counts, estimated values and genuinely prospective predictions; this transferable reasoning is the core skill of the guide.

38. Final synthesis: what an honest time-indexed dataset promises

A trustworthy time series begins with a definition of the observational unit and its measurement interval. It preserves a time index that makes elapsed gaps visible, a timezone convention that prevents ambiguous duplicates, units that distinguish stocks from flows, and completeness indicators that prevent missing values from becoming false zeros. It also preserves the origin-specific information set: the model cannot use a result, revision or future measurement simply because the historian has it now.

Return to the synthetic energy example. We can state with certainty that Monday's measured daily sum is 480 kWh. We can state that Tuesday's measured subtotal is 477 kWh over 23 of 24 expected hours. We can illustrate a Tuesday full-day estimate of 503 or 503.5 kWh under clearly declared imputations. We cannot infer from two days that tomorrow will have a repeating seasonal curve, calculate reliable long-run autocorrelation, or claim a proven forecast accuracy. That distinction between what the data show, what assumptions permit and what future tests must establish is the foundation for all subsequent time-series guides.

Before moving forward, ask yourself: can I specify the index, units, horizon, origin, coverage, legitimate features and dependence issue without looking at an algorithm? Can I reproduce the 480-kWh Monday total and explain why Tuesday's 477-kWh subtotal is not a full-day total? Can I identify a future feature even if it is present in an analysis file today? If yes, you are prepared to study trend, seasonality, autocorrelation modeling and forecast validation without committing the most fundamental design errors.
