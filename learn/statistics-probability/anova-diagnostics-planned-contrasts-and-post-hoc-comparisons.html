1. Why this guide is necessary

A significant analysis of variance (ANOVA) answers a useful but limited question: is there evidence that at least one population mean differs from another? It does not tell the engineer which process differs, whether the difference is practically worthwhile, or whether the model's assumptions are credible. Conversely, a global F test that is not significant does not automatically prohibit a single contrast that was specified in advance. These distinctions are the purpose of this guide. We will make decisions about the statistical model before selecting an impressive comparison, and we will explain the uncertainty attached to each reported difference.

Imagine three manufacturing processes, A, B, and C, producing material measured in kilograms of usable output per hour (kg/h). Management's substantive question is whether process B outperforms the average performance of A and C. That question is not identical to asking whether any pair among A, B, and C differs. We can formulate the former as one planned contrast, and the latter as a family of three pairwise comparisons. The designs, estimators, error guarantees, and interpretations are different even when they use the same observations.

The first dataset is deliberately small and entirely synthetic, created only to expose the arithmetic. No numbers are presented as actual factory observations, no causal assignment is claimed, and no economic benefit can be calculated without cost information. After developing the equal-variance route, we will use a second complete synthetic dataset with substantially different within-process variation. It will show why mechanically applying the first procedure to every dataset can be misleading. In each case, the raw data, assumptions, algebra, numerical substitution, conclusion, and limitations remain visible.

A reliable workflow asks four questions in order: what does the experimental design justify; how do the residuals behave; which comparison was the scientific target; and which simultaneous error guarantee matches the set of claims? A calculation is not a substitute for answering these questions. In applied research, the order matters because looking at outcomes before defining the question can quietly turn a planned comparison into an opportunistic search.

2. Prerequisites and learning map

You should know a sample mean, sample variance, standard deviation, independent observations, a null hypothesis, a p-value, a confidence interval, and the core logic of the one-way F test. Those ideas correspond to the prerequisites from Guides 42, 46 and 47. We reintroduce the necessary symbols here so you do not need another page open while following the calculations. Elementary algebra and the idea of a sampling distribution are sufficient for the main route; interpreting critical values from the Studentized range distribution requires a new concept, which we explain rather than assume.

For group i, let nᵢ denote its number of independently sampled observations, yᵢⱼ its j-th response in kg/h, ȳᵢ the arithmetic sample mean, μᵢ the unknown population mean, and sᵢ² the unbiased sample variance in (kg/h)². The total sample size is N=Σnᵢ, and k is the number of groups. A residual is eᵢⱼ=yᵢⱼ−ȳᵢ: the observed value minus the model's fitted group mean. Unlike a raw observation, it isolates the variation left unexplained after allowing each group its own center.

We use α=0.05 as the illustrative error level. This is a choice, not a law of science: an application with serious safety consequences might use a smaller threshold or emphasize interval widths instead. Degrees of freedom, written df, describe how many independent pieces of variation are available after estimating parameters. For a single-factor equal-variance model, the within-group df is N−k. The pooled mean square error MSE estimates the common population variance σ² when the common-variance assumption is defensible.

Our goals are observable. You will calculate residuals and explain diagnostic plots; formulate and test a prespecified contrast with its confidence interval; calculate Tukey's three simultaneous comparisons; derive the unequal-variance Welch weights and adjusted degrees of freedom; calculate Games–Howell pairwise statistics and intervals; and select a coherent analysis strategy without using a preliminary significance test as an automatic switch. Each calculation is followed by an explanation of what it does not establish.

Dataset II records A: 8, 9, 10, 11; B: 12, 14, 16; C: 7, 11, 15, 19, 23 kg/h. Sample sizes are 4, 3, and 5. The means are 9.5, 14, and 15 kg/h; the sample variances are 1.6667, 4 and 40 (kg/h)². These variance differences are built into the synthetic example to illustrate heteroscedasticity: the dispersion is not comparable across groups. Actual manufacturing data would require dates, equipment identity, batch identifiers, operator changes and the sampling/assignment protocol to evaluate dependence and possible confounding.

For Dataset I we will ask one planned question, B versus the average of A and C, and separately ask which of the three pairs differ after simultaneous adjustment. For Dataset II we will ask whether group means differ under a variance-unequal model and whether any specific pair is established by Games–Howell. The second question is intentionally more demanding: it protects a collection of claims about pairs instead of one overall claim. Do not treat answers from different models as interchangeable just because their p-values can be printed in the same table.

4. The model and the anatomy of a residual

The classical one-way model is yᵢⱼ=μᵢ+εᵢⱼ, equivalently yᵢⱼ=μ+τᵢ+εᵢⱼ with a suitable identification constraint on group effects τᵢ. Here εᵢⱼ is the random error relative to the group-specific mean. We estimate μᵢ by ȳᵢ, yielding eᵢⱼ=yᵢⱼ−ȳᵢ. In Dataset I, A's residuals are −1,0,+1 kg/h. B's residuals are −1,0,+1 and C's are −1,0,+1. Every group's residuals sum to zero because the mean is chosen to balance positive and negative deviations; this is an algebraic property of fitting group means, not evidence that independence or normality is true.

Square the nine residuals: each group contributes (−1)²+0²+1²=2 (kg/h)². The within-group sum of squares is SSE=2+2+2=6 (kg/h)², with df=N−k=9−3=6. Therefore MSE=SSE/df=6/6=1 (kg/h)². Its square root, 1 kg/h, is the pooled residual standard deviation. The units are essential: a variance is in squared units, while the standard error of a mean difference returns to kg/h.

The fitted value for all A observations is 9, for B it is 12, and for C it is 10. Plotting residuals against these fitted values produces three vertical columns centered near zero, with equal spreads in this artificial example. A residual plot is thus a visualization of model errors conditional on fitted level, not a plot of the raw group outcomes. A residual of +1 kg/h means a run exceeded its own process mean by 1 kg/h; it does not mean its process outperformed another process by that amount.

The pooled variance depends on the model. If the spreads are genuinely different, combining all squared deviations into one MSE can obscure differences in precision across groups. Welch's method will instead use each group's variance separately. Neither method rescues observations that share an unmodeled common cause, such as several measurements from the same manufacturing batch. Dependence is a question about how data were collected, not simply the symmetry of residuals.

5. Independence comes before attractive plots

Independence means that knowing an observation's random error does not systematically tell us another observation's error under the intended model. For example, if all runs of process B are measured on Monday while all runs of A are measured on Tuesday, an unrecorded temperature shift may masquerade as a process effect. If three successive samples come from the same batch, treating them as three independent replications creates pseudoreplication and makes uncertainty look artificially small. Neither a normal Q–Q plot nor a high p-value from a variance test can diagnose the experimental assignment history.

Ask what constitutes an experimental unit: a separate production run, a machine, a batch or a sample taken from within one run? A measurement within a batch is not automatically a new independent unit. If treatments were randomized across genuinely independent runs, causal interpretation of the process assignment becomes more credible. If processes were merely observed in different periods, comparisons can be descriptive associations and may reflect omitted conditions. Recording run order permits residuals-versus-time diagnostics; randomizing and blocking can prevent confounding in a future study.

An honest report states the unit, whether randomization occurred, how missing or rejected runs were handled, and whether there is clustering. If observations are paired or repeated on the same unit, a repeated-measures, blocked or mixed-effects analysis may be needed instead of ordinary one-way ANOVA. Choosing Welch for unequal variances does not correct repeated-measures dependence. This is why selecting software by the appearance of the sample standard deviations alone is insufficient.

6. Residual-versus-fitted plots

The horizontal axis of a residual-versus-fitted plot carries predicted group means; the vertical axis carries eᵢⱼ. Under an appropriate constant-variance model, the cloud's vertical scatter should be reasonably similar at the different fitted values, without a trend that suggests a missing systematic component. Because a one-factor model assigns the same fit to each observation within a group, the plot naturally has vertical stacks, not a continuous horizontal cloud. A wider stack for one process suggests more within-process variability, and an extreme isolated dot may deserve investigation for a measurement error or a real unusual operating condition.

In Dataset I the residual stacks at 9, 10 and 12 all occupy −1 through +1 kg/h. The display is compatible with equal spreads, but the extremely small, deliberately regular sample cannot establish a population-level assumption. Dataset II has within-group residuals A: −1.5,−0.5,+0.5,+1.5; B: −2,0,+2; C: −8,−4,0,+4,+8. The pronounced widening for process C is immediately visible. Its sample variance of 40 is twenty-four times A's 1.6667. That numerical comparison is strong descriptive evidence of differing sample dispersions, though population inference remains uncertain with small samples.

Interpret plot features as questions, not verdicts. A funnel asks whether variance changes with fitted level; a curve in a regression plot asks about an omitted relationship; ordered streaks ask about time dependence; isolated extreme residuals ask about recording and influence. Do not delete a run simply to make diagnostics beautiful. Establish an exclusion rule based on the measurement process, disclose what changed, and ideally show a sensitivity analysis both with and without justified exclusions.

7. Q–Q plots and the normality question

A normal Q–Q plot compares ordered sample residuals with the quantiles expected from a normal distribution. A roughly straight pattern suggests that the marginal distribution is not in obvious conflict with normality; a strong S-shape may suggest heavy or light tails, while a remote end point may suggest an outlier. The theoretical axis is measured in standardized normal quantiles; the vertical axis may retain residual units. A Q–Q plot does not demand that every dot lie precisely on a line. In small samples, both apparently straight plots and alarming deviations can occur by chance.

Normality in the classical model refers to the within-group random errors εᵢⱼ, not to the distribution of all raw outputs pooled across processes. Pooling raw data from groups with different means may create a multimodal pattern even when each group's errors are normal. Conversely, pooling residuals across groups with very different variances can give a misleading combined shape. Check group structure and sample sizes while interpreting the Q–Q plot; for very small groups, graphical evidence is weak.

Shapiro–Wilk and related tests offer numerical diagnostics, but their p-values are not proof of normality. A large p-value can simply reflect low power, whereas a tiny p-value in a huge dataset can detect a practically harmless departure. Good practice is to combine data provenance, plots, the consequences of deviations, and sensitivity analysis. The equal-variance ANOVA and Tukey procedures are often reasonably robust to modest normality departures in well-designed balanced samples, but severe skewness, outliers or dependence can change the error properties; robustness is conditional, not a universal exemption.

8. Equal variances: what may be tested

Homoscedasticity means that the population error variances are equal: Var(ε_A)=Var(ε_B)=Var(ε_C)=σ². Dataset I has equal observed sample variances s_A²=s_B²=s_C²=1. Dataset II instead has 1.6667, 4, and 40. Compare variances and their relation to sample sizes, not just whether a formal test crossed α=0.05. An especially troublesome combination for conventional ANOVA is imbalance coupled with different variances because the pooled standard error may assign misleading precision to some group comparisons.

Levene's test examines differences in variation, and the median-centered Brown–Forsythe version is less affected by certain forms of nonnormality than the original mean-centered approach. Yet a nonsignificant test does not establish equality. For Dataset II, a median-centered Levene calculation yields p≈0.0695; a rule saying 'because p exceeds .05, variances are equal' would overlook the observed 24-fold sample variance ratio and the small group's limited diagnostic power. The appropriate statement is that this particular sample provides inconclusive formal-test evidence, while the pattern warrants an unequal-variance analysis.

Do not choose the entire inference method through an automatic preliminary variance test without acknowledging its consequences. A two-stage selection procedure has its own error properties, and a nonsignificant diagnostic does not make a modeling assumption true. Instead, use subject knowledge, design, variance pattern, group imbalance, and a prespecified robust analysis when warranted. Welch ANOVA and Games–Howell are coherent options for an independent-groups unequal-variance setting, but do not repair dependence, nonrandom selection or every severe distributional departure.

9. Revisit the global F result

For Dataset I, the grand mean is (9+12+10)/3=31/3≈10.3333 kg/h. The between-group sum of squares is SSB=3[(9−31/3)²+(12−31/3)²+(10−31/3)²]=3[(−4/3)²+(5/3)²+(−1/3)²]=3(42/9)=14 (kg/h)². Its df is k−1=2; MSB=14/2=7 (kg/h)². We already obtained SSE=6 and MSE=1, so F=MSB/MSE=7. The F distribution with (2,6) degrees of freedom gives p≈0.027. This is evidence against the joint null H₀:μ_A=μ_B=μ_C under the model.

The global result does not say that each pair differs, that the largest observed mean is certainly the largest population mean, or that any gain is financially valuable. It asks about all three population means together. After observing significance, one might choose comparisons to explain where differences lie, but the error correction should match the number and selection of those comparisons. A prespecified scientific question remains conceptually separate from an exploratory request to examine every possible pair.

The identity SST=SSB+SSE is checkable here: 14+6=20 (kg/h)². This decomposition describes variation; it is not a causal decomposition unless the design justifies attributing differences to treatment. The F statistic grows when between-group variation increases relative to within-group variation, but its calibration depends on assumptions. The next sections move from this single omnibus statement to estimable differences with associated uncertainty.

10. Planned contrasts: specify the target first

A contrast is a weighted combination of population means L=Σcᵢμᵢ whose coefficients sum to zero: Σcᵢ=0. This restriction makes the quantity represent a comparison rather than an overall level: adding the same amount to every group mean leaves L unchanged. The estimate is L̂=Σcᵢȳᵢ. A simple pairwise contrast B−A uses coefficients (−1,1,0). The engineering question B−(A+C)/2 uses (−1/2,1,−1/2); these coefficients sum to −1/2+1−1/2=0.

Write the contrast before inspecting the sample outcomes whenever its planned status matters. Its signs are not decorative. A positive value of B−(A+C)/2 means that B's mean output exceeds the average of the other two process means; a negative value means the reverse. If scientific priority instead assigned twice the importance to A as to C, the weights would change and must be justified. One contrast can be tested on its own if it was genuinely the only preplanned question. If several contrasts were selected in advance, their family and error protection require separate consideration.

For Dataset I, L̂=−(1/2)(9)+1(12)−(1/2)(10)=−4.5+12−5=2.5 kg/h. This result has a direct physical interpretation: B's sample mean exceeds the equal-weight average of A and C by 2.5 kg of usable output per hour. It does not establish that B wins against A and C individually with the same level of confidence. The contrast concerns a specific combined benchmark, not a pairwise ranking table.

11. Standard error of a planned contrast

If group means are independent and share population error variance σ², then Var(Σcᵢȳᵢ)=σ²Σ(cᵢ²/nᵢ). The reason is that independent random components have additive variances and multiplying a random variable by c multiplies its variance by c². The squares prevent positive and negative coefficients from canceling uncertainty. We estimate σ² with MSE, producing SE(L̂)=√[MSE Σ(cᵢ²/nᵢ)]. Units under the square root are (kg/h)², so the resulting standard error is kg/h, as required.

For c=(−1/2,1,−1/2), every nᵢ=3 and MSE=1. Substitute without skipping arithmetic: Σ(cᵢ²/nᵢ)=(1/4)/3+1/3+(1/4)/3=1/12+4/12+1/12=6/12=1/2. Therefore Var(L̂) is estimated as 1×1/2=0.5 (kg/h)² and SE=√0.5≈0.7071 kg/h. The standard error measures sampling uncertainty in the estimated contrast; it is not the standard deviation of individual production readings.

The t statistic for testing H₀:L=0 is t=(L̂−0)/SE=2.5/0.7071≈3.5355, with the common residual df=6. A two-sided t distribution gives p≈0.0123. The sign of t follows the chosen coefficient orientation, but the two-sided p-value depends on its magnitude. This preplanned contrast is significant at 0.05 under the stated model. The result is a comparison of group means conditional on independence, credible error assumptions, and a genuinely prespecified target; it does not prove that a randomly selected future run of B will beat a future run of A or C.

12. Confidence interval and decision relevance

A two-sided 95% confidence interval for one individual planned contrast is L̂±t₀.₉₇₅,₆ SE, where the t critical value is approximately 2.4469. Multiply first: 2.4469×0.7071≈1.7302 kg/h. Subtract and add to 2.5 to get approximately [0.7698,4.2302] kg/h. The lower endpoint is positive, so this individual interval excludes zero, agreeing with the two-sided t test at α=0.05. This is a single-contrast interval, not simultaneous protection for every comparison someone may later request.

Repeated-sampling interpretation matters: when the model and procedure are appropriate, 95% of intervals generated by repeated independent samples would cover the fixed population contrast. It is not correct to say that the already-fixed population contrast has a 95% probability of lying in this particular frequentist interval. The observed interval expresses a range of values compatible with the procedure and data. Its width also communicates how little information nine measurements provide about the magnitude of improvement.

Suppose an engineer predefines a commercially meaningful gain of 2 kg/h. Although our estimated gain is 2.5, the interval includes gains smaller than 2, so the sample does not establish that the commercial threshold has been exceeded at corresponding confidence. Distinguish 'greater than zero' from 'large enough to matter'. Even the upper limit is a conditional mean difference, not a guaranteed realized increase in every production hour. Cost, downtime, variance and safety can all matter when making a decision.

13. What does 'planned' really mean?

A planned contrast is selected because of a substantive question before looking at the outcomes used to evaluate it. Writing a contrast in a report after seeing B's large mean does not retroactively make it planned. This distinction matters because choosing a particularly large-looking contrast among many possibilities biases its apparent evidential strength. The same problem appears when many outcomes, transformations, subgroups or time windows were tried and only the most favorable one was disclosed. The nominal p-value from one reported test does not describe the full selection process.

Planning does not mean that all future analysis is forbidden. Exploratory contrasts can be useful to generate hypotheses, identify unexpected patterns, and guide a new experiment. Their uncertainty should reflect the explored family or be presented openly as exploratory. A preregistered single contrast can be tested without first demanding a significant global ANOVA, provided its inferential plan genuinely stands on its own. Conversely, the mere fact that a global F test is significant does not exempt exploratory pairwise tests from multiplicity control.

An analysis plan should state outcomes, comparison coefficients, directionality, α, sample and unit definitions, missing-data handling, any planned multiplicity family, and what diagnostics will trigger a sensitivity analysis. Afterward, record deviations rather than hiding them. This creates an understandable link between the scientific question and the calculation instead of allowing the data to write the question retrospectively.

14. Why a family of pairwise comparisons changes the problem

With k groups there are m=k(k−1)/2 unordered pairwise comparisons. For three processes that means m=3: A−B, A−C and B−C. Testing each independently at α=0.05 without adjustment does not generally keep the probability of at least one false rejection across the family at 0.05. This probability is the family-wise error rate (FWER), introduced in Guide 46. Under independent true-null tests the probability of at least one false rejection would be 1−(1−0.05)³≈0.1426; actual pairwise statistics share sample means, so independence is not generally valid, but the arithmetic illustrates the multiplicity issue.

Bonferroni can control FWER for a declared family by assigning α/m per test, or by multiplying each unadjusted p-value by m and capping at one. Holm's step-down correction is another general option. Tukey's method is specialized to all pairwise comparisons of group means under a common-variance model. It uses the distribution of the maximum standardized range, accounting for the selection among pairs. These are different guarantees or calibrations, not three labels for the same calculation.

Define the family from the question. If a scientist promised only one contrast before observing data, all conceivable contrasts are not automatically part of its planned family. If the claim is 'we tested every pair and highlight the ones that worked', then all three comparisons belong to the family, including nonsignificant ones. Reporting the whole table prevents readers from mistaking selected discoveries for the result of one isolated experiment.

15. The Studentized range and Tukey HSD

Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) method uses the Studentized range distribution. Conceptually, imagine repeatedly sampling k equal-mean groups under the null, calculating the spread from the largest sample mean to the smallest, and dividing by an estimate of its standard deviation. The critical value q₁₋α;k,df is calibrated against the extreme difference among all groups. Because it targets the maximum, it protects the entire pairwise family rather than merely a preselected pair.

For equal sample sizes n and common error variance estimated by MSE, the Tukey comparison statistic is qᵢⱼ=|ȳᵢ−ȳⱼ|/√(MSE/n). The simultaneous 100(1−α)% confidence interval for μᵢ−μⱼ is (ȳᵢ−ȳⱼ)±q₁₋α;k,df√(MSE/n). Notice that the denominator differs from the standard t standard error for a difference: SE_t=√(2MSE/n). Indeed q=√2|t| for the same pair. The distinction reflects how the Studentized range distribution is defined; do not substitute t critical values into Tukey's formula.

For Dataset I, n=3, MSE=1, k=3 and df=6. The common Tukey scale is √(1/3)=0.57735 kg/h. The 95% Studentized range critical value is q≈4.3392. Therefore the simultaneous margin is 4.3392×0.57735≈2.5053 kg/h. A pairwise observed absolute difference must exceed this margin for the two-sided Tukey simultaneous interval to exclude zero. This common margin is possible because all sample sizes are equal; Tukey–Kramer modifies the scale when they are not.

The resulting pattern is not paradoxical. The global F test rejects equality of all three means; the planned B-versus-average contrast rejects its own null; but the all-pairs procedure establishes only B versus A. Each statement addresses a different null and error guarantee. A nonsignificant pair is not 'proved equal'; the interval may still allow a meaningful positive or negative population difference. It would also be wrong to rank the processes with certainty from these three estimates alone.

17. Tukey–Kramer when group sizes differ

If group sizes differ yet a common within-group variance is credible, use the Tukey–Kramer scale for pair i,j: SE_q,ij=√[(MSE/2)(1/nᵢ+1/nⱼ)]. The corresponding interval is (ȳᵢ−ȳⱼ)±q₁₋α;k,N−k SE_q,ij. For equal n, the square-root term simplifies to √(MSE/n), recovering Tukey HSD. The technique therefore adjusts the width for precision differences caused by different sample counts while still pooling a common variance.

To see why the scale changes, suppose MSE=4 (kg/h)², n_A=4 and n_B=9. Then SE_q,AB=√[(4/2)(1/4+1/9)]=√[2×13/36]=√(13/18)≈0.8498 kg/h. A naive formula using n=9 for both groups would give √(4/9)=0.6667 and produce unjustifiably narrow intervals for the smaller group. The proper scale reflects both n_A and n_B. A third group may have its own sample size, but the same overall k and residual df enter the Studentized range critical value.

Tukey–Kramer is not a repair for wildly unequal population variances. An unequal sample size is a different issue from heteroscedasticity. The former changes the precision available from each group; the latter changes the variance parameter itself. When both differ, applying a pooled-MSE method can yield poor error control, especially in unfavorable sample-size/variance combinations. The second scenario will therefore use a variance-aware alternative rather than mechanically selecting Tukey–Kramer.

18. Simultaneous confidence is a joint statement

One 95% confidence interval targets coverage of one fixed population parameter over repeated samples. A family of 95% simultaneous intervals targets a stronger event: all intervals in the specified family cover their respective fixed parameters together with probability at least approximately 95% under the method's assumptions. The simultaneous intervals tend to be wider because they protect against an extreme error somewhere in the family. A zero-excluding interval corresponds to rejection of that particular null at the simultaneous family level for a matched two-sided procedure.

The phrase '95% simultaneous' is not a probability that every conclusion in this one realized table is true. Nor does it mean a 95% chance that the best-looking process is best in all future operations. It is a repeated-sampling error guarantee for the defined family. Omitting comparisons because they are visually inconvenient changes what the reader thinks was protected. Report estimates, units, simultaneous bounds, method, family size, and relevant assumptions together.

The plotted interval for B−C can overlap zero while its point estimate is +2 kg/h. The estimate and uncertainty are not competing facts: one is the observed center, the other describes plausible sampling variation. A decision-maker may still investigate the potentially meaningful difference, but the correct claim is 'insufficient evidence to distinguish this pair with the chosen joint confidence', not 'the processes have identical output'.

19. Effect sizes and engineering thresholds

Statistical significance answers a calibration question about sampling evidence, not whether a process change is worth installing. If adopting B costs more in labor, energy or downtime, a mean advantage of 0.5 kg/h could be too small. Conversely, a possible advantage of several kilograms per hour may justify a larger experiment even if a simultaneous interval currently includes zero. State the engineering minimum meaningful gain before seeing the outcomes when possible, and compare confidence bounds with that threshold.

For our B−A interval [0.4947,5.5053] kg/h, we can say the data support a positive mean difference under Tukey's simultaneous procedure. If management requires at least 2 kg/h to justify the conversion, the lower bound of approximately 0.495 does not establish that the minimum is met. The correct distinction is between a claim of positive difference and a claim of sufficient benefit. We would need information on costs, yields, production scale and uncertainties beyond the modeled response before converting output to financial returns.

Standardized effect sizes, such as a mean difference divided by an error standard deviation, may help compare outcomes measured on different scales, but they cannot replace units in manufacturing decisions. In Dataset I, the pooled residual SD is 1 kg/h and the B−A difference is 3 kg/h, a standardized difference of 3. This unusually large value is an artifact of our carefully constructed teaching dataset and should not be treated as a general expected industry result.

20. A separate unequal-variance scenario

Now switch datasets rather than silently changing the model applied to Dataset I. Dataset II contains twelve invented independent observations: A=(8,9,10,11), B=(12,14,16), C=(7,11,15,19,23), all in kg/h. The group sizes are n_A=4, n_B=3, n_C=5. The sample means are 9.5,14,15 kg/h, and the sample variances are s_A²=5/3≈1.6667, s_B²=4, s_C²=40 (kg/h)². For A, the squared deviations 2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25=5 are divided by n_A−1=3. For B, 4+0+4=8 divided by 2 is 4. For C, 64+16+0+16+64=160 divided by 4 is 40.

The spread changes dramatically, and sample sizes are not equal. Compare the residual displays before any formal test: A's deviations reach only ±1.5, B's ±2, and C's ±8 kg/h. Because the data are invented, this pattern is intentionally obvious; in actual work, investigate whether process C's wider variation reflects physical reality, measurement scale, an outlier, time trend or correlated runs. We assume independent groups for the mathematical demonstration; that assumption must be verified from design in a real analysis.

For comparison, ordinary pooled-variance one-way ANOVA on these numbers gives F≈1.8707 and p≈0.2092, whereas Welch ANOVA gives F≈6.1197 and p≈0.0474. This contrast does not show that Welch is always 'more powerful' or that the smaller p-value is automatically correct. It shows that different weighting and variance assumptions can materially alter the question's estimated uncertainty. We choose the variance-aware route because this scenario was built with heterogeneous spreads and unequal sample sizes, not because we searched for a significant result.

21. Why Welch weights the means

A group mean with sample variance sᵢ² and sample size nᵢ has estimated sampling variance sᵢ²/nᵢ. Its inverse, wᵢ=nᵢ/sᵢ², is a precision weight: large groups with low variance supply more information about their own means than small, noisy groups. Welch ANOVA uses those weights rather than pooling all groups into one MSE. The weighted center is ȳ_w=(Σwᵢȳᵢ)/(Σwᵢ). This is not the raw grand mean, and it need not be the simple average of group means.

Compute Dataset II weights: w_A=4/(5/3)=2.4, w_B=3/4=0.75, w_C=5/40=0.125. Their sum W=3.275. The weighted numerator is 2.4×9.5+0.75×14+0.125×15=22.8+10.5+1.875=35.175. Therefore ȳ_w=35.175/3.275≈10.74046 kg/h. Notice how group C has the largest mean but receives the smallest precision weight because its mean is estimated with a very noisy sample.

These weights are estimated from the data, not known perfectly, so Welch's calibration includes a degrees-of-freedom correction. Weighted averages do not make extreme or dependent data harmless. In particular, a small group's variance estimate can be unstable, and treating a sample variance as absolutely certain would overstate the precision. The corrected test addresses this uncertainty approximately under independent samples with suitable distributional behavior.

22. Welch ANOVA from components

For k=3 groups, define W=Σwᵢ and C=Σ[(1−wᵢ/W)²/(nᵢ−1)]. The Welch statistic can be written F_W={Σwᵢ(ȳᵢ−ȳ_w)²/(k−1)}/{1+[2(k−2)/(k²−1)]C}. Its numerator df is k−1; the approximate denominator df is ν=(k²−1)/(3C). All divisions are dimensionally valid: wᵢ has inverse squared-output units, its product with squared mean deviations is dimensionless, and C is dimensionless. This is a Welch approximation, not the same exact pooled-variance F result under the classical normal common-variance model.

For our data, wᵢ/W are approximately 0.73282, 0.22901, 0.03817. Calculate C=(1−0.73282)²/3+(1−0.22901)²/2+(1−0.03817)²/4≈0.02379+0.29722+0.23128≈0.55229. The correction factor is 1+[2(1)/8]×0.55229=1+0.13807≈1.13807. The weighted mean-square numerator is Σwᵢ(ȳᵢ−10.74046)²/2≈6.965; division by 1.13807 produces F_W≈6.11973. The denominator df is ν=8/(3×0.552289)≈4.82839.

Referencing an F distribution with numerator df=2 and denominator df≈4.82839 gives p≈0.04744. At α=0.05, Welch's global test rejects the null of equal population means under its assumptions. Fractional df are intentional; they account for variance-estimation uncertainty. This test does not identify which pair differs, and it should not be followed by the pooled-MSE Tukey procedure as though the variance problem had disappeared.

23. Games–Howell: the pairwise companion

Games–Howell compares every pair while allowing group variances to differ. For groups i and j, define a=sᵢ²/nᵢ and b=sⱼ²/nⱼ. The standard error of their mean difference is SE_t=√(a+b); Games–Howell uses Studentized-range scale SE_q=√[(a+b)/2], so q_ij=|ȳᵢ−ȳⱼ|/SE_q. Its pair-specific Satterthwaite degrees of freedom are ν_ij=(a+b)²/[a²/(nᵢ−1)+b²/(nⱼ−1)]. The family of k groups enters the Studentized-range reference distribution; here k=3.

The simultaneous interval for μᵢ−μⱼ is (ȳᵢ−ȳⱼ)±q₁₋α;k,νij SE_q. Because SE_q and ν_ij vary by pair, Games–Howell intervals can have very different widths. That is the central reason they are not merely Tukey's method with a renamed p-value. In small samples its nominal family-wise error protection is approximate, not an unconditional exact theorem; the method can be unreliable with very small group counts or severe deviations from assumptions.

It is tempting to mix Welch's global p-value with ordinary Tukey intervals, but those calculations would assume incompatible error structures. Conversely, using Games–Howell does not certify that the global Welch test had to be significant first. Report the pairwise family and method explicitly. The next section calculates every pair so the contrast between estimates and simultaneous uncertainty is not concealed.

24. Games–Howell: A versus B

For A−B, the estimated mean difference is 9.5−14=−4.5 kg/h. The group-mean variance contributions are a=(5/3)/4=5/12≈0.41667 and b=4/3≈1.33333 (kg/h)². Add them: a+b=1.75. Thus SE_t=√1.75≈1.32288 kg/h, while SE_q=√(1.75/2)=√0.875≈0.93541 kg/h. The observed q is 4.5/0.93541≈4.81070. Note that q is not the t statistic; it uses the division by two inside the square root for the Studentized-range scale.

Now compute ν_AB=(1.75)²/[(0.41667²/3)+(1.33333²/2)]≈3.0625/(0.05787+0.88889)≈3.23472. For k=3, α=0.05 and ν≈3.235, the Studentized range critical value is approximately 5.63765, larger than the observed q. The interval margin is 5.63765×0.93541≈5.27354 kg/h. Consequently the simultaneous interval is −4.5±5.27354≈[−9.77354,0.77354] kg/h; adjusted p≈0.07614.

Despite a large negative observed A−B difference, the family-wise comparison is inconclusive at 0.05. The interval includes zero and also permits economically large differences. The result is not evidence that A and B have equal means; rather, three observations for B and heteroscedastic variability leave substantial uncertainty after accounting for the full pairwise family. Reporting only the estimated gap would hide this distinction.

25. Games–Howell: A versus C

For A−C, the difference is 9.5−15=−5.5 kg/h. We again have a=0.41667, but now b=40/5=8. The variance sum is 8.41667, and SE_q=√(8.41667/2)=√4.20833≈2.05142 kg/h. Therefore q_AC=5.5/2.05142≈2.68107. A large mean difference can produce a small standardized statistic when one group is extremely variable; evidence depends on signal relative to uncertainty, not the raw gap alone.

Calculate ν_AC=(8.41667)²/[(0.41667²/3)+(8²/4)]≈4.41156. The simultaneous Studentized-range critical value for k=3 is approximately 4.82865, yielding a margin of 4.82865×2.05142≈9.90561 kg/h. The interval is −5.5±9.90561≈[−15.40561,4.40561] kg/h, with adjusted p≈0.24463. It is very wide because C is noisy and because a family of comparisons is being protected.

Notice the scientific implication. It is not appropriate to conclude that C 'definitely outperforms A' just because its sample mean exceeds A's by 5.5 kg/h. Nor can the non-rejection be used to proclaim equality. An experiment with more independently collected runs, especially in the variable process, might provide sharper inference. Sample size planning should reflect anticipated unequal variances and the minimum practically meaningful effect.

This is mathematically possible because the global weighted test and the simultaneous pairwise procedure use different statistics, reference distributions and rejection regions. Never force the pairwise answers to mirror the global answer; explain the distinction and the uncertainty.

27. A coherent decision workflow

Start with the design: distinguish independent runs from paired or clustered observations and identify possible confounding. Define the population means and primary scientific question. Inspect the raw values, sample sizes, group spreads, measurement units and missingness. Examine residuals versus fitted values, residuals versus collection order if available, and Q–Q behavior; use formal diagnostics only as supporting evidence. A diagnostic p-value does not absolve an analyst from inspecting the design or understanding the sample.

Then choose the inferential family. For a preplanned single contrast under credible common variance, a pooled-MSE t interval directly addresses that target. For all pairs under credible common variance, Tukey HSD or Tukey–Kramer provides simultaneous comparison intervals. For independent groups whose variances differ meaningfully, Welch's global test and Games–Howell pairwise comparisons form a more coherent route. If outcomes are paired, hierarchical, skewed with severe outliers, or have other structural problems, the appropriate alternative may be a different model entirely. Do not combine procedures whose variance assumptions conflict.

Finally report decisions with their boundaries. Give raw group summaries, effect estimates, interval type and coverage, adjusted p-values if used, the family definition, model assumptions and what diagnostics revealed. Separate what the sample suggests from what it establishes. A single post-hoc comparison does not establish the superiority of a manufacturing process over all alternatives; that claim requires a clearly defined population, a family of comparisons, acceptable uncertainty and a meaningful operational benefit.

28. Common interpretive mistakes

Mistake: 'The global ANOVA was significant, so all means differ.' The global null is an intersection of equality claims. Rejecting it supports at least one difference, not all three. Mistake: 'No significant global F means no planned contrast can be tested.' A genuinely prespecified contrast may have its own test; obligatory omnibus gating is not a universal requirement. Mistake: 'Levene p>.05 proves equal variances.' Non-rejection cannot establish the assumption, especially with little data.

Mistake: 'One 95% interval is a 95% simultaneous interval.' A single-parameter guarantee does not automatically protect a family. Mistake: 'Games–Howell uses the pooled MSE.' It uses each pair's group variances and pair-specific degrees of freedom. Mistake: 'A non-significant pair is equivalent.' Equivalence requires a predefined equivalence margin and a suitable equivalence analysis. Mistake: 'The widest observed gap must be significant.' Its standard error and multiple-comparison critical value matter.

Mistake: 'The residuals look normal, so observations are independent.' Independence comes from design and often from temporal or clustering diagnostics, not a normal plot. Mistake: 'The best p-value defines the question.' A question selected after seeing the data needs transparent exploratory labeling and potentially selection-aware uncertainty. Mistake: 'A small p-value is an economic recommendation.' Cost, variability, scale, implementation and the minimum meaningful difference require independent consideration.

29. Worked practice 1: residual arithmetic

Problem. In Dataset I, calculate residuals for B=(11,12,13) and find their contribution to SSE. Why does their sum equal zero? Solution. B's mean is (11+12+13)/3=12 kg/h. Subtract this same fitted mean from each observation: 11−12=−1, 12−12=0 and 13−12=+1 kg/h. The sum is −1+0+1=0, because Σ(yⱼ−ȳ)=Σyⱼ−nȳ=0 by the definition of a sample mean. The squares sum to 1+0+1=2 (kg/h)². Check. Groups A and C produce the same squared sum, so total SSE=2+2+2=6. Interpretation. A zero residual sum is a fitted-model identity, not a test of distributional assumptions.

30. Worked practice 2: diagnose a pattern

Problem. Dataset II residuals for C are −8,−4,0,4,8, while A residuals are −1.5,−0.5,0,0.5,1.5. What would a residual-versus-fitted plot reveal, and would a nonsignificant Levene test erase your concern? Solution. C's vertical stack spans 16 kg/h, versus 3 kg/h for A. C's sample variance is 40 while A's is 1.6667, a ratio of 24. The plot shows sharply differing observed spreads. A median-centered Levene p-value near 0.0695 is not evidence that the two variances are identical; it may have low power with 12 observations. Action. Investigate operational causes and predefine an unequal-variance analysis for independent groups. Limit. Even Welch does not fix possible correlated batches.

31. Worked practice 3: build a contrast

Problem. Compare the mean of B with the equally weighted average of A and C. Give coefficients, estimate, standard error and t value for Dataset I. Solution. Coefficients in A,B,C order are (−1/2,1,−1/2); their sum is zero. The estimate is −9/2+12−10/2=2.5 kg/h. With MSE=1 and nᵢ=3, Σcᵢ²/nᵢ=1/12+1/3+1/12=1/2. SE=√(1/2)=0.7071 kg/h. Thus t=2.5/0.7071=3.5355, df=6, two-sided p≈0.0123. Interpretation. B exceeds the average benchmark in the sample; the corresponding population comparison is statistically supported under the model. Limit. This is one planned contrast, not three pairwise claims.

32. Worked practice 4: interpret the interval

Problem. A 95% individual planned-contrast interval is [0.7698,4.2302] kg/h. Does it establish a strictly positive difference? A gain of at least 2 kg/h? Solution. Zero is below the lower bound, so the interval excludes the null value zero and supports a positive mean difference using the matched two-sided 5% test. Yet 2 kg/h lies inside the interval; values such as 1 kg/h are also compatible with it. Thus it does not establish that the gain exceeds 2 kg/h. Check. Its midpoint is (0.7698+4.2302)/2=2.5, matching L̂. Limit. It is not simultaneous protection for additional contrasts found after the data were examined.

33. Worked practice 5: simultaneous pairwise decisions

Problem. In Dataset I, q_crit≈4.3392 and SE_q=√(1/3). Which pairwise differences pass Tukey HSD? Solution. The margin is 4.3392×0.57735≈2.5053 kg/h. The absolute mean differences are |B−A|=3, |B−C|=2, |C−A|=1. Only 3 exceeds 2.5053. Therefore only B−A has a simultaneous interval excluding zero: [0.4947,5.5053]. B−C yields [−0.5053,4.5053], and C−A yields [−1.5053,3.5053]. Check. The adjusted p-values 0.0242, 0.1089 and 0.4827 give the same decisions. Limit. Non-rejection does not demonstrate equality of the other pairs.

34. Worked practice 6: a changed sample size

Problem. Suppose MSE=4 (kg/h)², n_A=4 and n_B=9 under a credible common-variance model. Compute the Tukey–Kramer pairwise scale. Solution. The formula is √[(MSE/2)(1/n_A+1/n_B)]. Substitute √[(4/2)(1/4+1/9)]=√[2(9/36+4/36)]=√(26/36)=√(13/18)≈0.8498 kg/h. Verification. Using n=9 for both would produce √(4/9)=0.6667, less than the correct scale, because it falsely assigns A the precision of nine observations. Interpretation. Unequal group sizes change interval widths even with equal population variances. Limit. This does not address unequal group variances.

35. Worked practice 7: Welch precision weights

Problem. Calculate Welch's weights and weighted center for Dataset II. Solution. A has n=4 and s²=5/3, so w_A=4/(5/3)=12/5=2.4. B has n=3,s²=4, so w_B=3/4=0.75. C has n=5,s²=40, so w_C=5/40=0.125. Total W=3.275. The weighted sum of means is 2.4×9.5+0.75×14+0.125×15=35.175. Divide: 35.175/3.275≈10.74046 kg/h. Check. It lies between the smallest and largest group mean, as a weighted average with positive weights must. Interpretation. C receives the least weight because its sample mean is least precise, not because its physical output is unimportant.

36. Worked practice 8: Welch degrees of freedom

Problem. Dataset II has k=3, W=3.275 and C≈0.552289. Calculate Welch's denominator degrees of freedom and interpret it. Solution. The formula is ν=(k²−1)/(3C)=(9−1)/(3×0.552289)=8/1.656867≈4.82839. The number need not be an integer because it is a Satterthwaite-type approximation for the uncertainty introduced by unequal, estimated variances. Together with the weighted F≈6.11973 and numerator df=2, the reference F distribution yields p≈0.04744. Check. A numerical implementation of scipy.stats.f_oneway(A,B,C,equal_var=False) agrees. Limit. A significant global test does not itself identify an individual group pair.

37. Worked practice 9: Games–Howell for A–B

Problem. With A mean 9.5, variance 5/3 and n=4; B mean 14, variance 4 and n=3, calculate the Games–Howell statistic and a simultaneous interval. Solution. Difference A−B=−4.5. Contributions are 5/12 and 4/3, adding to 1.75. SE_q=√(1.75/2)=√0.875≈0.93541. q=4.5/0.93541≈4.81070. Pair-specific df=(1.75)²/[(5/12)²/3+(4/3)²/2]≈3.23472. With q_crit≈5.63765, margin=5.63765×0.93541≈5.27354, giving [−9.77354,0.77354] kg/h. Conclusion. The interval crosses zero; adjusted p≈0.07614. Interpretation. An observed gap is not enough to overcome uncertainty and multiplicity.

38. Worked practice 10: transfer to a new policy

Problem. An engineer observes five process groups and decides to publish only the largest estimated pairwise gap. Another researcher had committed before sampling to compare a new process against the average of four controls. Which analyses match the questions? Solution. The first engineer selected from m=5×4/2=10 possible pairs and cannot act as though the selected result were one isolated planned test. With credible equal variances and independent groups, a family-wise Tukey procedure addresses all ten pairs; with substantial unequal variances, a Games–Howell family may be more coherent. The second researcher has one explicit contrast with coefficients (1,−1/4,−1/4,−1/4,−1/4) in new-process-first order. They sum to 1−4/4=0. A planned-contrast estimate and variance formula directly address the predeclared question, with adjustments if other confirmatory tests are also planned. Transfer. If data consist of repeated measurements from the same machine, neither independent-groups route is automatically suitable: redesign the model to acknowledge machine-level dependence. Lesson. Method choice follows the scientific question and sampling structure, not which procedure yields the smallest p-value.

39. Final synthesis and self-explanation

To explain this topic without memorizing a list of test names, begin with the data-generating design. Ordinary one-way ANOVA models group means with a common error variance and independent errors. Residuals are differences from fitted group means; they reveal possible unequal spread, abnormal tails, outliers and time patterns, but only collection and randomization records can support independence and causal interpretation. A diagnostic p-value does not turn assumptions into facts.

A prespecified contrast makes one scientific comparison explicit through coefficients summing to zero. Its estimate, variance and t interval follow from the sampling variability of independent group means. The Tukey family instead asks about all pairs and uses a Studentized range threshold to protect simultaneous claims. Tukey–Kramer adjusts the pairwise scale for unequal sample sizes, not for genuinely unequal population variances. Welch ANOVA weights means by inverse estimated variance, and Games–Howell uses pair-specific variance estimates and approximate degrees of freedom for the all-pairs follow-up.

The two scenarios establish why interpretation must be conditional. Dataset I supports a particular planned contrast and only one of three Tukey pairwise differences. Dataset II has a significant Welch global test but no Games–Howell pairwise comparison passing 0.05. Neither result is contradictory, and neither licenses a claim of guaranteed future superiority or causation without suitable design. You should now be able to reproduce the arithmetic, justify each chosen procedure, and explain what a simultaneous interval does and does not cover.

Student Understanding Test. If you were encountering the topic for the first time, could you calculate every residual; explain why a contrast has coefficients summing to zero; distinguish t and Studentized-range scales; compute Welch's precision weights; reproduce one Games–Howell interval; and defend the chosen method to a colleague without quoting only a p-value? If any part remains unclear, return to the relevant worked calculation and redo it with the numbers hidden, then compare intermediate values rather than only the final verdict.
