01Why averages become tools for computation

A probability may be easy to define and difficult to calculate. A product can contain several components, a loss can depend on many uncertain inputs, or an event can involve a complicated rule. Monte Carlo simulation turns such a question into repeated experiments under a specified model. We compute an outcome in each experiment and average those outcomes. The Law of Large Numbers, LLN, explains why this can work, while a separate error calculation tells us how variable a finite simulation remains.

This guide develops both ideas rather than using “many repetitions” as an explanation. You will distinguish an individual outcome from a running average, prove a useful finite-variance version of the weak LLN, express a probability as an expectation, and construct a reproducible simulation. We will then budget precision, change assumptions, and write a report that separates computational accuracy from knowledge about the real world.

Our reference problem has an exact answer. That is a deliberate learning choice: we can check the simulation against a known target before transferring the method to harder problems. Agreement checks implementation and illustrates variability; it does not prove the LLN or validate a factory model. Every manufacturing number here is constructed for teaching, not obtained from a business dataset.

The prerequisites are expectation and variance from Guide 11, dependence from 13, and Bernoulli/Binomial models from 14. Necessary ideas are recalled locally. Calculus I is sufficient for the optional integration example; programming is optional for following the mathematical argument. Guide 28 will develop the Central Limit Theorem, CLT. We will quantify simulation error without quietly borrowing a Normal approximation from that later guide.

02Specify the modeled item and the event

A hypothetical item contains four components. Each component is defective with probability q=.10, and the four defect indicators within an item are mutually independent. Different simulated items are also independent under the ideal model. The two independence statements have different jobs: the first determines the item's defect-count distribution; the second controls the variability of the Monte Carlo average.

Let Dj equal 1 when component j is defective and 0 otherwise, for j=1,2,3,4. Let K=D1+D2+D3+D4 be the number of defective components in one item. Our event is A={K≥2}. Its probability p=P(A) concerns items with at least two defects, not the defect probability of one component. In general p and q are different numbers.

One simulation repetition generates one complete item. If R items are generated, the denominator for the event fraction is R. Generating four component values internally does not turn that item into four observations of A. In a physical study, component records and item records would need the same careful distinction before any analysis.

We hold q and the mechanism fixed while studying computational error. We do not estimate q from observations, inspect manufacturing records, or claim that actual components are independent. Later we change the mechanism to show how a precise answer can be precise for the wrong question. A useful first check is to explain in words what counts as one repetition and what qualifies that repetition as a hit.

03Separate an outcome, an average, and its target

Let Xr be a numerical output of repetition r. Its expectation μ=E[Xr] is the target of averaging when repetitions share a common law. The average after R repetitions is a random variable before the experiment: another run can produce different values of Xr and therefore a different average. A realized decimal is one estimate, not the fixed target itself.

X̄R=(X1+X2+⋯+XR)/R.

A concrete sequence makes the distinction visible. Consider the illustrative binary outcomes 0,1,0,0,1,0,0,0,1,0,0,0. These are a hand-constructed arithmetic example, not the first twelve outputs of our seeded manufacturing simulation. There are three hits in twelve repetitions, so the final average is 3/12=.25. The intermediate averages increase on some steps and decrease on others.

04Understand each update before discussing convergence

A running average can be updated without storing every previous result. The old sum is R times the old average. Add the next output, then divide by R+1. Rearranging reveals how the new result moves the average:

X̄R+1=(R X̄R+XR+1)/(R+1).X̄R+1−X̄R=(XR+1−X̄R)/(R+1).

For binary outputs, a new 1 raises an average below 1 and a new 0 lowers an average above 0. The step becomes smaller as R grows because the new observation receives weight 1/(R+1). Smaller updates help explain a smoother-looking trajectory, but they do not by themselves prove convergence to the correct target. An average of systematically wrong outputs can also change very slowly.

In the illustrative sequence, the first two averages are 0 and .5. Adding 0 gives 1/3. Relative to a hypothetical target .25, compare distances explicitly: |.5−.25|=.25 and |1/3−.25|≈.0833, so this particular step is closer. But from the fourth average .25, adding 1 gives .4, moving away. Choosing a favorable segment cannot establish a monotone rule.

The graph connects discrete checkpoints to make the updates legible; there are no fractional repetitions between integer positions. Its horizontal reference .25 is the final mean of this constructed sequence, not a population probability inferred from it. We use this small example to understand the arithmetic before asking a different question: how likely is a large average to miss a fixed model expectation by a chosen amount?

05Read the weak LLN with its quantifiers

A sufficient version we can prove uses independent and identically distributed variables, abbreviated iid, with finite mean μ and finite variance σ². Independent means their joint probabilities factor; identically distributed means they follow the same probability law. These are properties of the modeled sequence, not conclusions obtained by seeing a smooth average.

For every fixed tolerance ε>0, the weak LLN states:

P(|X̄R−μ|≥ε) → 0 as R→∞.

The expression inside the probability describes a bad approximation: the average is at least ε away from μ in either direction. First choose a positive tolerance in the same units as X. Then imagine repeating whole experiments of size R. The theorem says the fraction of such experiments that miss that tolerance becomes arbitrarily small as R increases. This is convergence in probability.

It does not state that the probability is already zero at some finite R, that each additional result improves the approximation, or that a new individual Xr becomes close to μ. Nor does it tell us a practical repetition budget without further information. “Large” must be judged relative to variability and the required accuracy, not a universal threshold such as thirty observations.

Finite variance is sufficient for our proof, rather than the weakest possible condition for any LLN. More general iid laws can be treated under finite absolute expectation. We keep the finite-variance version explicit because it supplies a direct error bound and applies to every indicator in our case. A simulation path illustrates its consequences; a mathematical argument must explain why the bad-event probability tends to zero.

06Derive the mean and variance of an average

Linearity of expectation allows us to average the expectations. It does not require independence. If each output has mean μ, then the mean of the average is μ. This unbiasedness concerns repeated experiments; it does not assert that one realized average equals μ.

E[X̄R]=(1/R)Σ E[Xr]=(Rμ)/R=μ.

Variance needs more care because two outputs can move together. Under independence, covariance terms vanish and the variance of the sum is the sum of variances. Multiplying a random quantity by 1/R multiplies its variance by 1/R², not 1/R. Combining those two facts gives:

Var(X̄R)=(1/R²)Σ Var(Xr)=Rσ²/R²=σ²/R.SD(X̄R)=σ/√R.

The factor 1/R² is the step at which a common denominator error occurs. A sum of R independent outputs becomes more variable in absolute units, with variance Rσ². The average divides that sum by R, producing the opposing squared scaling. There is no contradiction between a growing total variance and a shrinking average variance because they are different random quantities.

Without independence the exact variance includes covariance:

Var(X̄R)=[Σ Var(Xr)+2Σr<s Cov(Xr,Xs)]/R².

Positive covariance can prevent the simple 1/R reduction. Independence is stronger than merely zero pairwise covariance, but it is a transparent sufficient assumption for the iid simulation used here. We will later construct copied outputs where the covariance terms remain large enough to destroy the claimed improvement. First, the derived variance will let us turn a typical scale of fluctuation into a probability bound.

07Prove a useful LLN using Chebyshev’s inequality

We can justify the needed inequality without assuming an advanced theorem. Let Y have mean m and finite variance v. On the event |Y−m|≥ε, its squared deviation is at least ε². Outside that event the squared deviation is still nonnegative. Therefore its expectation must be at least ε² times the probability of the event.

E[(Y−m)²] ≥ ε² P(|Y−m|≥ε).P(|Y−m|≥ε) ≤ v/ε².

This is Chebyshev's inequality. Substitute Y=X̄R, m=μ, and v=σ²/R from the previous section. For every fixed ε>0 the upper bound tends to zero as R grows, and a probability cannot be negative. Hence the bad-event probability tends to zero, proving our finite-variance weak LLN.

P(|X̄R−μ|≥ε) ≤ min{1, σ²/(Rε²)}.

The minimum with 1 is a useful reporting convention: a bound larger than 1 is valid but says nothing beyond the fact that a probability is at most 1. We have derived a bound, not the exact probability of a simulation error. It can be conservative because it uses only variance rather than the whole distribution.

Notice what the proof accomplished and what it did not. It establishes convergence for the stated ideal iid model, without a Normal approximation or a favorable graph. It gives a finite-R guarantee if σ² is known or bounded. It does not measure the realized error of an unknown target, validate independence, or guarantee that a finite sequence never moves away from its mean. Those are different questions requiring different evidence.

08Distinguish a limiting statement from a finite certificate

The strong LLN describes entire infinite trajectories: under iid sampling with E[|X|]<∞, the averages converge to μ with probability 1. This is almost sure convergence. It is a cited result, not a consequence we have proved merely by drawing a smooth line or applying Chebyshev separately at each R. General proof requires additional probability theory.

The weak statement controls the probability of a bad approximation at each sufficiently large fixed R. The strong statement says that, outside a set of trajectories having probability zero, the sequence itself eventually stays within every chosen positive tolerance. Neither promises that absolute error decreases on every update. A convergent numerical sequence can approach its limit while oscillating around it.

For computation, a limiting statement and a finite budget answer different questions. Knowing convergence as R tends to infinity does not tell us that today's R is enough for a required decision. A useful report therefore states both the assumptions that justify averaging and a scale or bound for error at the actual repetition count.

Finite variance implies finite absolute expectation, so our bounded indicators satisfy the conditions discussed here. The general LLN is not restricted to Bernoulli or Normal variables, and it does not require an MGF. Conversely, if an output lacks a finite mean, averaging cannot be justified by simply invoking this theorem. If variance is infinite, the particular variance-based error formulas we derive cannot be used even when some broader LLN applies. Existence conditions belong to the operation being justified.

09Turn the probability into an expectation

For one modeled item, define I=1 if K≥2 and I=0 otherwise. I is an indicator: it records whether the event occurred without retaining how far beyond the threshold the count went. Counts 2,3,and 4 all produce I=1. A count 1 produces I=0, despite containing a defective component.

E[I]=1·p+0·(1−p)=p.E[I²]=p, because I²=I.Var(I)=p−p²=p(1−p).

This identity is the bridge from a probability question to averaging. Generate R complete items, compute one indicator Ir for each, and average them. The estimator p̂R is the fraction of event hits. It is random before the run and a fixed reported number after the generated outcomes are known.

p̂R=(I1+⋯+IR)/R=hits/R.

Under independent repetitions, E[p̂R]=p and Var(p̂R)=p(1−p)/R. The finite-variance LLN applies automatically because indicators are bounded. The theorem supports consistency: the chance of a fixed-size error tends to zero. Unbiasedness and consistency are different properties, although this estimator has both under the stated model.

The indicator must match the event precisely. Replacing K≥2 with K>2 estimates a smaller probability; averaging K estimates the expected number of defective components, not an event probability. These errors can produce stable, reproducible calculations of the wrong quantity. Before running a simulation, test the event rule on several possible counts and explain the resulting numerator and denominator.

10Calculate the exact benchmark in two ways

Within one item, four mutually independent Bernoulli(.10) indicators give K∼Binomial(4,.10). There are four possible locations for exactly one defect, six for exactly two, four for three, and one for all four. The Binomial coefficient counts those configurations; independence gives the probability of each configuration.

P(K=k)=C(4,k)(.1)k(.9)4−k, k=0,1,2,3,4.

For at least two defects, the complement contains only 0 and 1. No defects has probability .9⁴=.6561. Exactly one has probability 4(.1)(.9³)=.2916. Therefore:

p=1−P(K=0)−P(K=1)=1−.6561−.2916=.0523.

When an exact calculation is inexpensive, use it; this example teaches the simulation method rather than claiming simulation is the most efficient way to solve a five-term Binomial sum.

11Build the random experiment one layer at a time

An ideal Uniform(0,1) variable U assigns probability equal to interval length. Thus P(U<.10)=.10. Defining D=1 when U<.10 and 0 otherwise produces a Bernoulli(.10) indicator. The numerical threshold implements the component probability; it is not learned from generated values.

For each item, generate four separate Uniform values, compare each with .10, and add the four resulting indicators to obtain K. Then apply K≥2 once to obtain the item indicator I. Repeat the whole construction R times. Reusing the same U for all four components would instead make their defects perfectly dependent, a different item model.

A transparent diagnostic item uses the illustrative values .04,.63,.08,.51. Thresholding produces 1,0,1,0, so K=2 and I=1. The values .25,.31,.72,.09 produce 0,0,0,1, hence K=1 and I=0. These two hand-selected examples check the event rule; they are not claimed to be a seeded stream or representative data.

The mathematical model assumes ideal independent draws. Software uses a deterministic pseudo-random generator designed to imitate relevant distributional properties. The LLN theorem applies to the ideal model; reproducible software is an implementation that must be checked, not a literal infinite sequence of physically random independent values. This distinction does not make simulation useless. It tells us to document the generator, avoid accidental state resets, and verify that the implemented experiment matches the intended model before interpreting its average.

12Use a seed for reproducibility, not accuracy

A Random Seed initializes a pseudo-random stream. With the same generator, seed, and sequence of calls, a program can reproduce the same outputs. It is a control over computation, not evidence that its final estimate is unusually accurate. A different valid seed typically produces another trajectory around the same target.

Our main stream uses Python random.Random(2401) and four random() calls per item. Construct the generator once outside the item loop. Recreating Random(2401) inside the loop restarts the stream, repeating the same four values on every item. The resulting event average could be 0 or 1 forever while looking perfectly reproducible. Repeatability alone cannot detect this design error.

Checkpoint estimates at R=100,1000,10000,and 100000 share earlier items. They are nested averages from one stream, not four independent runs. To study run-to-run variation, generate fresh complete runs instead. We use a documented list of different seeds for that diagnostic; assigning different integer seeds is a practical separate-run convention, not a mathematical proof of independent streams or a general parallel-generation strategy.

Record software version and generator as well as seed. A switch to NumPy or a different algorithm may change the numerical path even if the probability model remains the same. Never search seeds until finding the closest answer and present that result as a typical run. Such selection hides variability precisely when the reader needs to understand it. Fix the reporting design in advance and show all prespecified checkpoints or runs.

13Run a complete, inspectable implementation

The following optional script uses only the Python standard library. It defines the analytical benchmark, generates the component counts, records item hits, and prints four checkpoints with Monte Carlo error summaries. Before executing, predict that a fraction of items will qualify, that a larger budget usually stabilizes the average, and that the final decimal need not equal .0523 exactly.

Read the inner and outer operations separately. The inner sum counts four Boolean defect comparisons; Python treats True as 1 and False as 0 in this sum. The outer counter adds one Boolean event result per item. The event denominator is therefore itemnumber. A condition on that number prints results but does not reset the stream or the accumulated hits.

from random import Random
from math import comb, fsum, isfinite, sqrt

def simulate_items(seed, repetitions, component_probability=0.10):
    if not isinstance(repetitions, int) or repetitions < 2:
        raise ValueError("at least two integer repetitions required")
    if not isfinite(component_probability) or not 0 <= component_probability <= 1:
        raise ValueError("component probability must be in [0, 1]")
    item_rng = Random(seed)
    indicators = []
    for item_number in range(repetitions):
        defect_count = sum(item_rng.random() < component_probability for j in range(4))
        indicators.append(int(defect_count >= 2))
    return indicators

q = 0.10
exact_probability = fsum(comb(4, k)*q**k*(1-q)**(4-k) for k in range(2, 5))
assert abs(exact_probability-0.0523) < 1e-14
indicators = simulate_items(2401, 100_000, q)
checkpoints = {100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000}
hits = 0
print("R hits estimate sample_MCSE exact_MCSE signed_error")
for item_number, indicator in enumerate(indicators, 1):
    hits += indicator
    if item_number in checkpoints:
        estimate = hits/item_number
        sample_mcse = sqrt(estimate*(1-estimate)/(item_number-1))
        exact_mcse = sqrt(exact_probability*(1-exact_probability)/item_number)
        signed_error = estimate-exact_probability
        print(f"{item_number} {hits} {estimate:.5f} {sample_mcse:.8f} "
              f"{exact_mcse:.8f} {signed_error:+.5f}")
assert len(indicators) == 100_000
assert all(value in (0, 1) for value in indicators)

15Distinguish Monte Carlo standard error from actual error

Monte Carlo Standard Error, MCSE, is the standard deviation of an estimator across hypothetical repeated simulations of the same size under the same model. For independent item indicators, the variance formula already derived gives an exact expression. With p=.0523, the indicator variance is .0523(.9477)=.04956471.

MCSE(p̂R)=√[p(1−p)/R].At R=10000: √(.04956471/10000)≈.00222631.

The individual indicator SD is√.04956471≈.222631. The average's MCSE at 10000 is one hundred times smaller, because√10000=100. Generating more items reduces uncertainty in the computed average; it does not reduce the variability of an individual item under the fixed model.

MCSE is not the realized absolute error |p̂R−p|. One run can lie very close to p even when its MCSE is relatively large, or farther away when its MCSE is smaller. A standard deviation is a repeated-experiment scale, not an upper bound on each realization. The exact MCSE is nonrandom when p and R are fixed, whereas a sample-based MCSE changes with the generated indicators.

Nor does “estimate plus or minus two MCSE” automatically define a justified 95% interval. That interpretation requires an appropriate distributional argument and can fail near boundaries or with very few hits. We defer Normal approximation and confidence-interval construction to later guides. Here MCSE supplies an exact variance scale under independence, and Chebyshev supplies a separate finite-sample probability bound without assuming a Normal shape.

16Derive the sample-based MCSE and its denominator

Usually p is unknown, so the exact indicator variance cannot be inserted. Estimate the output variance from the simulated indicators. For R≥2, define their sample variance with divisor R−1 and divide that variance by R to estimate the variance of the average. The estimated MCSE is its square root.

sI²=Σ(Ir−p̂R)²/(R−1).Estimated MCSE=√(sI²/R).

For binary outputs, the squared deviations simplify. If H hits occur, there are H ones and R−H zeros. The numerator is H(1−p̂)²+(R−H) p̂². Substitute H=R p̂, expand, and combine to obtain R p̂(1−p̂). Therefore:

sI²=R p̂(1−p̂)/(R−1).Estimated MCSE=√[p̂(1−p̂)/(R−1)].

A common plug-in convention instead uses√[p̂(1−p̂)/R]. Both approach the same value for large R, but they are not exactly identical. Our printed column uses the sample-variance version, and the code names that convention. The R−1 arises from estimating variance from data, not from having one fewer simulated item or changing the event denominator.

The sample variance estimates variance without bias under iid sampling; taking its square root does not make the MCSE estimate itself exactly unbiased. More importantly, zero observed hits makes both estimated formulas zero even when the true p is positive. This is a warning about sparse events, not evidence of perfect accuracy. At R=1 the sample-variance formula is undefined and must not be silently evaluated as zero.

17Plan computational effort using the square-root rule

For a fixed iid output distribution, MCSE is proportional to 1/√R. Multiplying R by 4 divides MCSE by 2; multiplying R by 100 divides it by 10. Doubling repetitions reduces MCSE by a factor 1/√2≈.7071, rather than cutting it in half. This diminishing return matters when each repetition is expensive.

A pilot estimate can guide planning but is not a guaranteed upper bound on p(1−p), especially after few hits. State whether a budget uses a known model value, a universal bound, or a preliminary estimate; these choices provide different levels of assurance.

18Choose a tolerance and a probability of missing it

A guaranteed probability bound needs two numbers: an error tolerance ε and an acceptable bound δ on the chance of missing it. Both must be chosen before substituting in Chebyshev. A small MCSE target alone does not specify δ. For independent indicators, the inequality gives:

P(|p̂R−p|≥ε) ≤ p(1−p)/(Rε²).To make the bound at most δ: R≥p(1−p)/(δε²).

Suppose we want absolute error below .005 with probability at least .95 under the ideal fixed model. Here ε=.005 and δ=.05. Use p=.0523: R≥.04956471/[.05(.005)²]=39651.768, so R=39652 suffices. This is a conservative finite-sample guarantee, not a claim about the observed error in every run.

Without knowing p, use .25 instead of .04956471. The corresponding budget is .25/[.05(.005)²]=200000. It is larger because it protects all Bernoulli probabilities rather than our particular target. No CLT approximation or multiplier 1.96 has been used.

At R=10000, the model-specific Chebyshev bound for ε=.005 is .19825884; at 100000 it is .019825884. These are upper bounds, not exact miss probabilities. If p is known, the actual distribution of hits is Binomial(R,p), and an exact tail sum can be smaller. We use the bound because it exposes assumptions and scaling transparently. When p is unknown, replacing it by a data-dependent p̂ in Chebyshev does not automatically preserve the stated guarantee.

19Understand relative error and the zero-hit trap

Absolute precision and relative precision are different goals. An MCSE of .001 is small in probability units but enormous relative to a probability of .000001. For independent indicators with 0<p<1, divide MCSE by p to get the relative Monte Carlo scale:

MCSE/p=√[(1−p)/(Rp)].

For a relative scale a, the budget is R≥(1−p)/(p a²). When p is small, it is roughly 1/(p a²). The expected number of hits Rp helps explain the difficulty: a large total R may still produce very little event information if p is extremely small. Our p=.0523 is not an extreme rare-event example, but the warning transfers to operational tail risks.

Consider a separate stipulated event with p=.000001 and R=100000. Expected hits are .1, and the probability of zero hits is(1−p)R≈.904837. A zero estimate is therefore common under a positive probability. A plug-in MCSE of zero would miss the uncertainty because no variation appeared in that finite sample.

The remedy is not to announce that the event is impossible or divide by the zero estimate to report relative error. Reconsider the repetition budget, use a suitable probability bound or later interval method, and investigate specialized methods if the event is too rare for direct simulation. Importance Sampling and other variance-reduction techniques require additional derivations and are outside this guide. The essential lesson is to plan precision relative to the event and to distinguish “not observed” from “probability zero.”

21Changing component dependence changes the probability

Suppose each item's four defect indicators are copies of one Bernoulli(.10) variable D. Every component still has marginal defect probability .10. However, now either all four are defective or none are, so K=4 D. The event K≥2 is exactly D=1, and its probability is .10, not .0523.

Independent components: P(K≥2)=.0523.Copied components: K=4 D, P(K≥2)=P(D=1)=.10.

The marginal inputs have not changed, but the joint mechanism has. A simulation using one Uniform value per item and reusing it for four comparisons implements the copied model. If different items are generated independently, its event average can converge correctly to .10. More repetitions will strengthen that convergence rather than repair the mismatch with the independent-component target.

This example separates model error from Monte Carlo error. If the intended mechanism has independent components but the program accidentally copies them, comparing with .0523 detects a structural implementation error. If a real factory genuinely has common-cause defects, the independent model may be the wrong scientific description. In either situation, a tiny MCSE around the wrong target is not a successful solution to the original question.

Do not infer the probability of two or more defects from component marginal rates alone. The dependence structure matters. The same issue arises when common environments, shared suppliers, or duplicated measurements make outputs related, although our constructed example is an extreme mathematical illustration rather than evidence about any such industry. State the joint assumption before applying a Binomial formula.

22Copying repetitions destroys the iid error calculation

Now retain the intended item-event probability p=.0523, but generate one item indicator I and copy it R times. Each stored output individually has the correct Bernoulli(p) law. Their average is nevertheless I itself. Its variance is p(1−p), no matter how large the displayed R becomes.

I1=I2=⋯=IR=I.p̂R=I; Var(p̂R)=p(1−p).

Every covariance between distinct copies equals Var(I). Substituting those terms into the general variance-of-an-average formula gives R² Var(I)/R²=Var(I). The apparent sample size counts copies, not independent information. The naive MCSE√[p(1−p)/R] would incorrectly shrink toward zero.

A less extreme calculation groups outputs into independent clusters of m identical copies. If there are B independent underlying indicators and R=Bm stored outputs, their overall average equals the average of those B indicators. Its MCSE is√[p(1−p)/B], a factor√m larger than the naive formula based on R. This exact construction illustrates dependence; it is not a universal effective-sample-size formula for every correlated process.

Real sequences with temporal or spatial dependence may satisfy other laws and require other variance estimators. We do not claim that independence is necessary for all averaging results. We claim that it is part of the sufficient argument and MCSE formula used here. When it fails, return to the joint mechanism rather than insert a large row count into an iid denominator. Time-series methods will develop more general dependence later in the curriculum.

23More simulation cannot repair a biased target

The LLN links an average to the expectation of the distribution actually sampled. It does not know which population a researcher hoped to describe. If observations come only from a selected subgroup, a large average can estimate that subgroup's mean accurately while missing the intended population mean.

For a simple constructed example, a population consists equally of two groups with event probabilities .02 and .10. Its overall event probability is .5(.02)+.5(.10)=.06. Sampling only the second group and averaging independent event indicators converges to .10. Relative to the population target .06, the limiting discrepancy is .04; increasing repetitions does not eliminate it.

This is why “large n” cannot substitute for representativeness. The issue is the mechanism that selects observations, not a failure of arithmetic or of the LLN. Random Sampling and sampling design, introduced in Guide 04, determine what population an observational average can support. Random Assignment concerns intervention comparisons and is a different design concept; simulation independence does not establish either design in an actual study.

The same reasoning applies to a program that implements the wrong event or parameter. It can have small Monte Carlo variance and converge to the expectation of its implemented rule. To audit a simulation, ask two questions in order: are we sampling and scoring the correct model target, and how much computational error remains in estimating that target? Reversing this order can spend substantial computation making a scientifically irrelevant number look increasingly precise.

24Separate numerical error, parameter uncertainty, and model error

Our q=.10 is stipulated. If a factory instead estimates q from observed components, that estimate has statistical uncertainty governed by the data and their design. Conditional on one chosen q, simulation adds numerical error while approximating p(q). Increasing R reduces that conditional simulation error; it does not add new component observations or make the chosen q certain.

The function p(q)=1−(1−q)4−4 q(1−q)3 describes the independent-component model. Evaluate it at q=.08,.10,and .12 to inspect sensitivity. These are hypothetical scenarios, not a confidence interval for q and not posterior draws. They show how a model conclusion changes when an input changes.

25Extend the method from probabilities to numerical losses

A probability is only one kind of expectation. Suppose the modeled item incurs a fixed illustrative charge of 50 monetary units when K≥2 and zero otherwise. Its loss output is L=50 I. By linearity E[L]=50 p=2.615 monetary units per item. Scaling variance gives Var(L)=50² p(1−p)=123.911775 monetary units squared.

A simulation estimates expected loss by averaging Lr across items. Its MCSE is 50 times the event-probability MCSE, because standard deviation scales with the absolute multiplier. At R=10000, the theoretical MCSE is approximately .111316 monetary units. Increasing R improves the computed expected-loss estimate; it does not make the actual cost of an individual item equal to 2.615.

This is a constructed decision rule, not a measured repair-cost model. Random repair durations, severity-dependent costs, or shared production shocks would require explicitly defined additional random inputs and an updated output variance. Multiplying an event probability by a fixed charge is valid here because the charge is stipulated as deterministic when the event occurs.

More generally, generate outputs Yr=g(Xr) from the specified input model and estimate θ=E[g(X)] by their average. If the Yr are iid with finite variance τ², their average has MCSE τ/√R and satisfies the finite-variance LLN. Defining the output g matters as much as defining the input distribution. A different cost rule changes the target, its units, and its variance even if the same simulated component values are reused.

26Use Calculus I to verify a Monte Carlo integral

A continuous example shows that simulation need not estimate a probability. Let U be ideal Uniform(0,1), with density 1 on that interval, and define Y=U². Its expectation is the integral of u² weighted by that density. Calculus I provides an exact benchmark:

E[Y]=∫01 u² du=[u³/3]01=1/3.E[Y²]=E[U⁴]=∫01 u⁴ du=1/5.Var(Y)=1/5−(1/3)²=4/45.

Generate R independent Uniform values, square them, and average. The output is bounded, so our LLN applies. Its exact MCSE is√[4/(45 R)], about .00298142 at R=10000. Using the indicator formula p(1−p) here would be wrong: Y is continuous on[0,1], not a binary event indicator, even though its mean also lies between 0 and 1.

from random import Random
from math import fsum, sqrt

integral_rng = Random(2410)
repetitions = 10_000
squared_outputs = [integral_rng.random()**2 for r in range(repetitions)]
integral_estimate = fsum(squared_outputs)/repetitions
sample_variance = fsum((value-integral_estimate)**2 for value in squared_outputs)/(repetitions-1)
sample_mcse = sqrt(sample_variance/repetitions)
print(f"Integral estimate: {integral_estimate:.8f}")
print(f"Exact benchmark: {1/3:.8f}")
print(f"Sample MCSE: {sample_mcse:.8f}")
print(f"Exact MCSE: {sqrt(4/(45*repetitions)):.8f}")

27Fix the reporting rule before inspecting results

Choose the model, event, precision goal, generator, seed, and repetition budget before inspecting the final estimate. This makes the reported numerical experiment reviewable. It also separates a planned diagnostic from an unrecorded search for a reassuring answer. Document any later change and explain which question it addresses.

A preliminary run can estimate output variability and help budget a larger run. However, a sample-based MCSE is itself estimated, and an unusually small pilot variance can lead to an overoptimistic budget. For indicators, the universal variance bound .25 is an alternative when a conservative guarantee is more important than minimizing work. Known analytical results should be used for verification rather than concealed to make the simulation seem more sophisticated.

Stopping when the estimate first looks close to an answer, or first crosses a desired threshold, changes the reporting procedure. Our fixed-R calculations do not automatically provide the same probability guarantees at such a data-dependent stopping time. Sequential guarantees require their own reasoning. Here the practical choice is to prescribe a finite budget and report all checkpoints, using them to explain behavior rather than select a favorable endpoint.

For reproducibility, keep input assumptions, output definition, number of independent units, code, software version, generator and seed together. Retaining only the final decimal loses the information needed to decide whether the estimate refers to the correct target. A future laboratory can extend these checks to measured data, but the study guide's simulated items should never be relabeled as observations merely because the code executed successfully.

28Communicate a conditional numerical result precisely

A complete Monte Carlo report begins with the quantity being approximated. Here it is P(K≥2) for four independent Bernoulli(.10) component defects per item. Then state the independent item repetitions, budget, pseudo-random implementation, and seed. Report the estimate alongside its estimated MCSE and name the variance convention. If an exact benchmark is available, show the discrepancy separately.

At R=100000, seed 2401 produced 5170 event hits: estimate 0.05170, sample MCSE 0.00070020, exact MCSE 0.00070402, and signed error -0.00060.

The reported MCSE describes repeated-run numerical variability under the fixed model. It does not describe uncertainty about a real factory's component probability or certify the independence assumptions. A discrepancy smaller than one MCSE is possible; one larger than a few MCSE prompts investigation but is not logically impossible. Check the implementation, arithmetic, generator use, and model before claiming either success or failure from one number.

Avoid reporting more meaningful digits than the numerical precision supports. Extra displayed decimals can help reproduce calculations, but they do not make all those digits scientifically established. Probabilities, percentage points, counts, and monetary amounts should remain clearly distinguished. An exact p=.0523 in our constructed model is different from a simulated estimate printed to five decimal places.

A useful conclusion states what the calculation supports and what would be needed for application: validate the physical model and parameters with suitable data, set a decision-relevant tolerance, and quantify remaining sources of uncertainty. Simulation can inform planning under assumptions, but it does not determine acceptable risk or establish a causal effect of a manufacturing intervention. The report should remain as careful as the derivation that produced it.

29Practice construction, error analysis, and model auditing

Attempt each problem before opening its solution. State the output, target, independent unit and assumptions before substituting numbers. A correct decimal attached to the wrong event or denominator is not a correct solution. The activities include derivation, reproduction, changed parameters, dependence and a final report audit.

When checking an answer, ask whether the method estimates a probability or a numerical expectation, whether the error quantity is a realized discrepancy or a repeated-run scale, and whether the stated guarantee uses a known variance or a bound. The solutions retain those distinctions through the last activity. They are part of the teaching, rather than an answer key that only lists final values.

A. Derive a probability bound for an average

Iid outputs have mean 12 and variance 9. Derive the mean and variance of their average at R=900. Bound the probability of absolute error at least .5 and explain what the bound does not say.

Complete worked solution

Linearity gives E[X̄]=900(12)/900=12. Independence makes the sum variance 900(9), and division by 900 scales variance by 1/900². Hence Var(X̄)=9/900=.01 and its SD is .1. The individual output SD remains 3; the SD .1 belongs to averages of 900 outputs.

Apply Chebyshev with ε=.5: P(|X̄−12|≥.5)≤.01/.5²=.04. Thus the chance of error strictly below .5 is at least .96 under the stated model. The units of .5 must match those of X. The bound is finite-sample and uses no Normal approximation.

It does not say that the realized error is .04 or that every run misses by at most .5. Nor does it promise monotone improvement along a trajectory. Increasing R with ε fixed makes 9/(Rε²) tend to zero, which proves the finite-variance weak-LLN conclusion. The crucial assumption was used in summing variances, not in the expectation calculation.

B. Construct the item event and verify its target

For four independent components with q=.10, find P(K≥2) by complement and direct summation. What do averaging K and dividing item hits by 4 R estimate instead?

Complete worked solution

The zero-defect mass is .9⁴=.6561 and the one-defect mass is 4(.1)(.9³)=.2916. The complement gives p=1−.6561−.2916=.0523. Direct summation gives 6(.1²)(.9²)+4(.1³)(.9)+.1⁴=.0486+.0036+.0001=.0523. Both calculations refer to the same complete item and its threshold event.

Averaging K estimates E[K]=4(.1)=.4 defects per item. It retains count magnitude instead of merely recording the event. Dividing the number of item-event hits by 4 R estimates p/4=.013075 under independent repetitions, because each hit is still an item hit while the denominator has been multiplied by four. It does not become an estimate of q=.10.

To estimate the component defect rate, count all defective components and divide by 4 R under the appropriate component model. To estimate the item event, count one indicator per item and divide by R. Checking both numerator meaning and independent unit is necessary even when every generated row has the expected format.

C. Audit a hypothetical run without confusing error quantities

A hypothetical R=10000 run has 540 item hits. Compute its estimate, signed error against .0523, exact MCSE and sample-based MCSE. Is the error equal to either MCSE?

Complete worked solution

The estimate is 540/10000=.054. Its signed discrepancy from the exact target is .054−.0523=.0017, or .17 percentage point. The absolute discrepancy is also .0017 in this case because the sign is positive. These totals are supplied exercise inputs; check the arithmetic independently of the seeded program.

The model-based MCSE is√[.0523(.9477)/10000]≈.00222631. With the sample-variance convention, estimated MCSE is√[.054(.946)/9999]≈.00226029. The estimate is about .764 exact MCSE above the target, a description of one realized discrepancy in units of a theoretical repeated-run scale.

Neither MCSE equals the observed error, and neither is a guarantee bounding it. The estimated MCSE differs from the exact value because it uses simulated p̂ and the sample-variance correction. Reporting these distinctions matters even when the numerical values look similar. Without the exact target, the signed-error calculation would not be available from the estimate alone.

D. Budget scale and probability guarantees separately

For p=.0523, find R for MCSE≤.001 and for P(|p̂−p|≥.005)≤.05 using Chebyshev. Repeat the latter without knowing p. Explain why these are different goals.

Complete worked solution

For the MCSE scale target, square√[.04956471/R]≤.001 to get R≥49564.71. Round upward to 49565. This constrains the theoretical SD of estimates, not every realized error.

For the probability bound, Chebyshev requires .04956471/[R(.005)²]≤.05. Rearranging gives R≥39651.768 and hence 39652. The tolerance here is .005, five times the MCSE target in the first task, so the smaller budget is not a contradiction. These are different definitions of precision, with different numerical inputs.

If p is unknown, p(1−p)≤.25 gives R≥.25/[.05(.005)²]=200000 for the same bound. Substituting a pilot p̂ instead would not automatically retain this universal guarantee. All budgets assume independent item indicators under a fixed model; none establishes whether the physical factory follows that model. A precision statement should identify its tolerance, probability requirement or scale criterion, and the variance information used.

E. Transfer the event calculation to another parameter

Change q from .10 to .20, retaining independent components and items. Find the new target and its exact MCSE at R=10000. Describe the necessary code change and a verification.

Complete worked solution

The complement becomes 1−.8⁴−4(.2)(.8³)=1−.4096−.4096=.1808. Direct masses for 2,3,and 4 defects are .1536,.0256,and .0016, which sum to .1808. The event remains K≥2; only the model's component probability changed.

The new indicator variance is .1808(.8192)=.14811136, and the exact MCSE at 10000 is√(.14811136/10000)≈.00384852. A larger event probability does not mean smaller absolute simulation error: Bernoulli variance increases as p moves from near zero toward .5. Precision must be recalculated when the output law changes.

In the function call, set componentprobability=.20 while retaining four separate draws per item and the event rule. Update the analytical benchmark as well; comparing the modified estimate with the old .0523 would manufacture an apparent failure. Reusing a seed can help reproduce the new experiment, but the resulting path need not have the same number of hits or the same realized error as the old model.

F. Distinguish the two dependence failures

Compare reusing one Uniform value for all four components within each independent item with copying one entire item indicator across all R items. What target or MCSE claim changes in each case?

Complete worked solution

In the first construction, each item has either four defects or zero. Its event probability is .10, although each component still has marginal probability .10. If new items use independent underlying draws, the item fraction converges to .10 and its MCSE is√[.1(.9)/R]. The within-item change alters the target from .0523 to .10.

In the second construction, start with one correctly generated event indicator I having probability .0523, then copy it R times. Each marginal indicator has the desired law, but the average remains I. Its variance is .0523(.9477), not that value divided by R. The across-item change destroys the iid error calculation and convergence-to-p claim for this average.

Both examples involve reuse, but at different levels. One changes the distribution of the modeled item; the other changes how many independent item outcomes inform the average. An audit must name the independent unit at each level. Merely checking marginal defect frequencies, row count or reproducibility cannot distinguish these mechanisms adequately.

G. Count independent information in copied clusters

You store 10000 item indicators, arranged as 1000 independent underlying indicators each copied 10 times. Find the correct MCSE for p=.0523 and compare with the naive formula.

Complete worked solution

Let Jb be the independent indicator in cluster b. The sum of the stored outputs is 10ΣJb, so dividing by 10000 givesΣJb/1000. The estimate is exactly the average of 1000 independent indicators. Its variance is .04956471/1000 and its MCSE is approximately .00704022.

Using all 10000 stored rows as independent would report√(.04956471/10000)≈.00222631. The ratio of correct to naive MCSE is√10≈3.16228. Duplicating each value increased storage but did not create new independent information.

This derivation depends on exact identical copies within equal-size independent clusters. It is not a general recipe for replacing sample size in any dependent dataset. For unequal clusters or partial correlation, return to the actual covariance or sampling structure. The important skill is to derive the estimator from independent units before assigning it an error formula, rather than accepting the row count as the precision denominator.

H. Verify a continuous expectation and its units

With independent Uniform(0,1) draws, estimate the integral of u² from 0 to 1. Derive the exact output variance and MCSE at 10000. What multiplier is needed on a general interval [a,b]?

Complete worked solution

Use Y=U² and estimate the integral byΣUr²/R. Since the Uniform density is 1, E[Y]=∫u² du=1/3. The second output moment is E[Y²]=E[U⁴]=1/5, so variance is 1/5−1/9=4/45. The MCSE at 10000 is√[4/(45·10000)]≈.00298142.

For U Uniform(a,b), the density is 1/(b−a), so E[g(U)] equals the desired integral divided by b−a. Multiply the simulated mean by b−a to estimate the integral. Its MCSE also scales by |b−a|. If the integration variable or output carries physical units, retain the corresponding interval-width units in the result.

Do not insert mean 1/3 into a Bernoulli variance formula: a squared Uniform output has many possible values between 0 and 1, rather than only endpoints. Deriving E[Y²] checks both output definition and precision. The availability of an antiderivative supplies a reference for realized error; it does not force a finite Monte Carlo result to equal the exact integral.

I. Diagnose a zero estimate for a rare event

A stipulated probability is 10⁻⁶ and R=100000 independent indicators produce zero hits. Compute expected hits and zero-hit probability. Explain why estimated MCSE zero does not certify the result.

Complete worked solution

Expected hits are Rp=100000(.000001)=.1. Under independence, all indicators are zero with probability(1−.000001)100000≈.904837. Thus zero hits are unsurprising even though the true probability is positive. The fixed model remains the source of these calculations; zero observations do not change its stipulated p.

The realized estimate is 0. Both plug-in variance and sample indicator variance are zero because all recorded values agree. But the theoretical MCSE is√[.000001(.999999)/100000], approximately .00000316228, larger than the target probability. Its relative scale is about 3.16228, indicating poor relative precision.

A zero estimate therefore cannot justify “impossible event” or “zero numerical error.” Plan a substantially larger budget or use another justified rare-event method and appropriate uncertainty analysis. Do not divide by the zero estimate to claim a relative error. The example shows why sufficient event information, not total row count alone, must guide a precision discussion.

J. Rewrite an operational report with all assumptions intact

A report claims: “A fixed seed proves accuracy. Every checkpoint is an independent experiment. LLN makes error decrease at every step. Repeating a model 100000 times validates a factory probability. Zero MCSE proves no error, and any estimate ±2 MCSE is a 95% interval.” Diagnose the claims and replace the report.

Complete worked solution

A seed supports reproducibility of a specified stream, not its accuracy. Checkpoints from that stream reuse earlier items and are dependent. LLN concerns convergence under conditions, not monotone absolute error. The 100000 items are simulated outputs, not newly observed factory evidence. Estimated zero MCSE can result from no observed rare events. A 95% interval requires an appropriate distributional or coverage argument, not a universal two-standard-error label.

A defensible replacement begins: “Under the constructed model of four independent components with q=.10 per item and independent item repetitions, we estimated P(K≥2) using R=100000, Python Random and seed 2401. The final checkpoint produced 5170 hits, an estimate of .05170 and sample-based MCSE .00070020. The exact model benchmark is .0523; the displayed discrepancy checks this computation. MCSE measures variation between repeated simulations conditional on the model.” These are the prescribed main-run results; a closer selected run would conceal variability.

Conclude: “The result does not validate q or independence in a physical factory. Applying it requires suitable observations and a check of model structure. More R reduces conditional numerical fluctuation, while parameter uncertainty and model inadequacy need separate analysis.” This correction connects target, independent unit, mechanism, computational budget, error interpretation and scientific limits. It changes the reasoning, not merely the wording of a final percentage.

30Carry the reasoning into the next statistical questions

The computational argument has a specific chain. Define the modeled random experiment and an output whose expectation is the desired quantity. Generate suitably independent repetitions of that output, average them, justify convergence under stated conditions, and quantify the remaining fluctuation at the actual repetition budget. Then verify the implementation through an analytical answer, a different calculation, or an independently designed check where possible.

For the item event, this chain connected component indicators to K, K to the event indicator I, and E[I] to p. The LLN explained convergence of the fraction; variance explained its MCSE; Chebyshev connected variance to a finite probability bound. None of these steps made the components independent in an actual factory or estimated their real defect rate. The dependence examples showed two distinct failures: changing the item model changes the target, while copying repetitions prevents the claimed variance reduction.

The next guides study distributions across samples, estimation and Standard Error in greater depth. Guide 28 addresses the shape of standardized fluctuations through the CLT. LLN concerns the average approaching its target; CLT concerns a suitably rescaled error's limiting distribution. These are connected but different questions. Do not infer a Normal shape merely from stabilization.

For practical transfer, design a small study that specifies an operational event, a model, an exact or independently checkable benchmark, and a numerical tolerance. Explain what real data would be needed before using the model for decisions. Being able to write that design and diagnose its assumptions is a stronger outcome than simply producing a long list of simulated rows.
