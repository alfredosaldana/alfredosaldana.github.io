1. Why seasonal ARIMA is a separate modeling problem

Suppose a transportation authority observes monthly passenger entries. The last December count is much lower than the last July count, and the entire series also rises over time. A conventional ARIMA model can describe changes and short-run shocks, but it may leave predictable relationships between the same months of consecutive years. Seasonal ARIMA, abbreviated SARIMA, gives those relationships their own autoregressive, moving-average and differencing components. It is not simply a regression with twelve month labels, nor is it an instruction to differentiate every monthly series twelve periods apart.

By the end of this guide, you should be able to read the entire SARIMA order, calculate ordinary and seasonal differences without software, expand the lag-operator equation to reveal its cross-lag terms, fit and diagnose several plausible models, reconstruct forecasts on the original scale, and compare them fairly against Seasonal Naive and Exponential Smoothing. Every comparison must use the same origin, horizons, outcome units and evaluation period.

The illustration is fully synthetic: sixty consecutive months of passenger counts in thousands of passengers, constructed with a positive trend, an explicit twelve-month seasonal pattern and correlated stochastic disturbances. Months 1–48 are training data; months 49–60 are held out. This construction helps verify mathematics. It is not evidence of actual transit demand, a causal effect of policy or a universally best forecasting method. Seasonal patterns in real transportation depend on the calendar, school schedules, holidays, unusual events and measurement practices.

2. Prior knowledge and the distinction between seasonality and trend

Prerequisite Guide 63 introduced systematic trend, seasonality and decomposition; Guide 67 explained exponential smoothing; Guide 69 supplied nonseasonal ARIMA, differencing and residual diagnostics. We recall the minimum concepts here. A time series is a sequence Y_t indexed at equal intervals. A trend is sustained evolution over many periods. Seasonality means behavior that tends to recur at a known calendar or operational spacing m, although the values need not repeat exactly. A cycle need not have a fixed duration. Autocorrelation is the association between observations at separated times; it can persist after removing a deterministic monthly seasonal curve.

A distinction with practical consequences is deterministic seasonality versus stochastic seasonal dependence. Deterministic seasonality can be represented by fixed month effects; stochastic dependence means a shock this January may still predict the next January even after month means are removed. SARIMA is useful for the latter. A plausible seasonal component does not guarantee it is required: inspect residual ACF and compare held-out forecasts before adding parameters.

The student needs algebra, expected values and variance, conditional prediction, basic regression and the meaning of a confidence or prediction interval. We define every new order and explain the additional operator algebra before using it. We will call the unexpected innovations ε_t, distinguished from the fitted residuals e_t obtained after estimation. The theoretical assumption that innovations are uncorrelated does not follow merely from a graph that looks irregular.

3. The data-generating recipe, provenance and units

To make every observation reproducible, set t=1,...,60 and Y_t=105+0.75t+s_{j(t)}+u_t, where j(t) indexes a repeating list of twelve seasonal offsets: −12, −9, −4, 2, 7, 11, 15, 13, 5, −1, −9, −18. These offsets sum to zero, so they do not themselves change the annual average. The trend rises by 0.75 thousand passengers per month, or 750 passengers/month in the absence of random variation. The disturbance follows u_t=z_t+0.38z_{t−1}+0.45z_{t−12} when the lagged shocks exist, with z_t drawn from a Normal(0,2.4²) distribution in thousands of passengers. Missing presample innovations are treated as zero in the construction.

The Python generator uses NumPy default_rng with fixed seed 7062026. This is not an observation of actual transit activity and the seed is not a guarantee that future versions of unrelated generators will give identical results. The numerical table below contains all sixty realized values rounded to three decimals. Results in model fitting and tests use unrounded values from the included JSON file. Values greater than zero are still continuous approximations expressed in thousands, rather than literally counting fractional passengers.

Separate the model used to invent this lesson's data from the SARIMA model fitted later. Even with known generating ingredients, the estimation stage must not use future holdout observations or the hidden generating shocks. In real analysis, knowing the true generating recipe is almost never possible; diagnostic evaluation becomes more important, not less.

4. Frequency m and why a period is not a season label

A monthly record with annual recurrence has m=12 because the same position in the annual sequence is twelve observations apart. For quarterly data with an annual cycle m=4; weekly data with a weekly cycle is different from daily records with m=7. The value of m describes a lag in observations, not the number of categories you happened to name. A gap in data, duplicated timestamp or change in temporal aggregation can invalidate the assumed spacing. Check that every month exists once, in correct order, before using the number twelve.

A feature may also have overlapping periodicities: hourly transit records may have daily and weekly structure; fixed single-seasonal SARIMA is generally not sufficient to represent both simultaneously. A holiday changing its Gregorian date does not create exactly periodic monthly observations. Such effects can require external regressors, calendar adjustments or another modeling framework. These extensions should not be smuggled into a one-seasonal model by increasing P and Q indiscriminately.

The empirical mean for each calendar position is a helpful visualization, not proof of a seasonal unit root. Four years of training provide four Januaries and four Julys, a thin basis for estimating complex month-specific behavior. An apparent repeated jump can reflect a temporary policy or a measurement change. Our first decision is therefore not 'set D=1'; it is to establish a trustworthy time axis, record the season definition and then inspect evidence of persistence at multiples of m.

5. Ordinary differencing versus seasonal differencing

The ordinary first difference measures the one-month change: ΔY_t=Y_t−Y_{t−1}. The seasonal difference measures change from the same month one year earlier: Δ_12Y_t=Y_t−Y_{t−12}. With the unrounded synthetic series, Y_26≈113.143, Y_14≈100.968, so the seasonal change at month 26 is about 12.176 thousand passengers. This is not the same as Y_26−Y_25≈1.496. A seasonal difference compares February of year three with February of year two, not February with January.

Ordinary differencing may remove a stochastic trend, while seasonal differencing removes certain stochastic persistence patterns at lag twelve. These are distinct hypotheses. A deterministic linear trend survives a seasonal difference as an approximately constant 12-times-monthly slope; additional ordinary differencing removes that constant. A series with fixed month effects but stationary fluctuations may need no seasonal difference at all: using D=1 can create unnecessary negative seasonal correlation and inflate forecast variance.

We do not treat the sample mean after differencing as a substitute for checking stability of the underlying process. Plot levels, ordinary differences and seasonal differences over the same training interval. Inspect both short lags and lags twelve and twenty-four. Reserve the last twelve values so the choice of d and D is based on training information, not on the visually convenient holdout pattern.

Key equation
\[\Delta_{12}Y_t=(1-B^{12})Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-12}\]

6. Applying both differences and proving the order does not matter

A model with d=1 and D=1 works with W_t=(1−B)(1−B^12)Y_t. Multiply the two lag polynomials: (1−B−B^12+B^13)Y_t. Hence W_t=Y_t−Y_{t−1}−Y_{t−12}+Y_{t−13}. To calculate W_26, take Y_26≈113.1434, Y_25≈111.6470, Y_14≈100.9677 and Y_13≈103.2999. Substitution produces 113.1434−111.6470−100.9677+103.2999≈3.8287 thousand passengers. Each operation compares changes from one year with changes from the previous year.

The operators commute because B and B^12 are powers of the same lag operator: (1−B)(1−B^12)=(1−B^12)(1−B). Thus one may first calculate all ordinary changes and then their seasonal changes, or reverse the order, provided the same observations and indices are used. A difference is not an arbitrary detrending operation: it removes information about initial levels. To reconstruct the original scale, retain enough initial observations, and never use an actual future Y_t to invert a forecast when that value is unknown.

After applying d+D differences, the first available modeled observation is later than the first raw one; with d=D=1,m=12, W_14 is the earliest possible value, not W_1. Count the effective sample honestly when interpreting AIC, tests, estimates and the number of independent seasonal cycles. The same conceptual caution applies if some original observations are missing.

Key equation
\[\begin{aligned}W_t&=(1-B)(1-B^{12})Y_t\\&=Y_t-Y_{t-1}-Y_{t-12}+Y_{t-13}\end{aligned}\]

7. Understanding all seven SARIMA order numbers

We write SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m. The first three entries describe the nonseasonal component: p autoregressive lags, d ordinary differences and q moving-average lags. Uppercase P, D and Q specify seasonal AR lags, seasonal differences and seasonal MA lags; m is the number of observations per season. For example, SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12 has an MA component at lag one, a seasonal MA component at lag twelve and both difference operators. It contains no autoregressive coefficients. This compact specification is commonly called an 'airline-type' seasonal model, but no particular airline data are required.

A seasonal AR coefficient Φ_1 multiplies a lagged state at t−12, not a dummy variable for December. Seasonal MA Θ_1 multiplies an innovation from t−12, not the observed count last year. Mixing these objects would confuse prediction with explanations of the calendar. When P=2, the seasonal AR polynomial can involve lags 12 and 24; it does not mean twenty-four separately estimated month effects.

Because multiplicative seasonal polynomials interact with ordinary polynomials, an ostensibly small model may generate cross-lag terms at 13, 14 or other sums of lags. The product structure does not mean ordinary and seasonal forecasts are made independently and averaged. We use the full polynomial equation in the next two sections to expose that interaction.

Key equation
\[\operatorname{SARIMA}(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_{m}\]

8. The multiplicative polynomial equation

Define B Y_t=Y_{t−1}. Let φ(B)=1−φ_1B−⋯−φ_pB^p and Φ(B^m)=1−Φ_1B^m−⋯−Φ_PB^{Pm}. Likewise θ(B)=1+θ_1B+⋯+θ_qB^q and Θ(B^m)=1+Θ_1B^m+⋯+Θ_QB^{Qm}; the plus signs are a convention and alternative software may use reversed MA signs. A centered, zero-mean representation is φ(B)Φ(B^m)(1−B)^d(1−B^m)^DY_t=θ(B)Θ(B^m)ε_t. If we include drift or other deterministic terms, these must be stated explicitly and the interpretation of intercepts changes with differencing.

The stationary assumptions, when applicable, concern the transformed process and the roots of AR polynomials; invertibility concerns the MA polynomials. Innovations ε_t are model-implied shocks with conditional mean zero and finite variance. Estimation recovers parameters and filtered innovations from training observations; it does not reveal the original random disturbances used in our synthetic generator.

A quick dimensional check is helpful: B is unitless, all polynomial coefficients are unitless and ε_t has the same physical units as Y_t, thousands of passengers, after linear differencing. σ² therefore has units of thousands-of-passengers squared. Comparing this innovation variance to MAE, which has units of thousands of passengers, without taking a square root would be meaningless.

Key equation
\[\phi(B)\Phi(B^{12})(1-B)^d(1-B^{12})^DY_t=\theta(B)\Theta(B^{12})\varepsilon_t\]

9. Expanding the seasonal AR cross-term

Consider the hypothetical stationary model (1−0.4B)(1−0.3B^12)Y_t=ε_t. Multiply it as ordinary algebra: (1−0.4B−0.3B^12+0.12B^13)Y_t=ε_t. Solve for the current observation: Y_t=0.4Y_{t−1}+0.3Y_{t−12}−0.12Y_{t−13}+ε_t. The t−13 term is not an extra parameter separately estimated by the model: its coefficient is the product −φΦ. That restriction reduces parameter count and is characteristic of multiplicative SARIMA.

To check numerically, suppose the three past observations are Y_{t−1}=110, Y_{t−12}=100 and Y_{t−13}=98 thousand passengers, with expected innovation zero. The conditional mean is 0.4(110)+0.3(100)−0.12(98)=44+30−11.76=62.24 thousand. This is an algebra demonstration with hypothetical states, not the fitted forecast for the transit dataset; the example coefficients and states are intentionally different.

If you omit the negative cross-term you obtain 74 instead of 62.24, an error of 11.76 thousand passengers in this constructed substitution. This large contrast illustrates why reading an SARIMA model as just AR(1) plus seasonal AR(1) is misleading. The same product principle applies to higher seasonal and nonseasonal orders.

Key equation
\[(1-0.4B)(1-0.3B^{12})=1-0.4B-0.3B^{12}+0.12B^{13}\]

10. Expanding moving-average shocks at four lags

The most important example for our empirical case is SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)12. After the two differences, let W_t=(1−B)(1−B^12)Y_t. The stochastic equation is W_t=(1+θB)(1+ΘB^12)ε_t. Expanding gives W_t=ε_t+θε{t−1}+Θε_{t−12}+θΘε_{t−13}. Notice the last term at lag thirteen. Both the one-month and twelve-month shock effects interact; there are only two estimated MA coefficients, not three freely chosen lag effects.

For teaching, set θ=−0.5 and Θ=−0.4, and choose hypothetical innovations ε_t=2, ε_{t−1}=−1, ε_{t−12}=3 and ε_{t−13}=1, all in thousands of passengers. Substitute carefully: W_t=2+(−0.5)(−1)+(−0.4)(3)+(0.20)(1)=2+0.5−1.2+0.2=1.5. This is a transformed-series value, not the original monthly passenger count. To recover Y_t one must use the previously observed Y_{t−1}, Y_{t−12} and Y_{t−13} appropriately.

MA coefficients estimated by SARIMA describe unobserved filtered innovations, not direct multipliers on observed passenger counts. A fitted residual may approximate an innovation but is not an exact observation of it. Confusing past Y with past ε creates a different model and invalid predictions, even if the mistaken formula seems to have the correct number of lags.

Key equation
\[W_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}+\Theta\varepsilon_{t-12}+\theta\Theta\varepsilon_{t-13}\]

11. Choosing d and D without mechanically over-differencing

Begin with the training level plot and the ACF, not with the assumption that every seasonal series requires D=1. Look for a persistently evolving level, changing seasonal averages, and autocorrelation at 12, 24 and other multiples. An ordinary unit-root diagnostic may help distinguish deterministic trend from a unit root, but seasonal roots are a distinct issue and require further examination. A single conventional ADF result is not a formal test for all seasonal unit roots. Compare whether differences make the series easier to model without producing unnecessary negative correlations at lag one or lag twelve.

In our illustrative candidates the model with both d=1 and D=1 has an effective transformed period after month 13, while a D=0 candidate retains more immediate information. The different likelihood implementations account for initial states using state-space methods; they are not equivalent to simply discarding the first thirteen rows in every setting. Avoid interpreting reduced usable observations as identical across software and initialization methods.

An especially instructive counterexample is Y_t=100+0.75t+s_{month}+ε_t with independent stationary ε_t. A deterministic trend and fixed seasonal effects are not evidence that the underlying process has both an ordinary and a seasonal stochastic unit root. Regression with trend and monthly effects plus stationary errors may represent this series more economically. Over-differencing can turn a conceptually simple series into a noisy moving-average process and widen intervals. Diagnose the transformed data and compare out-of-sample outcomes.

12. Using seasonal ACF and PACF for provisional identification

After removing appropriate components, ACF and PACF provide clues about which orders to test. An MA-type seasonal shock often produces a prominent seasonal ACF feature near lag m; a seasonal AR effect can show a different tailing pattern at multiples of m. These are heuristics, not exact signatures in short, noisy samples. Ordinary and seasonal components interact, and differencing itself can alter the correlogram. Inspect lag one, lag twelve, lag thirteen and lag twenty-four together; a lag-thirteen peak may reflect multiplicative interaction rather than the need for thirteen free coefficients.

A training span of 48 months contains only four full annual cycles. Estimating many seasonal parameters from four cycles is risky even though the raw sample has 48 rows. If diagnostics are ambiguous, favor a short list of substantively sensible models. Our candidates include SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12, SARIMA(1,1,0)(1,1,0)_12 and a seasonal alternative with D=0. The shortlist is an analysis plan, not a claim that an automated search discovers the data-generating truth.

An instructive failure is observing residual seasonal ACF after fitting a nonseasonal model and immediately adding P=Q=2. Such a modification raises complexity and may create nearly unidentifiable parameters. Ask first whether the data contain a measurement break, a movable holiday, missing months or a deterministic seasonal mean that the model omitted. A dependence feature may signal misspecification beyond one SARIMA coefficient.

13. Estimation by likelihood and interpretation of software output

A SARIMA model is normally estimated by maximizing a likelihood conditioned on a stochastic state-space representation, with assumptions about innovations and the initial state. The software does not simply regress Y_t on thirteen past values. For each candidate our reproducibility script uses statsmodels SARIMAX, sets trend='n', requests stationary and invertible parameter regions, and estimates only on months 1–48. Different software, initializations and conventions can produce somewhat different parameter estimates or likelihood comparisons; reproducibility therefore requires both equations and configuration.

For SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12 the fitted ordinary MA parameter is about −0.7140 and the seasonal MA parameter about −0.3586; the innovation variance estimate is about 7.4748 in squared thousands of passengers. These parameters describe filtered changes and seasonal shocks, not a −71% change in raw passenger counts. The implied cross-lag MA coefficient is the product (−0.7140)(−0.3586)≈+0.2560. That coefficient is constrained, not estimated independently.

The model reports an AIC near 178.102 and converged according to the optimizer. Convergence means the numerical procedure reached its stopping criterion, not that the model is causally correct, that residuals are independent, or that future intervals are calibrated. Read parameter stability, near-boundary roots, residual tests and temporal forecast errors before declaring the model adequate.

Key equation
\[\widehat{\theta}\approx-0.7140,\quad\widehat{\Theta}\approx-0.3586,\quad \widehat{\theta}\widehat{\Theta}\approx0.2560\]

14. What AIC measures, and what it does not measure

AIC combines the maximized log likelihood with a penalty for fitted parameters: AIC=−2ℓ(θ̂)+2k. In our candidate table it ranks the specified fits on training data. Among the four computed candidates, SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12 has AIC 178.102, compared with 181.204 for SARIMA(1,1,0)(1,1,0)_12. The difference is about 3.102 AIC units under the stated state-space likelihood implementation. A smaller value favors a model for the training likelihood-penalty criterion, not a guarantee of better future prediction.

AIC values are not arbitrary model-independent quality scores. They should be compared only when likelihoods describe comparable observed outcomes under comparable estimation conventions. Differencing choices, initial-state methods and transformations may make naive comparisons of printed AIC values misleading. Report the exact software and train sample. AICc can be important with small effective seasonal samples; our four cycles are a reason to avoid large seasonal orders.

The final decision must incorporate residual diagnostics, a baseline and evaluation on months that never participated in fitting or tuning. Indeed, our main candidate has lower held-out fixed-origin MAE than the other considered SARIMA models, but the Holt–Winters reference is close. A second dataset might reverse the ordering. Model selection is about predictive evidence conditional on an evaluation protocol, not about proving a single correct seasonal structure.

Key equation
\[\operatorname{AIC}=-2\ell(\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta})+2k\]

15. Reconstructing forecasts after double differencing

To forecast the original monthly Y_t from a predicted W_t=(1−B)(1−B^12)Y_t, rearrange the definition: Y_t=W_t+Y_{t−1}+Y_{t−12}−Y_{t−13}. For a one-step forecast at t=49, the three lagged levels are known at the end of month 48. For t=50, Y_49 is no longer known at forecast origin 48, so its forecast must be substituted. This recursive rule continues through a twelve-month horizon. A forecast implementation that accidentally inserts realized holdout Y_49 while constructing the t=50 prediction is leaking information.

For example, use hypothetical known levels Y_48=121.85, Y_37=121.864 and Y_36=114.98, with a transformed prediction Ŵ_49=1.0. The reconstructed illustrative forecast is 1.0+121.85+121.864−114.98=129.734 thousand. The actual fitted main-model month-49 forecast is about 130.582, because its filtered innovation state implies a different Ŵ_49. We deliberately label the former a demonstration and the latter a fitted result: do not use one as a check of the other's numeric value.

An ordinary difference by itself reconstructs by cumulative addition; a seasonal difference reconstructs using the corresponding earlier season. With both operators, the combined recurrence links the previous month, the previous year and the previous year's preceding month. Errors in future reconstructed levels can accumulate and make horizons have different uncertainty. This is why forecast intervals cannot be generated by copying the one-step standard error at every horizon.

Key equation
\[\widehat{Y}_{t|48}=\widehat{W}_{t|48}+\widehat{Y}_{t-1|48}+\widehat{Y}_{t-12|48}-\widehat{Y}_{t-13|48}\]

16. Seasonal Naive is a real benchmark, not a straw man

Seasonal Naive forecasts each coming month using the observation exactly one cycle earlier: Ŷ_{48+h|48}=Y_{36+h}, for h=1,...,12. Thus the forecast for month 49 is the actual month 37, about 121.864 thousand, and it does not see month 49. The month-49 realized value is approximately 132.223, giving absolute error 10.359 thousand. The remaining eleven absolute errors must be computed using the corresponding year-prior values from months 38–48, never by reusing December or the last overall monthly average.

Over all twelve holdout months, Seasonal Naive has MAE about 8.691 and RMSE computed in the accompanying results file. Its simplicity is a strength: it has minimal fitted parameters, is easy to explain and is often difficult to beat on strongly seasonal data. The lesson's synthetic series has a positive trend, so an unadjusted year-old forecast tends to lag behind recent demand; however, that observation in this particular dataset is not a reason to dismiss seasonal benchmarks generally.

A convincing model comparison explains where the gain comes from and what it costs. SARIMA uses estimated parameters, assumptions about stochastic dynamics, optimization and more complex prediction intervals. Seasonal Naive can still win if the seasonal pattern is stable, if shocks destroy the fitted relationship or if training history is too limited. The correct benchmark depends on the operational decision and horizon.

Key equation
\[\widehat{Y}^{SN}_{48+h\mid48}=Y_{36+h},\quad h=1,\ldots,12\]

17. Comparing Holt–Winters with identical forecast origins

Guide 67 developed Holt–Winters additive Exponential Smoothing. We fit its level, trend and seasonal states only to months 1–48, then produce a fixed-origin twelve-month path for months 49–60. This is essential: a Holt–Winters method updated after observing month 49 cannot fairly be compared to a SARIMA path that remained frozen at month 48. They answer different forecasting questions. All methods must forecast passenger counts in thousands, not some transform that has not been inverted.

In this synthetic series, additive Holt–Winters achieves held-out MAE about 2.516 thousand; our SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12 achieves about 2.465 thousand. Their difference is only about 0.051 thousand passengers—approximately 51 passengers in the units of the constructed continuous approximation—far too little to justify a universal claim of meaningful operational superiority from twelve errors. Seasonal Naive is much less accurate on this particular holdout, with MAE about 8.691.

When deciding between two close models, document robustness across multiple rolling origins, the stability of seasonal factors, forecast interval calibration, maintenance burden and how the organization acts on errors. A method that wins by 0.051 in one synthetic year may lose after refitting, during an unusual year or when the business objective penalizes underforecasting more heavily than overforecasting. Report the protocol before the leaderboard.

18. Residual diagnostics at seasonal and ordinary lags

After estimation, residuals should contain little predictable serial structure if the chosen conditional model is adequate. Plot residuals against time, their ACF to at least 24 lags, the distribution of residual magnitudes and their relationship to the fitted level or calendar. In SARIMA, pay special attention to lags 12 and 24: a nonseasonal Ljung–Box check at just lag six could overlook annual residual dependence. Also examine whether residual spread changes between summer and winter; conditional mean dynamics alone do not guarantee constant variance.

For the main fit, a Ljung–Box check at 12 lags on residuals after a presample burn-in gives p≈0.496 in the reproducible script. This provides no evidence to reject the composite null of zero autocorrelations through the tested lags at a 5% threshold. It does not prove independent innovations, correct distributional assumptions, stable parameters or reliable tail coverage. The result depends on which residuals are included and whether degrees-of-freedom adjustments are used; state those choices explicitly.

Early state-space residuals can be unusually large because of diffuse initialization and differencing. For our diagnostic demonstration we omit the first 24 residuals, retaining a short set; this reduces sample size and test power. An alternative burn-in may change the p-value. We must not hide these choices or claim that a large p-value certifies a model. Compare full residual plots and forecasts, not a single diagnostic threshold.

19. Forecast intervals and increasing horizon uncertainty

The state-space model supplies a conditional distribution for each future observation. Its point forecast is an estimated conditional mean, and a model-based 95% interval typically uses a normal approximation to a predictive standard deviation that depends on horizon and the assumed innovation variance. The interval is not merely a confidence interval for a coefficient and does not claim that the realized future value has a 95% probability after it is observed. Frequentist repeated-forecast calibration, if the model and estimation assumptions are adequate, is the relevant interpretation.

For month 49 the main candidate predicts about 130.582 thousand passengers, with an approximate 95% model-based interval [125.221,135.944] thousand. The observed month-49 count is about 132.223, within that interval; one successful inclusion says almost nothing about actual long-run coverage. At farther horizons, seasonal and ordinary integration can accumulate shocks, so interval widths may grow unevenly and depend on the season. Inspect the full sequence of twelve intervals, not just the first.

Parameter uncertainty, holidays, structural breaks, model-selection uncertainty and measurement error may not be fully represented by reported conditional intervals. A significant future service change could make the normal innovation assumption inappropriate. Empirical backtesting of coverage and interval width at each horizon is necessary before operational promises are based on these numbers. Distinguish an internally consistent model interval from a demonstrated calibrated interval.

20. Why fixed-origin evaluation differs from rolling-origin evaluation

A fixed-origin evaluation trains once on observations 1–48 and predicts months 49–60 without updating the information set. That is the comparison reported in our numerical table, and it answers a genuine twelve-month planning question. A rolling-origin one-step evaluation would predict month 49 using 1–48, then predict month 50 using the actual month 49 once it becomes available, and continue likewise. Such information is legitimate for a new origin, but not for the old frozen origin.

For a fair rolling benchmark, Seasonal Naive likewise uses the relevant actual value twelve months before each new target. Holt–Winters must update its states from data available by the new origin, and SARIMA may either update filtered states with fixed parameters or reestimate parameters—two different protocols. Document which occurs. A method can perform better at one step yet worse at twelve steps because shocks and integration accumulate differently.

A stronger evaluation than our single split uses several chronological origins and reports errors by horizon rather than mixing all horizons indiscriminately. Origins must be far enough into the training history to estimate a stable seasonal model. Do not create training records after their own evaluation target or tune d, D and seasonal orders repeatedly on the last twelve months. If exploratory tuning used the holdout, the holdout is no longer independent evidence and should be replaced with a new evaluation period.

21. Several seasonal frequencies, holiday effects and structural change

Single-period SARIMA is not a universal seasonal engine. A transit authority might need to forecast hourly entries affected by hour of day, weekday and annual patterns. Setting m=24 captures one daily cycle but does not independently model a seven-day cycle of 168 hours. Increasing P does not automatically create a second independent seasonal frequency. Alternative approaches include Fourier seasonal terms with regression errors, multi-seasonal state-space or specialized models, chosen for the data and objectives.

Movable holidays, strikes, fare changes, extraordinary weather and major construction are not guaranteed to repeat exactly at lag twelve. Their effects may need calendar regressors, intervention indicators or scenario analysis. We are not implementing a full SARIMAX external-regressor workflow here; that belongs to the next guide on external predictors. The essential boundary is conceptual: forecasting a future holiday effect requires knowing or scenario-specifying the relevant future calendar and expected impact rather than borrowing a future observation.

Structural breaks can invalidate both seasonal-AR relationships and the stationary innovation assumption. Compare before/after plots, consider alternative evaluation windows, and explain when you would retrain or suspend deployment. A transparent Seasonal Naive baseline may be preferable to a complicated SARIMA whose parameters swing after a few new observations. Applied time-series analysis is iterative model criticism, not merely selecting the order tuple with the best printed score.

22. A full worked decision and its evidential limits

We fitted four SARIMA candidates on forty-eight months. The main SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12 model has the lowest AIC among those candidates, approximately 178.102. Its fixed-origin holdout MAE is 2.465 thousand passengers. A competing seasonal autoregressive model SARIMA(1,1,0)(1,1,0)_12 has MAE about 3.896; the additive Holt–Winters baseline has about 2.516 and Seasonal Naive about 8.691. We can therefore say the main candidate is the most accurate by this MAE on these twelve synthetic months among these specified methods, not the best general method for transit.

Operationally, the SARIMA and Holt–Winters MAEs are close. Before adopting the more complex model, evaluate several rolling origins, inspect seasonal residual dependence and interval coverage, estimate costs of demand underprediction and check how stable the results remain when the evaluation year changes. The model intervals describe conditional uncertainty but do not include all possible schedule changes or interventions. Present a baseline in every deployment report, and record which input observations were available at each origin.

The guide's claims are supported by derivations, one fully declared synthetic dataset, a reproducible fitted comparison and limitations. No student learning trial has been performed; the Student Understanding Test is an editorial judgment about whether calculations can be followed. In the next section, transfer exercises require applying the same ideas to different season lengths and forecasting protocols without borrowing unknown future data.

23. Finite samples, convergence and boundary estimates

A model with four letters of orders can appear simple, yet estimating it is demanding when the seasonal period is twelve. Forty-eight training observations contain only four annual cycles. Differencing at lag twelve removes twelve starting values from the straightforward transformed representation, and ordinary differencing removes another beginning difference; exact state-space likelihood handles initialization more carefully, but it cannot invent independent annual replications. Estimates close to stationarity or invertibility boundaries may be sensitive to initialization or optimization settings. Our results are numerical illustrations, not claims of asymptotic certainty from a short sample.

Check converged, parameter constraints, estimates of innovation variance, standardized residuals and whether different initial guesses yield materially different answers. A converged optimizer is only evidence that a numerical search stopped according to its rules. It is not proof of identification, unbiasedness, correctly specified dynamics or stable future performance. If alternative starts lead to different likelihood maxima, disclose this rather than selecting whichever result is easiest to explain.

A practical audit asks whether at least two full cycles survive the preparation steps, whether seasonal orders are modest relative to data length and whether estimated seasonal dependence is distinct from fixed calendar effects. For a seasonal period of 52 weeks, just two years supply only two repetitions of each seasonal position; a complex seasonal model is especially fragile. Thus the same choice of orders must be judged relative to observational information, not simply accepted because software reports coefficients.

24. A hand-calculated seasonal forecast and its assumptions

To see the role of a seasonal operator without software, start with the simpler SARIMA(0,0,0)(1,0,0)12 equation Y_t=\mu+0.6(Y{t-12}-\mu)+\varepsilon_t. Suppose the long-run level is 110 thousand passengers, the observation twelve months ago is 124 thousand and the future innovation has conditional expectation zero. The forecast is 110+0.6(124-110)=118.4 thousand passengers. The computation has three visible stages: subtract the long-run level (14), retain 60% of the seasonal deviation (8.4), and return to the original level (118.4). It does not copy last year's value unconditionally, unlike Seasonal Naive, and it does not claim that 118.4 is the actual future outcome.

At a two-year horizon the conditional expectation of the deviation becomes 0.6^2(124-110)=5.04, producing 115.04 thousand, provided no new observations intervene and the assumed seasonal AR structure remains stable. This illustrates that seasonal dependence decays under |\Phi|<1. If \Phi=1, the seasonal deviation would persist without decay; the stationary seasonal AR assumption no longer holds. For a real fitted model, additional ordinary AR, MA and differencing components modify the recursion substantially.

This hand calculation is a hypothetical submodel, not a calculation from the sixty-passenger dataset. Keeping the contexts separate is essential. Our actual fitted SARIMA includes ordinary and seasonal differencing and two MA components, so its forecasts must be produced from the full fitted state and back-transformed by the double-difference identity.

Key equation
\[\widehat Y_t=110+0.6(124-110)=118.4\;\text{(thousand)}\]

25. The same seasonal pattern under differing model assumptions

Consider three superficially similar scenarios. First, exactly the same annual seasonal shape repeats every twelve months while the rest of the series consists of independent disturbances. Fixed seasonal indicator variables may be sufficient, and a seasonal AR or MA parameter need not add value. Second, each January deviation depends on last January's unexpected deviation; that is stochastic seasonal dependence, and a seasonal AR model may be natural. Third, the expected January effect itself changes after a major service expansion. Neither a fixed indicator nor a stationary seasonal AR component alone guarantees adequate forecasts across the break.

Why does this matter? A plot of monthly means can display nearly identical curves for all three. The difference concerns the conditional distribution of the series after accounting for the systematic components, not merely whether summer is consistently high. Examine residual ACF after plausible detrending, compare structural-break periods and use a held-out forecast test to distinguish proposals. We should avoid asserting that a seasonal peak in the raw ACF proves a seasonal unit root. It might instead arise from fixed calendar means.

An especially useful contrast involves D=1. Seasonal differencing converts Y_t into Y_t-Y_{t-12}; when the seasonal pattern is stable and deterministic, it cancels but also introduces dependence in the transformed errors. If the raw series is already otherwise stationary around fixed monthly means, automatically adding seasonal differences can over-difference and reduce forecast accuracy. The correct action comes from model diagnostics and evaluation, not from a rule that monthly data always require D=1.

26. Reproducible comparison protocol and ethical reporting

For reproducibility, state the outcome, calendar frequency, source or synthetic construction, training window, forecast origin, model orders, initialization, optimizer, software version, restrictions, scoring rule and exact baselines. In our teaching example the outcome is thousands of monthly passenger entries, the training cutoff is month 48, the evaluation spans months 49–60, and the principal performance score is MAE. Fixed-origin predictions are frozen at the cutoff. Model comparison uses the same twelve realized targets.

Report both AIC and holdout errors but do not pretend that they estimate the same object. AIC concerns a penalty-adjusted fit to data used for estimation; MAE measures realized absolute forecast error over a chosen evaluation period. Likewise, note that a Ljung–Box p-value above 0.05 is not evidence that the model is true; it only fails to reject a specified null under testing assumptions and limited power. Investigate visibly important seasonal errors despite a non-significant omnibus diagnostic.

If a model is selected by repeatedly checking holdout scores, the reported holdout has become part of the selection process. Reserve later data for a final test or use nested time-series evaluation. For publication, display the synthetic provenance explicitly, identify all units and expose the complete small dataset. Never describe this artificial case as evidence that an actual transit agency would benefit by a stated percentage. Our final recommendation should therefore be conditional: compare several rolling origins and choose only after evaluating operational costs and calibration.

27. A closing audit a student can reproduce

To audit the argument without software, take the given twelve seasonal offsets and verify that their sum is zero. Reconstruct an observation from trend, seasonal offset and the declared disturbance. Calculate at least one ordinary, seasonal and double difference using four rows. Expand an AR cross-term, expand an MA cross-term and use the inverse-difference identity to recover an original-scale forecast. These steps verify how the equations fit together before any numerical optimization is involved.

Then distinguish what needs software: estimating a state-space likelihood, maintaining latent innovations and producing model-based forecast covariance at twelve horizons. The included reproducibility script fits only training observations, stores the full unrounded series and prints the selected candidate estimates and forecast paths. A student should be able to explain, without merely repeating output, why an optimizer result needs residual criticism and a genuinely held-out evaluation.

Finally, make the deployment decision conditional on data availability and intended use. A good answer must mention that a low AIC does not guarantee a low MAE, that a good MAE does not establish interval calibration, and that a model of one calendar period may fail when several seasons or a structural intervention matter. If any of these distinctions cannot be explained in the student's own words, revisit the corresponding calculation before treating the guide as mastered.

29. A full numeric audit of seasonal errors and scale

A fitted forecast is useful only when its accuracy is measured in a scale a decision-maker recognizes. Consider the first held-out month: actual arrivals are 132.223 thousand, the SARIMA point prediction is 130.582 thousand, and Seasonal Naive predicts 121.864 thousand. Their signed errors, defined as actual minus forecast, are 1.641 and 10.359 thousand respectively. Their absolute errors are the same positive magnitudes here because both methods underpredict. If an operating manager requires staff per thousand additional arrivals, a seemingly small difference of 1.641 thousand represents roughly 1,641 passengers, not 1.641 people. Unit conversion matters.

Across twelve held-out months, the main SARIMA has MAE 2.465 and RMSE 2.969 thousand. RMSE exceeds MAE because squaring disproportionately penalizes large errors before taking the square root. Seasonal Naive has MAE 8.691. The relative decrease in MAE, (8.691-2.465)/8.691≈0.716, is about 71.6% for this constructed holdout. This is a comparison of error sizes, not a 71.6% increase in transit revenue, a causal effect of SARIMA, or a guarantee for next year. Since the generating process was engineered with seasonal correlated disturbances, performance can be unusually favorable.

A transparent report should show the model complexity and the operational consequence alongside each aggregate metric. For example, if overpredicting by 2,000 passengers leads to excess staffing and underpredicting by the same amount causes queueing, absolute error alone may not reflect the asymmetric costs. Inspect signed errors and their calendar concentration before recommending adoption. A model can have low overall MAE while systematically underforecasting holiday peaks, precisely when the operational cost of an error is highest.

30. How seasonal differencing changes the forecasting question

Ordinary differencing compares adjacent months: ΔY_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}. It asks how much the level changed from last month. Seasonal differencing compares the same seasonal position in consecutive cycles: Δ_12Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-12}. It asks how much this January differs from last January. Applying both asks whether the month-to-month change differs from the corresponding change a year ago. These are three distinct empirical questions and should not be described simply as 'removing trend' and 'removing seasonality' without inspecting the resulting process.

Take four hypothetical observed values in thousands: Y_25=140, Y_24=138, Y_13=125, Y_12=124. Ordinary change is 2; year-over-year difference is 15; the combined difference is 1. Notice that the combined difference can be close to zero even when the original level and annual growth are large. This is exactly why plotting and evaluating the differenced scale alone can obscure operational magnitude. The inverse identity puts model forecasts back into meaningful passenger units.

A useful checking question is what happens after seasonal differencing when the cycle is stable but the trend continues. If Y_t=a+bt+s_{j(t)} with stable monthly offsets, then Δ_12Y_t=12b, a constant. Applying the ordinary difference makes ΔΔ_12Y_t=0 in the noise-free case. This proves algebraically why double differencing removes a deterministic linear trend plus fixed seasonal offsets; it does not prove it is always optimal or required. If disturbances are independent before differencing, unnecessary differences can create artificial negative serial correlations and inflate forecast uncertainty.

Key equation
\[\Delta\Delta_{12}Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}-Y_{t-12}+Y_{t-13}\]

31. A counterexample about overfitting seasonal memory

Imagine monthly sales whose calendar averages vary strongly by month but whose deviations around those averages are independent. A deterministic model with twelve monthly means can capture the repeating curve, and its residuals can be almost uncorrelated. Adding seasonal AR and seasonal MA parameters to those residuals might fit chance patterns in the training period but make uncertain out-of-sample predictions. A seasonal-looking line graph does not by itself warrant stochastic seasonal memory.

Reverse the situation: suppose the monthly seasonal means are almost flat but January's unexpected increase predicts next January's increase. Fixed month indicators may add little, while a seasonal autoregressive coefficient can improve forecasting. ACF computed after removing means can distinguish the cases better than a chart of monthly averages alone. Another practical complication is a moving holiday: its date shifts from year to year, so a fixed January effect or lag-twelve shock is not automatically an adequate representation of its influence.

The diagnosis requires more than picking the lowest in-sample AIC among many tried specifications. If each candidate's lag order is chosen by inspecting its performance on the same holdout, the final score is optimistic. State how candidate models were proposed, which diagnostics were consulted and how the holdout remained protected. If the family of candidate models is large relative to the 48 training months, emphasize uncertainty and the possibility that a simple Holt–Winters or Seasonal Naive model offers more robust operational behavior.

32. Forecasting is a decision made before observations occur

Suppose the planner must set next year's staffing today. Forecasting the whole twelve-month path from month 48 matches the decision: months 49–60 are unknown. Updating one prediction after each new month answers a different question: how should staffing be revised once information arrives? Both are legitimate, but they cannot be mixed in a single performance score without a careful definition of origins and horizons.

There is a further distinction between prediction and explanation. A seasonal model may predict July passenger numbers well because past July shocks recur, while offering no explanation of why passengers choose to travel. It does not isolate the causal impact of opening a route, changing fares or promoting tourism. When decisions intervene in the system, predicted values may cease to follow the old relationship. Operational forecasting requires checking information availability, monitoring performance and communicating scenarios, not interpreting fitted correlations as mechanisms.

33. Exercise 1: Read a SARIMA order and interpret its period

Problem. A monthly series is modeled with SARIMA(1,1,0)(0,1,1)_12. Explain each number and specify which differences are applied. Distinguish twelve months from twelve independent observations.

Complete solution. The ordinary component has p=1, d=1 and q=0: one ordinary autoregressive coefficient after one ordinary difference and no ordinary MA coefficient. The seasonal component has P=0, D=1 and Q=1, with m=12: there is one seasonal difference and a shock term at seasonal lag twelve but no seasonal AR coefficient. Compute W_t=(1-B)(1-B^12)Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}-Y_{t-12}+Y_{t-13}; each subtraction uses values separated by the indicated calendar intervals. For t=20, this formula uses observations 20,19,8,7. A period of twelve is a spacing, not twelve replicate experiments. Before estimation, inspect whether both differences are justified; the notation states a candidate model, not a diagnosis.

34. Exercise 2: Calculate a double difference with original-scale units

Problem. Suppose four monthly counts in thousands are Y_25=140, Y_24=138, Y_13=125 and Y_12=124. Calculate ordinary, seasonal and combined differences at month 25.

Complete solution. Ordinary difference: ΔY_25=140-138=2 thousand passengers. Seasonal difference: Δ_12Y_25=140-125=15 thousand. Combined: ΔΔ_12Y_25=(140-138)-(125-124)=2-1=1 thousand, or 140-138-125+124=1. The two routes agree, an independent algebraic check. The transformed value measures the change in the year-over-year increase, not a monthly total. Inference on the transformed scale must be inverted before communicating a forecast in passenger counts.

35. Exercise 3: Expand a multiplicative seasonal AR equation

Problem. Expand (1-0.4B)(1-0.3B^12)Y_t=ε_t and calculate Y_t if Y_{t-1}=120, Y_{t-12}=115, Y_{t-13}=110 and E(ε_t)=0.

Complete solution. Multiplication gives 1-0.4B-0.3B^12+0.12B^13, so Y_t=0.4Y_{t-1}+0.3Y_{t-12}-0.12Y_{t-13}+ε_t. The conditional mean is 0.4(120)+0.3(115)-0.12(110)=48+34.5-13.2=69.3 in the units of Y. Algebra verifies that the lag-thirteen coefficient is constrained by 0.4×0.3, rather than freely estimated. The absolute value is hypothetical: this no-intercept equation is not a realistic level model for monthly passengers. Add an appropriate mean when modeling a nonzero stationary level.

36. Exercise 4: Expand the seasonal moving-average interaction

Problem. For (1+0.5B)(1-0.2B^12)ε_t, write all nonzero shock terms. Evaluate the result when ε_t=2, ε_{t-1}=-1, ε_{t-12}=3 and ε_{t-13}=4.

Complete solution. Expand the product: ε_t+0.5ε_{t-1}-0.2ε_{t-12}-0.1ε_{t-13}. Substitute 2+0.5(-1)-0.2(3)-0.1(4)=2-0.5-0.6-0.4=0.5 units. Re-multiplying the operators verifies the lag-thirteen cross-term is -0.1. Four terms do not imply four independent MA coefficients: only two free coefficients plus the innovation scale. The numerical innovations are assumed known for this exercise, whereas in a real fit they are latent and inferred.

37. Exercise 5: Recover a forecast on the original scale

Problem. Suppose Ŵ_49=2 and observed levels Y_48=145, Y_37=130, Y_36=127. Forecast Y_49 using W_t=(1-B)(1-B^12)Y_t.

Complete solution. Rearrange Y_49=W_49+Y_48+Y_37-Y_36. Substitute 2+145+130-127=150 thousand passengers. Verify by inserting the result back: 150-145-130+127=2, equal to the transformed forecast. The initial levels are not optional; losing them prevents inversion. For a second-step forecast from origin 48, use the predicted Y_49 in any term involving month 49, never the observed future month-49 value.

38. Exercise 6: Calculate Seasonal Naive at the correct origin

Problem. A monthly series has training months 1–48. Give the formula for the next twelve Seasonal Naive forecasts and compute month 49 if Y_37=121.864 and the realized Y_49=132.223.

Complete solution. At origin 48, Ŷ_{48+h|48}=Y_{36+h} for h=1,...,12, so the first forecast is Y_37=121.864 thousand passengers. Absolute error for month 49 is |132.223-121.864|=10.359 thousand. The observed month 49 may be used to score the forecast after it occurs but not to produce it. 39. Exercise 7: Interpret a seasonal model selection table

Problem. Two fitted candidates show AIC values 178.102 and 181.204. Explain the AIC difference and whether it guarantees that the first has lower holdout error.

Complete solution. Subtract 181.204-178.102=3.102. This is a 3.102-point advantage for the first model in the training likelihood-penalty criterion, assuming comparable likelihood definitions and the same fitting data. It is not an error expressed in passenger units. The models can reverse order in held-out MAE; fitting criteria trade off complexity and fit whereas evaluation measures future errors. In our synthetic comparison the first also has lower MAE than the second, but that agreement is evidence for these data and these candidate sets only. Additional rolling origins would make the assessment less dependent on the chosen year.

40. Exercise 8: Compute MAE and compare with a benchmark

Problem. A method makes four frozen-origin forecasts [110,118,122,127] against [112,115,125,129]. Calculate MAE and compare to another benchmark with MAE 4.0.

Complete solution. Absolute errors are |112-110|=2, |115-118|=3, |125-122|=3 and |129-127|=2 units. Their sum is 10 and MAE=10/4=2.5 units. Against a benchmark MAE of 4.0, the improvement in absolute-error magnitude is 4.0-2.5=1.5 units, or 37.5% of the benchmark MAE. This is not a 37.5% reduction in total passenger demand and does not prove that the effect repeats elsewhere. Confirm that both methods used the same four targets, forecasting origins and horizon definitions before comparing.

41. Exercise 9: Diagnose residual seasonal autocorrelation

Problem. A residual Ljung–Box test at lag twelve yields p=0.496. What can and cannot be claimed? Explain why a plot at lag twenty-four may still help.

Complete solution. At level 0.05, p=0.496 exceeds the threshold, so this particular test does not reject its joint null of no autocorrelation across the examined lags, under the testing convention used. This is not proof of white noise, independence, model truth or adequate coverage of forecast intervals. With a short series, the test may have low power. A residual ACF to lag twenty-four can show remaining annual structure at the second multiple of twelve and reveal whether a few large residuals drive the result. Diagnostic lag choices and any degrees-of-freedom corrections should be stated explicitly.

42. Exercise 10: Separate model prediction from interval calibration

Problem. For month 49, a fitted model predicts 130.582 with a nominal 95% interval [125.221,135.944]; observed Y_49=132.223. Is its 95% coverage empirically established?

Complete solution. Check membership: 125.221≤132.223≤135.944, so the realized month is covered. The lower distance is 132.223-125.221=7.002 thousand and upper distance 135.944-132.223=3.721 thousand. One successful interval out of one is not evidence of 95% coverage. The reported nominal interval conditions on fitted dynamics, innovation assumptions and the state at month 48; it may omit estimation and intervention uncertainty. Estimate actual coverage using many truly out-of-sample origins and evaluate each forecast horizon separately.

43. Exercise 11: Distinguish fixed and rolling forecast protocols

Problem. A team forecasts all months 49–60 from origin 48. Another team observes month 49 before forecasting month 50. May their errors be compared without qualification?

Complete solution. No. Team A makes a fixed-origin multi-step path and cannot use any realized observation after month 48. Team B uses a rolling-origin one-step path and may use month-49 information because it has genuinely become available at the new origin. These correspond to different operational tasks. A fair comparison holds information sets, horizons and targets constant. In particular, if a method updates its seasonal states with month-49 outcomes, all contenders should have access to corresponding observations at that same origin. Explain whether SARIMA parameters were reestimated or only its filtered state updated; the two operations can differ.

44. Exercise 12: Transfer: defend a seasonal model after structural change

Problem. A transit agency expands service after month 48, changing capacity and the summer seasonal shape. Explain how to review an earlier SARIMA selection without claiming a causal service effect.

Complete solution. First, define what changed: routes, operating hours, ticket counting and expected seasonal capacity. Record the exact start date and ensure no postchange measurement enters earlier training folds. Plot residuals before and after, compare at least one transparent seasonal baseline and use new rolling-origin evaluations when enough postchange months accumulate. Check monthly bias, MAE by horizon and interval coverage; if the historical relationship is unstable, consider retraining, an intervention regressor or a scenario approach, subject to future predictor availability. Do not interpret a fitted seasonal coefficient as the causal impact of service expansion: demand may also respond to prices, weather and other changes. A defensible decision is conditional deployment with monitoring, or temporary reliance on a simpler baseline.
