MODULE 01 / 32

01. Why one survey estimate is not the whole story

A public-service office wants to estimate the average number of minutes residents spend waiting for assistance. It cannot interview every resident at every visit, so it samples customers and reports an average. That average is a point estimate: one number calculated from the sample. A different random sample, taken under the same design, would generally give a different number. The central question in this guide is not merely “How spread out are the waiting times?” but “How much could our estimated average change if we repeated the sampling process?” Standard Error (SE) addresses the second question. Its reference is the behavior of an estimator across hypothetical repeated samples, not the distance between individual observations in the sample already collected.

This distinction supports honest statistical communication. Suppose the estimated average is 14 minutes. Saying “14 minutes” without the study design, sample size, or uncertainty may make the result sound exact. Reporting a Standard Error describes its sampling precision, although it does not by itself provide a confidence interval or establish that a convenience sample represents the entire community. An estimate can have a very small SE for the wrong target population. A point estimate can also be numerically stable but systematically biased by nonresponse or flawed measurement. Sampling precision, measurement quality, and validity of the study population are different dimensions of evidence.

We will move from a complete, inspectable miniature population to survey examples, independence assumptions, finite-population sampling, and clustering. Throughout, every illustrative dataset is constructed for teaching; none represents observed municipal data. The goal is to make the mathematical operations reproducible by hand before interpreting their practical consequences. We will also distinguish known theoretical SE from an estimated SE computed with sample data.

MODULE 02 / 32

02. Learning objectives, prerequisite bridge, and the statistical target

After this guide, you should be able to derive the variance and SE of an independent sample mean; derive and estimate the SE of a sample proportion; distinguish Standard Deviation (SD), Standard Error (SE), and observed estimation error; and diagnose why dependence, sampling without replacement, or clustering changes a familiar formula. You should also be able to explain the conditions under which a reported SE supports a defensible statement about precision and which questions it cannot answer.

From Guide 13, recall that a random variable is a quantity whose value depends on chance. Its expectation is its probability-weighted long-run center, its variance describes dispersion about that center, and covariance measures how two quantities vary together. From Guide 26, recall the roles of estimand, estimator, and estimate. The estimand might be the population mean wait time μ; the estimator is the sampling rule X̄; the realized estimate might be 14 minutes. For a parameter θ and estimator T, sampling variability concerns the distribution of T, not a single realized difference T−θ.

Our basic assumptions will always be made explicit. An independent-and-identically-distributed (i.i.d.) sample means the observations have the same probability law and are mutually independent. We use μ for population expectation, σ² for population variance, n for the number of units, X̄ for the sample mean, and s² for the sample variance. Time is measured in minutes; variances of time have units of minutes squared; SD and SE return to minutes. When independence fails, the number of rows is not necessarily the number of independent pieces of information. When we sample a fixed finite population without replacement, a different variance calculation applies.

MODULE 03 / 32

03. Three different kinds of variation

Consider the teaching sample of six wait times, in minutes: 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18. These values differ substantially from one another. Their mean is (8+10+12+14+16+18)/6 = 13 minutes. The deviations are −5, −3, −1, 1, 3, 5 minutes; their squared deviations total 25+9+1+1+9+25 = 70 minutes². The sample variance is 70/(6−1) = 14 minutes² and the sample SD is √14 ≈ 3.7417 minutes. The SD characterizes variability among individual sampled waiting times.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\bar x=13,\quad s^2=\frac{70}{5}=14,\quad s=\sqrt{14}\approx3.7417,\quad \widehat{SE}=\frac{s}{\sqrt6}\approx1.5275\]

Now imagine repeating the entire six-person sampling experiment many times. Each experiment produces one mean, not six independent estimates. A collection of those means would have its own distribution: the sampling distribution of X̄. Its SD is the Standard Error of the mean. If i.i.d. sampling with variance σ² is a reasonable model, the theoretical SE is σ/√6. Because σ is usually unknown, we estimate it by s/√6 = √14/√6 ≈ 1.5275 minutes. This number is smaller than the individual-level SD because averaging partly cancels independent high and low observations.

A third quantity, the realized estimation error, is X̄−μ, the difference between this particular estimate and the actual population mean. We cannot normally compute that error because μ is the unknown object of the study. SE is not that error. It describes the spread of an estimator over repeated hypothetical samples under the assumed design. These concepts must not be collapsed: SD describes data dispersion; SE describes estimator dispersion; realized error compares one estimate with its true target. Bias is yet another concept: the expected difference between an estimator and its target, not its SD.

MODULE 04 / 32

04. A miniature population you can enumerate completely

To remove any mystery about a sampling distribution, consider a constructed population consisting of four individuals whose values are 2, 4, 6, and 8 minutes. The population mean is μ=(2+4+6+8)/4=5 minutes. Squared deviations from 5 are 9,1,1,9; hence the population variance, using divisor N=4, is σ²=20/4=5 minutes². The population SD is √5 ≈ 2.2361 minutes. We first select two individuals independently with replacement, so every ordered pair from the four-person population is possible. There are 4²=16 equally probable ordered samples. A person's value can be selected again; this is a mathematical experiment, not a recommendation for a real survey.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[E(\bar X)=\frac{80}{16}=5,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{40}{16}=2.5,\qquad SE=\sqrt{2.5}\]

The sixteen ordered means are 2,3,4,5 for first draw 2; 3,4,5,6 for first draw 4; 4,5,6,7 for first draw 6; and 5,6,7,8 for first draw 8. They add to 80, giving E(X̄)=80/16=5. Their frequencies at values 2,3,4,5,6,7,8 are respectively 1,2,3,4,3,2,1. This full listing is a sampling distribution, not another set of raw individual observations. The squared deviations from 5, weighted by those frequencies, total 40; thus Var(X̄)=40/16=2.5 minutes² and SE(X̄)=√2.5≈1.5811 minutes.

Independently, σ/√n=√5/√2=√2.5. The enumerated calculation and the theoretical formula agree exactly. That agreement is meaningful: we have verified the logic under a completely stated probability mechanism. It does not prove the same formula is valid under nonrandom recruitment, clusters, or sampling without replacement. The mechanism used to select the sample is part of the mathematical statement.

MODULE 05 / 32

05. Deriving the Standard Error of the sample mean

Let X₁,…,Xₙ be random observations sharing finite mean μ and finite variance σ². The sample mean is X̄=(X₁+⋯+Xₙ)/n. By linearity of expectation, E(X̄)=(nμ)/n=μ, whether or not observations are independent, provided the expectations exist. To calculate sampling precision we need the variance. The general identity is Var(ΣXᵢ)=ΣVar(Xᵢ)+2Σ_{i<j}Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ). This identity should be understood before dropping the covariance terms; it is precisely there that many naïve SE calculations go wrong.

Under independence all covariance terms vanish. Under the common-variance assumption, ΣVar(Xᵢ)=nσ². Scaling a random variable by 1/n scales its variance by 1/n², giving Var(X̄)=nσ²/n²=σ²/n. Taking a positive square root produces the theoretical Standard Error of the mean. The √n, rather than n, in the denominator follows from taking the square root of variance. A fourfold increase in independent observations halves the SE; doubling n divides it by √2, not by two.

CORE DERIVATION
\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)&=\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\right)\\&=\frac1{n^2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i)+2\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)\right]\\&=\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\\\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)&=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.\end{aligned}\]

This derivation is an exact variance result under its conditions; it is not an invocation of the Central Limit Theorem. It does not require the observations to be normally distributed. A normal approximation to the shape of the sampling distribution is a separate question, studied in Guide 28. Finite variance, independence, and the relevant sampling design are the present requirements. If the population SD is known to be 12 minutes and n=36 independent observations are drawn, SE=12/6=2 minutes, regardless of whether individual wait times are normally distributed.

MODULE 06 / 32

06. Why the estimated SE is different from the theoretical SE

In real surveys σ is almost never known. Rather than pretending otherwise, we replace it with the sample SD, s, calculated with the n−1 denominator when the ordinary i.i.d. sample-variance estimator is appropriate. The resulting estimated Standard Error is s/√n. This is itself a random statistic: a second survey would produce both a different sample mean and a different estimated SE. Do not call it the exact SE without stating that it is estimated. The theoretical SE refers to the population variance and a specified sampling design; its plug-in estimate uses observed data to approximate the unknown component.

KNOWN VERSUS ESTIMATED
\[\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\bar X)=\frac{s}{\sqrt n},\quad s^2=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}.\]

Return to the six waiting times in Section 03. We obtained s²=14, so s≈3.7417 and estimated SE≈1.5275 minutes. A useful dimension check is that s is measured in minutes, √n is dimensionless, and the SE is measured in minutes. If a report labels the SE in “minutes squared,” it has confused variance with SD. Conversely, if it reports a huge number because it divided by n−1 again, it may have applied the sample-variance correction twice.

The estimation procedure must follow the actual design. The displayed s/√n formula is not a universal correction for any dataset that happens to contain n rows. It is particularly misleading when interviewers collect many correlated responses per household or when repeated measurements on the same individual are treated as different people. Later sections derive those effects rather than merely attaching a cautionary label.

MODULE 07 / 32

07. Standard Deviation versus Standard Error as n changes

Suppose a population has SD σ=12 minutes. For samples of n=9, 36, 144, and 576 independent observations, the theoretical SEs of the mean are respectively 12/3=4, 12/6=2, 12/12=1, and 12/24=0.5 minutes. The underlying individual-level SD stays 12 minutes throughout these calculations. Nothing about collecting more people forces their underlying wait times to become more uniform. The estimator becomes more stable because its average includes more independent information. The contrast should be visible in a table or figure rather than inferred from the common word “spread.”

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\begin{array}{c|rrrr}n&9&36&144&576\\\hline SE(\bar X)\;[\mathrm{min}]&4&2&1&0.5\end{array}\]

If the sample size changes from 36 to 72, the SE changes from 2 to 12/√72≈1.4142, a reduction of about 29.3%, not 50%. If the target is to halve SE, one needs approximately four times as many independent observations when σ remains the same. This is a precision planning relationship, not a promise about representativeness. A bigger convenience sample may still consistently miss the relevant population. Nor can arbitrary multiplication of repeated responses create additional independent people.

For an unknown σ, s may fluctuate with the sample, so the numerical estimated SE does not necessarily decrease monotonically in every realized sequence of datasets. The theoretical 1/√n relationship keeps the population variance and sampling model fixed. A student should recognize the distinction between a mathematical comparison with fixed assumptions and the actual variability of realized sample SDs.

MODULE 08 / 32

08. The sample proportion is an average of indicators

A survey asks whether each resident received a response within one business day: yes=1, no=0. Define Yᵢ as the binary indicator for respondent i. Under independent Bernoulli sampling with success probability p, E(Yᵢ)=p and Var(Yᵢ)=p(1−p). The sample proportion p̂=(Y₁+⋯+Yₙ)/n is exactly a sample mean of these indicators. Applying the mean-variance derivation gives Var(p̂)=p(1−p)/n and the theoretical SE √[p(1−p)/n]. Because p is typically unknown, the usual estimated SE substitutes p̂.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\hat p=\frac{64}{100}=0.64,\qquad\widehat{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{0.64(0.36)}{100}}=0.048\]
PROPORTION STANDARD ERROR
\[\operatorname{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}},\qquad\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}.\]

Suppose 64 of 100 independently selected respondents answer yes. Then p̂=64/100=0.64, with estimated SE=√(0.64·0.36/100)=√0.002304=0.048. That is 4.8 percentage points, because the proportion scale runs from zero to one. It is not 4.8% of 0.64; that would be a relative error. Percentage points and percent differences are distinct units of communication. A report may say “64% answered yes, estimated SE 4.8 percentage points,” while acknowledging the design assumptions.

There is a subtle boundary issue. If all 10 observed respondents answer yes, the plug-in formula gives estimated SE zero, even though a population proportion less than one is plausible. The mathematical plug-in estimate degenerates at p̂=1; it does not establish certainty. For rare events, small n, or proportions near zero or one, naïve symmetric normal intervals are unreliable. Interval construction and alternatives belong to later inferential guides; the central lesson here is to recognize what a computed SE can and cannot claim.

09. Numerical audit of a two-question survey

Consider a constructed survey of twelve independently selected residents. Their service times in minutes are 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18; their yes/no responses to a separate satisfaction question are 1,1,0,1,1,0,1,1,0,1,0,1. Both lists are the complete teaching dataset and are aligned by respondent in the order shown. The total time is 144 minutes, so x̄=12 minutes. The squared deviations around 12 are 49,16,9,4,1,0,1,1,4,9,16,36; they total 146 minutes². Hence s²=146/11≈13.2727 minutes²; s≈3.6432 minutes, and estimated SE=s/√12≈1.0517 minutes.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\begin{aligned}\bar x&=\frac{144}{12}=12\;\mathrm{min},\quad s^2=\frac{146}{11}\approx13.2727\;\mathrm{min}^2,\\\widehat{SE}(\bar X)&=\sqrt{\frac{146}{11\cdot12}}\approx1.0517\;\mathrm{min}.\end{aligned}\]

The indicators sum to eight successes, so p̂=8/12=2/3. The estimated Bernoulli SE is √[(2/3)(1/3)/12]=√(1/54)≈0.1361, or 13.61 percentage points. The mean wait time and the satisfaction proportion are different estimands, even though the same twelve people supplied data. It would be incorrect to report the SD of waiting times as the SE of the proportion or the proportion's SE in minutes. Each parameter has a different estimator and appropriate units.

MODULE 10 / 32

10. Exact finite-sample sampling without replacement

Our earlier four-person population {2,4,6,8} has N=4, μ=5, and population variance σ²=5. This time draw n=2 distinct people uniformly without replacement. There are C(4,2)=6 equally likely unordered samples: {2,4},{2,6},{2,8},{4,6},{4,8},{6,8}. Their means are 3,4,5,5,6,7. The average of these six means is 30/6=5. Their squared deviations from 5 are 4,1,0,0,1,4, with total 10. Therefore the exact sampling variance is 10/6=5/3≈1.6667 minutes² and SE=√(5/3)≈1.2910 minutes.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\{3,4,5,5,6,7\}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{10}{6}=\frac53,\quad SE=\sqrt{\frac53}\]

If we had incorrectly used the with-replacement formula σ/√2=√(5/2)≈1.5811, we would have overstated sampling uncertainty in this design. Why is the without-replacement SE smaller? Once one person has been selected, the available second value is constrained; sampling a high value removes it from future draws. Sample selections become negatively related, and the variance of the average falls. This is not a violation of probability theory but a different probability design.

For simple random sampling without replacement (SRSWOR) from a finite population, the corrected expression using the population variance defined with divisor N is Var(X̄)=[σ²/n]·[(N−n)/(N−1)]. Thus the multiplier on the with-replacement SE is √[(N−n)/(N−1)]. In the example it is √[(4−2)/(4−1)]=√(2/3); multiplying √(5/2) by √(2/3) yields √(5/3). Different textbooks define finite-population variance with denominator N−1 instead; their algebraically equivalent FPC uses √(1−n/N). Always inspect the variance convention before selecting the correction.

FINITE POPULATION CORRECTION
\[\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma_N}{\sqrt n}\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}}\quad\left(\sigma_N^2=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\mu)^2\right).\]
MODULE 11 / 32

11. Units and finite-population fraction

Finite Population Correction (FPC) matters when a substantial fraction of a fixed roster is sampled without replacement. Suppose a roster contains N=200 registered clients and an SRSWOR sample includes n=50 people. Then √[(200−50)/(200−1)]=√(150/199)≈0.8682 using the N-divisor population variance convention. If σ_N=10 minutes, the corrected theoretical SE is (10/√50)·0.8682≈1.2278 minutes, compared with 1.4142 minutes under the with-replacement calculation. This is a meaningful reduction because a quarter of the entire roster was observed.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[SE=\frac{10}{\sqrt{50}}\sqrt{\frac{150}{199}}\approx1.2278\;\mathrm{min}\]

At n=N, the correction becomes zero: if all 200 values are observed without measurement error, the population mean is known exactly for that finite list and sampling variance is zero. That does not eliminate measurement error, nonresponse among those supposedly on the list, or uncertainty about a future population. The estimand matters. If the goal is the mean among people who will visit next year, a complete census of today's roster is not necessarily a census of that future target.

For a large population and small sampling fraction, the correction approaches one. But the finite-population formula is not a cure for clustering or nonprobability recruitment. A survey with 50 people drawn from five households is not equivalent to a simple random sample of 50 independent households. Sampling without replacement and dependence from social proximity are separate features that may operate simultaneously. When a sample survey has unequal selection probabilities, stratification, or cluster stages, specialized design-based variance estimation may be necessary.

MODULE 12 / 32

12. Deriving the effect of covariance on the sample mean

Drop independence but retain a common variance σ². The general formula from Section 05 remains Var(X̄)=[nσ²+2Σ_{i<j}Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ)]/n². Covariance may increase or decrease the sampling variance, depending on its sign and the joint data-generating process. When observations within a group resemble each other, positive covariance means their average contains less independent information than the raw number of entries suggests.

As an instructive equal-correlation model, suppose every distinct pair of observations has correlation ρ and variance σ². Each covariance is ρσ². There are n(n−1)/2 distinct pairs, so twice the sum of pairwise covariances is n(n−1)ρσ². Substitution gives Var(X̄)=σ²/n·[1+(n−1)ρ]. The square bracket is the design effect relative to independent sampling under this simplified exchangeable model. If ρ=0, it equals one. If ρ>0, it exceeds one; the naïve σ/√n understates true precision loss. Negative ρ is mathematically possible within valid correlation bounds, but the formula should not be extrapolated to arbitrary negative values that cannot form a valid covariance matrix.

DEPENDENT OBSERVATIONS
\[\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\big[1+(n-1)\rho\big],\qquad\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\sqrt{1+(n-1)\rho}.\]

This equal-correlation calculation is a conceptual bridge, not a universal design-based SE estimator. Real surveys may have correlations within clusters but independence between clusters, unequal cluster sizes, weights, and varying outcomes. The next section uses the cluster-specific approximation to show how pseudoreplication can produce a falsely reassuring report. The general covariance identity is always the safest mathematical starting point.

MODULE 13 / 32

13. Pseudoreplication: people, households, and interviews

Imagine a study collects five interviews from each of ten households. The raw file has 50 rows, but responses from the same household may share economic circumstances, exposure, and unobserved characteristics. The study therefore has ten clusters, not fifty entirely independent pieces of information. If the within-household Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) is ρ=0.40 and all clusters have size m=5, an approximate cluster design effect is DEFF=1+(m−1)ρ=1+4×0.40=2.6. Relative to an independent-row calculation, the SE is multiplied by √2.6≈1.6125.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[DEFF=1+4(0.4)=2.6,\qquad SE_{\mathrm{cluster}}\approx\frac{10}{\sqrt{50}}\sqrt{2.6}\approx2.2804\]

Take a hypothetical outcome SD of 10 units. A naïve SE using all fifty rows independently is 10/√50≈1.4142 units. With the stated equal-size cluster model, the approximate SE becomes 1.4142×1.6125≈2.2804 units. The effective independent-sample-size heuristic is n_eff≈n/DEFF=50/2.6≈19.23, not 50. This does not mean that 19.23 real people were observed; it is a shorthand for a variance comparison under the assumed correlation structure. The actual sampling design determines the appropriate estimator and degrees of freedom.

CLUSTER DESIGN EFFECT
\[\mathrm{DEFF}\approx1+(m-1)\rho,\quad n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx\frac{n}{\mathrm{DEFF}},\quad\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{cluster}}\approx\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{naive}}\sqrt{\mathrm{DEFF}}.\]

Diagnostic question: what happens if the interviewer duplicates each household response five times in the spreadsheet? The naïve row-based SE shrinks as though new independent evidence appeared, even though no information was collected. This is pseudoreplication. A student must be able to explain why adding correlated rows cannot automatically produce a √5 improvement in precision. Cluster-robust methods, appropriate survey weights, multilevel models, and replication-based survey estimators are developed more fully in specialized regression and survey-design contexts; here the purpose is to identify the mathematical defect in the naïve formula.

MODULE 14 / 32

14. A contrasting example: correlated paired measurements

Dependence does not always enlarge every SE. Consider the difference between two measurements on the same person, such as a before-and-after wait time. Let Dᵢ=Aᵢ−Bᵢ for person i. Within-person covariance enters Var(Dᵢ)=Var(Aᵢ)+Var(Bᵢ)−2Cov(Aᵢ,Bᵢ). Positive covariance between before and after values can reduce variability of their differences, making a paired analysis more precise than an independent-groups comparison. The target estimator is now the average of differences, D̄, not the difference between two unrelated samples.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\bar d=3,\quad s_D^2=\frac{10}{4}=2.5,\quad\widehat{SE}(\bar D)=\sqrt{\frac{2.5}{5}}\approx0.7071\;\mathrm{min}\]

For example, suppose the complete set of differences in minutes for five people is 2, 4, 1, 3, 5. Their average is D̄=15/5=3 minutes. Their deviations from 3 are −1,1,−2,0,2; squared deviations total 1+1+4+0+4=10. Thus s_D²=10/4=2.5 minutes², s_D≈1.5811 minutes, and estimated SE(D̄)=s_D/√5≈0.7071 minutes, assuming the person-level differences are independent across people.

Applying an independent-groups SE formula to ten values from these five people ignores pairing and changes the estimand and covariance structure. Similarly, estimating two separate means and subtracting them requires their covariance to compute the variance of the difference. The larger lesson is not “correlation is always bad” but “the correct SE is derived for the estimator and dependence pattern actually used.” It is legitimate to exploit paired design when it reflects the question; it is not legitimate to pretend repeated measurements are independent participants.

MODULE 15 / 32

15. Standard Error is not a probability statement about the parameter

A Standard Error is a scale of sampling variability. The number s/√n alone is neither a probability that the parameter lies near the estimate nor a guarantee of a specified coverage percentage. The parameter is fixed under the ordinary frequentist framework; the estimator is random across repeated samples. To turn a Standard Error into a confidence interval or test, we need a justified reference distribution, an uncertainty-estimation method, and conditions that connect the statistic with that distribution.

If outcomes are normally distributed and independent, the sample mean is normal; when σ is unknown, uncertainty procedures often involve a t distribution and additional degrees-of-freedom reasoning. If outcomes have a nonnormal distribution with finite variance, the exact Var(X̄)=σ²/n still holds under independence even though the sampling distribution of X̄ might be far from normal when n is small. For certain settings a Central Limit Theorem supports an approximation when n grows; that topic comes next in Guide 28. Therefore “the SE formula is valid” and “a normal interval is accurate” are different mathematical claims.

Do not confuse uncertainty with bias. In a convenience survey of only smartphone users, the estimated mean may have a small SE for that recruitment mechanism while remaining an invalid estimate of the mean for all residents. Standard Error does not measure coverage error, survey nonresponse bias, interviewer effects, or systematic miscalibration. Those problems require attention to the estimand and how the data were generated. A careful report states both precision conditional on its design and threats to generalization.

MODULE 16 / 32

16. A simple reproducible simulation, and what it checks

A simulation can visually reinforce a proven sampling-variance identity, but it cannot replace the derivation. Consider the population of Section 04, {2,4,6,8}, and repeatedly draw two observations independently with replacement. The exact sixteen ordered outcomes give E(X̄)=5 and SE=√2.5. A computer simulation with many independent replications would generate observed means near this same distribution; a histogram would show more concentration around 5 than the individual observations and would converge in frequency toward the exact counts 1,2,3,4,3,2,1.

If you simulate 10,000 independent sample means, the empirical SD of those 10,000 means approximates the theoretical SE of one two-person mean. Dividing that empirical SD by √10,000 estimates the Monte Carlo error of the simulation's average, a completely different quantity. This distinction is essential. Repetition can make a numerical calculation of a known sampling distribution accurate, but does not change how variable one original two-person survey would be. Similarly, running a flawed sampling design a million times does not remove its bias toward a wrong target.

For an independent reproducibility check without software, use the exact count of each of the sixteen outcomes; multiply each squared deviation by its frequency; divide by sixteen; and take the square root. This is the pedagogical value of a small, complete population. Larger computational experiments later use the same conceptual operations, but their statistical claims must be traced back to the data-generating model.

MODULE 17 / 32

17. Why averaging highly skewed data still has an SE

Imagine daily expenses that are usually modest but occasionally very high. The distribution of individual observations is strongly right-skewed. If its variance is finite and successive independent observations share the same distribution, the exact SE of their arithmetic mean remains σ/√n. It is not necessary to pretend the raw observations form a bell curve. The variance calculation depends on linearity, scaling, covariance, and finite moments—not on a picture of the data histogram.

However, a small-sample mean from a skewed distribution can also have a skewed sampling distribution. A Gaussian-looking standard-error band around the estimate is therefore not guaranteed to have the intended probability coverage. A further limitation arises if extremely heavy tails make the population variance infinite; then the ordinary theoretical σ/√n expression is not a finite quantity. One should not routinely apply the familiar formula without checking that the target variance exists and the observations are sufficiently comparable for the assumed model.

If the question is better answered by a median or another robust estimator, its Standard Error requires its own derivation or valid estimation method. It is incorrect to compute s/√n and attach it to any arbitrary statistic. For the mean, the derivation above explains exactly why the formula works. For a sample quantile or trimmed mean, the corresponding sampling distribution is different. This observation links Standard Error back to Guide 26: the uncertainty statement must follow the chosen estimand and estimator, not the convenience of a spreadsheet function.

MODULE 18 / 32

18. Unequal measurement quality and weighted estimators

Suppose two independent instruments produce unbiased measurements of the same fixed quantity θ, but their variances differ: Var(X₁)=4 and Var(X₂)=9 units². An equal-weight estimate is T=(X₁+X₂)/2. Its variance is (4+9)/4=13/4=3.25 units², giving SE≈1.8028 units. The measurements are independent but not identically distributed, so writing “σ/√2” without specifying σ is ambiguous. One must compute the variance of the estimator directly.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[w_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac9{13},\qquad\operatorname{Var}(T_w)=\frac{36}{13},\qquad SE(T_w)=\sqrt{\frac{36}{13}}\approx1.6641\]

Consider instead a weighted average T_w=wX₁+(1−w)X₂. Its expectation is θ for any weights summing to one. By independence, its variance is 4w²+9(1−w)². Differentiating with respect to w yields 8w−18(1−w)=26w−18; setting this to zero gives w=18/26=9/13 for the more precise first instrument. The second weight is 4/13. The resulting variance is 4(9/13)²+9(4/13)²=36/13≈2.7692 units² and SE≈1.6641 units. Thus the choice of estimator can change the SE even when the underlying measurements and estimand are fixed.

UNEQUAL-VARIANCE WEIGHTING
\[\operatorname{Var}(T_w)=w^2\sigma_1^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_2^2,\qquad w_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}.\]

This is not a generic recipe to give larger weights to more appealing observations. It assumes independence, unbiasedness for a common θ, and known variances. In real surveys, unequal selection probabilities create a different reason for weights, and their SE requires an appropriate sampling-design calculation. The purpose of this example is to show how Standard Error emerges from the mathematical form of an estimator.

MODULE 19 / 32

19. Separate random-sampling variability from data-collection problems

A good Standard Error is not a certificate of quality for the data that enter the estimator. Consider a customer survey with a high response rate among daytime visitors but almost no coverage of evening visitors. A naïve i.i.d. formula may measure how the daytime sample mean would fluctuate under repeated daytime recruitment, yet the target is the mean across all visitors. Even an enormous sample of daytime visitors can give a very small estimated SE while failing to estimate the intended overall mean. The discrepancy is a selection or coverage problem, not merely sampling noise.

Measurement error introduces another distinction. If every recorded wait time is inflated by exactly three minutes because the system clock is misconfigured, the sample mean is shifted by three minutes. Its SE might be unchanged because adding a constant changes the mean but not variance. Therefore the reported precision can look excellent while the point estimate is systematically wrong. If measurement errors are random and independent, they can increase the population variance and the SE; if they are shared within a device or office, they can introduce covariance. The direction depends on the mechanism.

A defensible statistical explanation asks: who is in the target population, how were units selected, which measurements are independent, what is the estimand, and how was variability computed? Only after these questions can an SE be interpreted as evidence about precision. The same logic applies to scientific experiments, educational surveys, and business analytics, even though their application-specific variance estimators differ.

MODULE 20 / 32

20. Precision requirements and responsible sample-size planning

Suppose a preliminary independent survey suggests σ≈12 minutes and the researcher wants the theoretical SE of the mean to be at most 1.5 minutes. Starting from σ/√n≤1.5, square both sides: σ²/n≤2.25. Rearranging, n≥σ²/2.25=144/2.25=64. Thus at least 64 independent observations would satisfy the target if σ=12 remains reasonable and the sampling assumptions hold. This calculation does not specify a confidence level because it is a target for SE itself, not for an interval's margin of error.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[n\ge\frac{12^2}{1.5^2}=64,\qquad n_{\mathrm{cluster}}\gtrsim64(2.6)=166.4\Rightarrow167\]
PRECISION TARGET
\[\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le e\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\ge\left(\frac{\sigma}{e}\right)^2.\]

For a sample proportion, a conservative planning value uses p(1−p)≤1/4. If desired SE≤0.05, then √[0.25/n]≤0.05 requires n≥0.25/0.0025=100 independent Bernoulli observations. This is a bound over possible p under the basic model. It is not an appropriate final design calculation for an unequal-probability complex survey without its design effect, nonresponse allowance, or finite-population features.

With cluster design effect DEFF≈2.6, the rough number of sampled observations needed for the same mean-SE target rises from 64 independent observations to approximately 64×2.6=166.4, or 167 rows, under the model's assumptions; one must then also ensure a sufficient number of independent clusters. A larger nominal n does not guarantee independent precision. Sample-size arithmetic is conditional planning, not a substitute for a valid sampling protocol.

MODULE 21 / 32

21. Distinguishing an SE, an SD, a variance, and a margin of error

These four quantities can appear next to the same survey summary, yet answer different questions. Variance is an average squared deviation and has squared units. SD is the square root of variance and reports dispersion of individual values in original units. SE is the SD of a statistic's sampling distribution and reports how the statistic would fluctuate across repeated samples. A margin of error, when justified by a particular inferential procedure, generally multiplies an SE by an appropriate reference cutoff and may include additional design features. SE alone is not a margin of error.

For example, a sample reports mean wait time 12 minutes, SD≈3.6432 minutes, and estimated SE≈1.0517 minutes. These three quantities coexist without contradiction. A person might wait much longer or shorter than 12 minutes even though the estimate of the mean is relatively stable. Reporting “12 ± 1.05 minutes” as a confidence interval would be unjustified without defining what the ± means and the probability procedure it represents. Likewise, the empirical difference between one sample mean and another is not automatically an SE.

A helpful reading strategy is to check units and the entity that varies. “Wait times vary between residents” signals SD; “the estimate varies between possible samples” signals SE. “How far is this particular sample estimate from the unknown parameter?” signals error, which is generally unobservable. “Which range has a specified long-run confidence coverage?” signals a later inference method, not just a numerical calculation of SE. These distinctions protect both mathematical reasoning and public-facing communication.

MODULE 22 / 32

22. A rigorous checklist for choosing the correct SE

Before computing, write the estimand in words and symbols. Is it a mean, a proportion, a paired difference, or a weighted quantity? Define the observational unit and the sampling unit. Ten classrooms with twenty students each may constitute ten independent sampled clusters, not 200 independently selected students. Document whether observations were collected with replacement, without replacement from a fixed roster, through stratified sampling, through clusters, or by convenience. The correct variance formula depends on that design.

Next write the estimator algebraically. For an independent mean, X̄=ΣXᵢ/n; for a proportion, p̂=ΣYᵢ/n; for a paired effect, D̄=ΣDᵢ/n; for a weighted estimate, ΣwᵢXᵢ. Then identify random components and their covariance structure. Derive Var(T), take its positive square root, and specify whether the unknown population quantities were substituted with estimates. Check that the computed answer has the appropriate units and a plausible magnitude.

Finally ask whether the sample-selection mechanism actually supports generalization to the target. Distinguish the measured SE from errors it omits: selection bias, nonresponse, mismeasurement, or violations of independence. If the design is complicated and no justified variance estimator is available, the appropriate statement is “the SE has not been validly estimated”, not an unsupported use of s/√n. This is a constructive research outcome: it reveals what additional design information or methodological support is needed.

25. Solution 1 — Mean, SD, and estimated SE

The complete observations are 4,6,8,10,12,14 minutes, with n=6. Their sum is 54, so x̄=54/6=9 minutes. Subtracting the mean gives deviations −5,−3,−1,1,3,5. Their sum is zero, an immediate check that the computed mean is correct. Squaring yields 25,9,1,1,9,25 minutes², totaling 70 minutes². Sample variance uses divisor n−1=5: s²=70/5=14 minutes². The sample SD is s=√14≈3.7417 minutes. The estimated Standard Error of the sample mean is s/√6=√(14/6)=√(7/3)≈1.5275 minutes.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\bar x=\frac{54}{6}=9,\quad s^2=\frac{70}{5}=14,\quad\widehat{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{14}{6}}\approx1.5275\;\mathrm{min}\]

The SD expresses dispersion among the six individual waits. The estimated SE expresses the sampling variability of the mean estimator, conditional on a plausible independent sampling model. It does not state that every person waits within 1.5275 minutes of the mean; several observations demonstrably lie farther away. It also does not state that the population mean must be within 1.5275 minutes of 9: the quantity is an estimated spread of hypothetical sample means, not an observed error or guaranteed confidence bound. The squared-unit check distinguishes variance, 14 minutes², from SD and SE, both in minutes.

Transfer: If these six values were six repeated timing attempts for one individual, they would not constitute six independent random residents. The arithmetic for s would still be correct as a description of those timings, but s/√6 would not automatically be the SE of an estimate for the mean across all residents. The population being targeted and the sampling independence must be reconsidered.

26. Solution 2 — Full sampling distribution by enumeration

The population {0,2,4} has μ=(0+2+4)/3=2 and population variance σ²=[(−2)²+0²+2²]/3=8/3. With replacement and ordered n=2 sampling, the nine pairs yield means: (0,0)→0; (0,2)→1; (0,4)→2; (2,0)→1; (2,2)→2; (2,4)→3; (4,0)→2; (4,2)→3; (4,4)→4. Thus the nine mean values are 0,1,2,1,2,3,2,3,4, each with probability 1/9. They sum to 18, so E(X̄)=18/9=2. This verifies unbiasedness for μ under the stated experiment.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{12}{9}=\frac43=\frac{(8/3)}2,\quad SE=\frac2{\sqrt3}\approx1.1547\]

The squared deviations around 2 are 4,1,0,1,0,1,0,1,4. Their sum is 12; dividing by nine yields Var(X̄)=12/9=4/3, and SE(X̄)=√(4/3)=2/√3≈1.1547. Independently, the theoretical formula predicts σ²/n=(8/3)/2=4/3. The enumeration and formula agree exactly. The nine numbers are sampling estimates, not an expanded population sample to which one should apply an n−1 variance formula: their probabilities are determined by the enumerated sampling experiment.

Transfer: If the draws were without replacement, pairs (0,0), (2,2), and (4,4) would be impossible, while the remaining ordered pairs would have probability 1/6 rather than 1/9. The sampling distribution and its SE would change, even though the underlying three population values remain identical.

27. Solution 3 — A Bernoulli proportion with units

There are n=75 independently sampled binary responses, 45 yes and 30 no. The sample proportion is p̂=45/75=0.60. Its plug-in variance estimate on the proportion scale is p̂(1−p̂)/n=0.60×0.40/75=0.24/75=0.0032. Taking the square root gives estimated SE=√0.0032≈0.0565685, which is 5.6569 percentage points. We therefore report approximately 60% yes, with estimated SE 5.66 percentage points, provided the independent Bernoulli model appropriately describes how the sample was obtained.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\hat p=\frac{45}{75}=0.6,\quad\widehat{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{0.6(0.4)}{75}}\approx0.05657\]

Do not describe this as “5.66% of the estimate”: 5.66 percentage points corresponds to 0.0566 on a zero-to-one scale, while 5.66% of 0.60 would be only 0.034. The difference is substantive. A small SE under a flawed sampling frame still leaves selection bias. For instance, if only smartphone owners were contacted, the resulting p̂ might not target the proportion among all residents. The formula quantifies sampling fluctuation under a probability mechanism; it cannot repair missing groups.

Transfer: If the 75 responses came from 15 families with five family members each, dependence would require attention to within-family correlation. The nominal n remains 75 rows, but the naïve plug-in SE could be too small. Revisit Section 13 before making a claim about precision.

28. Solution 4 — Finite population and exact correction

The fixed population is {1,3,5,7}, so μ=(1+3+5+7)/4=4. Its squared deviations are 9,1,1,9, total 20; σ_N²=20/4=5. The six distinct unordered samples of size two have means: {1,3}→2, {1,5}→3, {1,7}→4, {3,5}→4, {3,7}→5, {5,7}→6. Their mean is (2+3+4+4+5+6)/6=24/6=4. Squared deviations from four are 4,1,0,0,1,4, adding to 10. Therefore Var(X̄)=10/6=5/3 and SE=√(5/3)≈1.2910 in the units of the original observations.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{5}{2}\frac{4-2}{4-1}=\frac53,\quad SE=\sqrt{\frac53}\approx1.2910\]

The without-replacement correction gives Var(X̄)=(σ_N²/n)×[(N−n)/(N−1)]=(5/2)×[(4−2)/(4−1)]=(5/2)×(2/3)=5/3. Both the enumeration and the design formula agree. The with-replacement variance would instead be 5/2, larger by a factor of 3/2. A smaller variance here is not an estimator that “works better by luck”; it reflects the different selection mechanism that prevents the same value from being drawn twice.

Transfer: A complete census would have n=N=4 and a sampling SE of zero for this fixed population. That would still not say anything automatically about future populations or measurement-error uncertainty. Always specify the target whose mean is being estimated.

29. Solution 5 — Household clustering and effective n

The survey contains 12 households with 4 responses each, so n=12×4=48 rows. Under the assumed outcome SD of 8 units, naïve independence gives SE_naive=8/√48≈1.1547 units. But within-household ICC=0.25 and equal cluster size m=4 yield DEFF≈1+(4−1)×0.25=1+0.75=1.75. Therefore the cluster-adjusted approximation is SE_cluster≈1.1547×√1.75≈1.5275 units. The effective-size heuristic is n_eff≈48/1.75≈27.43 independent observations' worth of mean precision under the specified model.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[DEFF=1+3(0.25)=1.75,\quad SE_{\mathrm{cluster}}\approx\frac8{\sqrt{48}}\sqrt{1.75}\approx1.5275\]

The variance inflation emerges algebraically because responses from a household contribute positive covariance terms to the variance of the overall mean. If an analyst counts each row as independent, those terms are omitted. The calculation is a model-based illustration, not a substitute for actual design-based methods when clusters differ in size, households are sampled with unequal probabilities, or strata are present. A precise numerical estimate with an inapplicable formula is not trustworthy.

Transfer: Suppose respondents in one household share a systematically mistaken timing device. More interviews within that household may increase the number of rows without reducing that shared error. The SE formula also does not detect households excluded from the survey frame. Thus correcting for clustering addresses a precision component; it does not establish absence of bias or valid coverage.

30. Solution 6 — Paired changes use independent persons

The six person-level before-after differences are 1,2,1,4,3,1 minutes. Their sum is 12, giving D̄=12/6=2 minutes. Subtracting 2 gives deviations −1,0,−1,2,1,−1. Their squared deviations are 1,0,1,4,1,1, totaling 8 minutes². The sample variance of differences is s_D²=8/(6−1)=8/5=1.6 minutes². The SD of differences is s_D=√1.6≈1.2649 minutes, and estimated SE(D̄)=√(1.6/6)=√(4/15)≈0.5164 minutes.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\bar d=\frac{12}{6}=2,\quad s_D^2=\frac85=1.6,\quad\widehat{SE}(\bar D)=\sqrt{\frac{1.6}{6}}\approx0.5164\]

The appropriate independent units are the six people. Each contributed two measurements, but the estimator of the paired effect uses six within-person differences. Treating twelve raw measurements as twelve unrelated people discards the paired structure, ignores covariance, and may answer a different question. Positive within-person correlation can improve precision of a difference, which is why dependence cannot always be summarized as automatic SE inflation.

Transfer: If these six people all belonged to the same household, independent differences across people would also be questionable. Pairing removes one within-person source of variation; it does not eliminate possible dependence across distinct people. A second covariance structure would require consideration.

31. Solution 7 — Critical appraisal and defensible redesign

The statement “the mean for all residents is within one minute of 16” is unsupported for several reasons. First, app users may not represent all residents; coverage and selection bias can remain regardless of sample size. Second, the report assumes 200 independent observations, but repeat app visits or households can create dependence. Third, the reported 0.707 is an estimated SE under the unverified i.i.d. model, not an observed bound on error or a confidence interval. Fourth, the mean and SD may be distorted by nonresponse, measurement error, skewness, or a mismatch between the recruitment frame and the intended population. Fifth, “within one minute” has no declared confidence procedure or error criterion. A single SE cannot support that deterministic guarantee.

WORKED CALCULATION
\[\widehat{SE}_{\mathrm{naive}}=\frac{10}{\sqrt{200}}\approx0.7071\;\mathrm{min}\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad\lvert\bar X-\mu\rvert\le1\;\mathrm{min}\]

The arithmetic itself is 10/√200≈10/14.1421≈0.7071 minutes. That is correct conditional on s=10 and independent rows—but correct arithmetic does not validate incorrect assumptions. A defensible plan would identify the target group and period, obtain a probability-based sampling frame covering residents with and without apps, record the actual selection probabilities and nonresponse, document how repeat users are handled, and derive an SE that matches the final design. The researcher would distinguish estimated sampling uncertainty from possible coverage or measurement bias and would only construct an interval using a method justified by the sampling distribution and the study conditions.

Transfer: If the same 200 interviews were collected from 20 offices with ten related respondents per office, office clustering would have to enter the SE calculation. Even a perfect cluster correction would not solve the app-only coverage problem. This is precisely why Standard Error, uncertainty statements, and research validity must be interpreted as connected but distinct questions.

MODULE 32 / 32

32. Final synthesis: explain why the calculation works

Standard Error is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of an estimator. It answers how much an estimator may fluctuate across repeated valid samples. Its relationship to individual SD is not a slogan or a shortcut: it comes from Var(ΣXᵢ)=ΣVar(Xᵢ)+2ΣCov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ) and the 1/n² scaling of a sample mean. For independent identical observations, covariances vanish and the familiar σ/√n follows. For an estimated SE, σ is replaced by a defensible estimate such as s. For a Bernoulli proportion, the indicator variance p(1−p) yields the corresponding expression.

The same logic explains why the formula changes. Without-replacement sampling introduces negative dependence and a finite-population correction. Positive within-cluster correlation inflates the variance; pairing can reduce the variance of a difference because the target statistic contains a subtraction. Nonidentical measurements or weighted estimators demand direct variance calculations. No single row-count formula survives all these settings unaltered. The correct SE follows the estimand, the estimator, and the sampling mechanism.

Before leaving this guide, check that you can (1) distinguish σ, s, and SE by role and units; (2) enumerate a small sampling distribution and reproduce its variance; (3) derive the independent-mean and proportion formulas; (4) calculate an FPC or cluster design effect and state its conditions; and (5) identify at least one form of bias or design failure that a small SE cannot fix. If you can do these steps and explain each operation aloud, you have the foundation needed for Guide 28's study of the Central Limit Theorem and later statistical inference. If a step is unclear, revisit the example and reproduce its arithmetic rather than memorizing its conclusion.
