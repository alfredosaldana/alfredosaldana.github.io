1. The question behind an apparently precise regression

A regression can draw a convincing straight line and still give misleading uncertainty. Imagine comparing eight companies of different operational sizes. A small firm's monthly expenditure can fluctuate by relatively modest amounts; a large firm can experience much larger departures from its expected spending because one delayed shipment or one large contract changes the total. A fitted line describes the conditional mean, but the distance of observations from that line may expand with size. The issue is not simply whether the line fits: it is whether we are entitled to use one common error variance to quantify how uncertain its slope is.

This guide distinguishes three questions that are often collapsed. Estimation asks which coefficients minimize the sum of squared vertical residuals. Inference asks how much estimated coefficients might change across repeated samples from the underlying population. Model adequacy asks whether the mean function, independence, sampling process, and assumptions needed for an inference procedure are defensible. A heteroscedasticity-consistent standard error chiefly addresses the second question when one particular part of the usual covariance formula is wrong. It does not automatically repair the first or third.

The prerequisites are elementary random-variable variance (Guide 13), sampling uncertainty (Guide 27), multiple regression (Guide 52), and regression diagnostics (Guide 54). No previous matrix-algebra course is assumed: we will introduce every row, column, transpose, inverse, and dimension before multiplying matrices. The full calculations use eight observations so that every result is reconstructible with arithmetic, a scientific calculator, and careful checking. All numbers in the teaching dataset are synthetic and invented solely for explanation; they are not evidence about real firms.

Learning outcomes. By the end, you should be able to diagnose a plausible unequal-variance pattern without declaring it proven, derive classical and HC3 coefficient covariance matrices, compute and compare uncertainty intervals, explain when weighting is justified, and defend sensitivity checks while recognizing problems that neither HC3 nor weighting solves. Every advanced claim will be labeled either an algebraic identity developed here or a theorem whose general validity depends on stated assumptions.

2. Data, units, design, and the estimand

Our synthetic observational units are eight independent companies, one company per row. Let x be an operational-size index in units from 1 to 8, and let y be monthly operating expenditure in thousands of dollars. The size index is not a dollar amount. We assume that each company is represented exactly once and that the observations do not share a parent organization or recurring measurement date. The sample was deliberately constructed to illustrate large residuals at the high end; it is not a random sample that independently verifies any statistical hypothesis.

We will estimate \(E(Y\mid X=x)=\beta_0+\beta_1 x\), a conditional mean model, not a guaranteed value for every firm. The slope \(\beta_1\) is the expected increase in thousands of dollars per one-unit increase of the size index, conditional on using this particular linear mean specification. This is an association, not a causal effect of making a company larger. The intercept \(\beta_0\) concerns index zero, outside the observed range, so its operational interpretation is limited even though it remains essential to the fitted equation.

The errors \(u_i=Y_i-E(Y_i\mid X_i)\) represent deviations from the population conditional mean; they are unobserved. Residuals \(e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i\) are computed after estimating coefficients; they are observable approximations, not identical to population errors. Keeping this distinction prevents a common error: declaring that we know the population error variance just because we can calculate sample residuals. The distinction matters especially when the squared residuals are used as plug-in estimates in robust covariance calculations.

3. Compute OLS without a software black box

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) selects intercept \(b_0\) and slope \(b_1\) to minimize \(SSE=\sum_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2\). For a regression with an intercept and one predictor, the normal equations imply \(b_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) and \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\). These are exact algebraic results of minimizing the sample squared error when the predictor has nonzero variation.

Here \(n=8\), \(\sum x_i=36\), \(\sum y_i=142\), \(\bar x=4.5\), \(\bar y=17.75\), \(\sum x_i^2=204\), and \(\sum x_iy_i=747\). Therefore \(S_{xx}=204-36^2/8=42\). The cross-product is \(S_{xy}=747-(36)(142)/8=108\). Divide: \(b_1=108/42=18/7=2.57142857\), in thousands of dollars per index unit. Subtract \(b_1\bar x\) from the sample mean: \(b_0=17.75-(18/7)(4.5)=173/28\approx6.17857143\), in thousands of dollars.

OLS fitted mean
\[\widehat y_i=6.17857143+2.57142857x_i\]

For company A, \(\widehat y_1=6.17857143+2.57142857=8.75\); the residual is \(10-8.75=1.25\) thousand dollars. For company H, \(\widehat y_8=6.17857143+8(2.57142857)=26.75\), and its residual is \(30-26.75=3.25\). Notice that the last residual exceeds the first even though both firms follow the same fitted line; that observation motivates diagnosis but by itself does not establish heteroscedasticity.

4. What heteroscedasticity means, and what it does not mean

The classical equal-variance assumption states that \(\operatorname{Var}(u_i\mid X)=\sigma^2\) for every observation. Heteroscedasticity instead permits \(\operatorname{Var}(u_i\mid X)=\sigma_i^2\), with different conditional variances. The subscript emphasizes that uncertainty can depend on company size or other characteristics. Variance is measured in squared thousands of dollars; its square root, a standard deviation, returns to thousands of dollars. A large mean does not automatically imply large variance, and a large observed residual does not, by itself, identify the population variance at that predictor value.

Heteroscedasticity can arise from scale: enterprises with larger budgets might have larger absolute fluctuations. It can also reflect omitted groups, mixtures of processes, measurement precision changing with the predictor, or an incorrect mean function. These mechanisms have different practical remedies. The first might justify a variance model; a missing nonlinear trend might require improving \(E(Y\mid X)\); a group mixture might require additional predictors; repeated measures might call for clustered covariance or a longitudinal model.

If the conditional mean is correctly specified and \(E(u_i\mid X)=0\), OLS coefficients can remain unbiased conditional on the design even with unequal error variances. Under appropriate regularity conditions, they may still be consistent. But the usual textbook OLS standard-error calculation is generally unreliable because it substitutes one common variance for a collection of different variances. This is a statement about the sampling distribution of estimated coefficients, not a claim that every estimated slope must change. With the data and fitted model held fixed, selecting HC3 changes the covariance estimate and inferential quantities, not \(b_0\), \(b_1\), \(\widehat y\), residuals or \(R^2\).

Heteroscedasticity does not mean errors are correlated with each other. Unequal variances concern the diagonal entries of an error covariance matrix; cross-observation dependence concerns its off-diagonal entries. A robust estimator that corrects only the diagonal cannot be assumed to handle serial correlation or clustering. This distinction will matter again when we discuss branches, families, and measurements over time.

5. Learn the matrix language from dimensions

Write the eight observed expenditures as a column vector \(y\) with dimensions \(8\times1\). The design matrix \(X\) has eight rows and two columns: column one contains eight ones for the intercept, and column two contains the size indices 1 to 8. Thus \(X\) is \(8\times2\). The coefficient vector \(\beta=(\beta_0,\beta_1)^\mathsf T\) is \(2\times1\), so \(X\beta\) is \(8\times1\), matching \(y\). A transpose \(X^\mathsf T\) swaps rows and columns, making it \(2\times8\).

Matrix form of the regression
\[y=X\beta+u,\qquad \widehat\beta=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf Ty\]

A square matrix inverse is the matrix analogue of division; it exists here because the intercept column and varying predictor column are linearly independent. The normal equations are \(X^\mathsf TX\widehat\beta=X^\mathsf Ty\). In this example, \(X^\mathsf TX=\begin{pmatrix}8&36\\36&204\end{pmatrix}\). Its determinant is \(8(204)-36^2=336\neq0\), so the inverse exists and is \(\frac1{336}\begin{pmatrix}204&-36\\-36&8\end{pmatrix}\). Its entries are approximately 0.607143, -0.107143, -0.107143, and 0.0238095.

The dimension check matters. \(X^\mathsf TX\) is \(2\times2\), its inverse is \(2\times2\), \(X^\mathsf Ty\) is \(2\times1\), and their product produces two coefficients. The inverse does not convert data into causal evidence or guarantee a well-specified model: it only solves the least-squares algebra provided the design has full column rank. Later we will put an error covariance matrix in the middle; checking its dimensions prevents guessing a formula from memory.

6. Derive the coefficient covariance before changing the assumption

Start with the fitted OLS estimator \(\widehat\beta=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf Ty\). Substitute the population model \(y=X\beta+u\): then \(\widehat\beta=\beta+(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf Tu\). This identity explains where estimation uncertainty comes from. Across hypothetical repetitions of the sample mechanism with design \(X\) held fixed, different error vectors produce different estimates, even if the true coefficients stay the same.

Let \(\Omega=\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)\), an \(8\times8\) covariance matrix. The general covariance identity for a fixed matrix \(A\) is \(\operatorname{Var}(Au\mid X)=A\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)A^\mathsf T\). Apply it with \(A=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T\). Since \(X^\mathsf TX\) is symmetric, its inverse is symmetric as well. We obtain the general covariance formula:

General OLS coefficient covariance
\[\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta\mid X)=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T\Omega X(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\]

Each multiplication has compatible dimensions: \((2\times2)(2\times8)(8\times8)(8\times2)(2\times2)\) gives a \(2\times2\) matrix. Its first diagonal entry is the intercept variance, its second diagonal entry is the slope variance, and the off-diagonal entries describe how those two estimators co-vary across repetitions. A standard error is the nonnegative square root of a diagonal entry. Do not confuse a coefficient standard error with the standard deviation of the outcome.

If independent or conditionally uncorrelated errors have the same variance, then \(\Omega=\sigma^2I_8\), where the identity matrix has ones along its diagonal and zeros elsewhere. Substituting \(X^\mathsf T I_8X=X^\mathsf TX\) cancels one factor and yields \(\sigma^2(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\). This is the textbook shortcut. With unequal variances, the cancellation fails. That, rather than an arbitrary preference for a newer standard-error label, is the mathematical reason we need a different estimator.

7. Calculate classical standard errors and interpret the baseline

The sample has \(n=8\) observations and \(p=2\) estimated coefficients. The conventional residual-variance estimate is \(s^2=SSE/(n-p)=27.78571429/6=4.63095238\) squared thousands of dollars. We divide by six, not eight, because the fitted intercept and slope impose two constraints on the residuals. The number six is the residual degrees of freedom. The estimated residual standard deviation is \(s=\sqrt{4.63095238}\approx2.1520\) thousand dollars.

Multiply the matrix inverse from Section 5 by \(s^2\). The classical estimated coefficient covariance is \(s^2(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2.811650&-0.496173\\-0.496173&0.110261\end{pmatrix}\). The diagonal entries are variances in squared coefficient units. Thus \(SE_{classic}(b_0)=\sqrt{2.811650}=1.676797\) thousand dollars, while \(SE_{classic}(b_1)=\sqrt{0.110261}=0.332055\) thousand dollars per size-index unit.

Classical covariance shortcut
\[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{classic}(\widehat\beta)=s^2(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1},\qquad s^2=\frac{\sum_i e_i^2}{n-p}\]

With six residual degrees of freedom, the 97.5th percentile of Student's t distribution is approximately \(t_{0.975,6}=2.446912\). The conventional 95% two-sided interval for the slope is \(2.571429\pm2.446912(0.332055)\), or approximately \([1.758918,3.383939]\). Under the full classical assumptions, this is a conventional t interval. Without equal error variance, plugging this standard error into that t formula is not automatically justified, even though the arithmetic is correct.

The t statistic for testing zero slope under the conventional method is \(2.571429/0.332055\approx7.74398\). Its numerical p-value under a t distribution with six degrees of freedom is about 0.000244. This number is conditional on a specified inferential reference distribution and assumptions; it is not the probability the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value alone cannot diagnose whether unequal variance invalidates the chosen reference procedure.

8. Diagnose a pattern before choosing a correction

A useful first plot puts fitted expenditure on the horizontal axis and residuals on the vertical axis, marking a horizontal zero line. If error variance increases with expected expenditure, a fan-shaped vertical spread can appear. But eight observations are only eight observations: our chosen sample includes a large negative residual at x=6 and a large positive residual at x=8, not enough to establish a population variance function. Diagnose the pattern as suggestive, then ask whether there are known structural reasons for unequal spread.

Plotting squared residuals versus fitted values emphasizes magnitude at the cost of hiding positive and negative signs. A scale-location plot uses the square root of absolute standardized residuals and may reveal changing spread. Inspect results by groups as well, since two populations with different means can create a pattern that disappears after improving the mean model. Repeat the diagnostics after alternative plausible mean specifications. The purpose is to discover conditions under which an inference method becomes untrustworthy, not to manufacture a pretext to remove inconvenient observations.

A formal test such as Breusch–Pagan can supplement plots: its null concerns a structured equal-variance hypothesis and its calibration depends on assumptions and versions of the test. Tests can have little power with eight observations and need not detect all forms of unequal variance. Selecting HC3 should not require a significant preliminary test, particularly when robust uncertainty was chosen as a defensible planned default. Testing first and choosing a standard error only after seeing a desired p-value introduces data-dependent selection that should be disclosed.

Practical decision: report the classical and robust calculations side by side, describe the qualitative residual pattern, state the sample size, and explain why HC3 was chosen. If you decide to model the variance itself, do so because your scientific or measurement context supports that structure, not merely because a residual scatterplot looks irregular.

9. Why the sandwich estimator has three pieces

Assume the individual errors are conditionally independent or at least uncorrelated across companies but may have different conditional variances. Then \(\Omega\) is diagonal, with entries \(\sigma_1^2,\ldots,\sigma_8^2\). We do not observe those population error variances. A heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC) covariance estimator replaces the unknown matrix by a diagonal matrix computed from observed residuals, with a convention for small-sample or leverage adjustment.

The multiplication looks like a sandwich: \((X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\) on the left and right are the bread, while \(X^\mathsf T\widehat\Omega X\) is the meat. This naming is not a metaphor for robustness against every violation. The outer matrices translate observation-level error variability into coefficient-level variability through the regression design. The middle matrix captures where estimated error variability is located along the predictor values; high-error observations at high leverage can contribute heavily to slope uncertainty.

Sandwich covariance estimator
\[\widehat V_{HC}=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n q_i\,x_i^\ast x_i^{\ast\mathsf T}\right)(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\]

Here \(x_i^\ast=(1,x_i)^\mathsf T\) is the \(2\times1\) design column for company i, and \(q_i\) is a selected estimate of its conditional error variance. The outer product \(x_i^\ast x_i^{\ast\mathsf T}\) is \(2\times2\), so the sum is also \(2\times2\). A notation warning: this \(q_i\) is a covariance contribution, not the inverse-variance weight used later in Weighted Least Squares (WLS). The same letter would mean different things; from now on, we use \(q_i\) for HC covariance contributions and reserve \(w_i\) for WLS weights.

HC0 uses \(q_i=e_i^2\). HC1 multiplies HC0 by \(n/(n-p)\) as a degrees-of-freedom correction. HC2 uses \(q_i=e_i^2/(1-h_{ii})\), and HC3 uses \(q_i=e_i^2/(1-h_{ii})^2\). These are different estimators, not interchangeable labels. HC3 is commonly attractive in small samples because it more strongly inflates contributions from high-leverage observations; it does not guarantee exact finite-sample coverage.

10. Derive leverage and HC3 observation by observation

The hat matrix is \(H=X(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}X^\mathsf T\). It maps observed outcomes to fitted outcomes, \(\widehat y=Hy\). Its diagonal \(h_{ii}\) measures the extent to which observation i's outcome influences its own fitted value under the fixed design. For simple regression with an intercept, the diagonal can be calculated without assembling the full 8×8 matrix: \(h_{ii}=1/n+(x_i-\bar x)^2/S_{xx}\).

HC3 leverage adjustment
\[h_{ii}=\frac1n+\frac{(x_i-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}},\qquad q_i=\frac{e_i^2}{(1-h_{ii})^2}\]

At x=1, \(h_{11}=1/8+(-3.5)^2/42=0.125+12.25/42=0.4166667\). Its residual is 1.25; therefore \(q_1=1.25^2/(1-0.4166667)^2=1.5625/(0.5833333)^2\approx4.591837\). At x=8, leverage is also 0.4166667 because x is equally distant from the mean; its residual is 3.25, so \(q_8=10.5625/(0.5833333)^2\approx31.040816\). Same leverage does not mean same HC3 contribution: residual magnitude also matters.

The largest HC3 contributions occur for F and H; they warn us that the slope's estimated uncertainty may depend strongly on these observations, although neither should be deleted automatically.

11. Assemble the HC3 matrix without skipping the middle

Each observation contributes \(q_i\begin{pmatrix}1&x_i\\x_i&x_i^2\end{pmatrix}\). For instance, company A contributes \(4.591837\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\), and company H contributes \(31.040816\begin{pmatrix}1&8\\8&64\end{pmatrix}\). Summing all eight contributions produces the HC3 meat matrix, approximately \(M=\begin{pmatrix}58.707523&382.204729\\382.204729&2737.486792\end{pmatrix}\). The numbers in the bottom-right position are large because a squared predictor multiplies the contribution, not because the estimated slope is 2,737.

Now multiply \(BMB\), where \(B=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0.607142857&-0.107142857\\-0.107142857&0.023809524\end{pmatrix}\). The HC3 covariance matrix is approximately \(\begin{pmatrix}3.340527&-0.889746\\-0.889746&0.275778\end{pmatrix}\). Its slope-variance entry is 0.275778, so \(SE_{HC3}(b_1)=\sqrt{0.275778}\approx0.525145\). Its intercept standard error is \(\sqrt{3.340527}\approx1.827711\).

Calculated HC3 coefficient covariance
\[\widehat V_{HC3}=\begin{pmatrix}3.340527&-0.889746\\-0.889746&0.275778\end{pmatrix}\]

The slope coefficient is still 2.571429. The OLS fitted values and residuals are unchanged. Only the estimate of coefficient sampling variability has changed. This is the operational meaning of a sandwich standard error: the point estimate is not refitted, but the assumed pattern of error variance used in the uncertainty calculation is no longer forced to be constant. A covariance matrix is not a variance model for predicting every individual new firm's expenditure; the latter asks a different question and needs additional assumptions.

A small algebraic cross-check avoids multiplying the whole matrix. In simple regression, the slope is a weighted sum of observed outcomes with weights \(a_i=(x_i-\bar x)/S_{xx}\). Under diagonal error covariance, the HC3 slope variance becomes \(\sum_i a_i^2q_i\). Evaluating that eight-term sum gives the same 0.275778. This independent route is useful if a matrix cell or transpose is copied incorrectly, and demonstrates why observations far from the predictor mean matter so much for slope uncertainty.

12. Compare confidence intervals and be careful about reference distributions

For a transparent side-by-side teaching comparison, use the same critical number \(t_{0.975,6}=2.446912\) with each estimated standard error. The classical interval is \([1.758918,3.383939]\); substituting HC3 gives \(2.571429\pm2.446912(0.525145)=[1.286444,3.856413]\). In the robust calculation the interval is wider because the standard error is larger. But it would be false to say robust standard errors always increase. Depending on the residual pattern and design, an HC estimate may be smaller than the classical one.

The HC3 covariance is motivated by asymptotic consistency under independence, exogeneity, suitable moments, and design regularity. Its finite-sample t calibration is not an exact theorem simply because the software reports a t statistic. Using six degrees of freedom here is an illustrative convention, chosen so students can isolate the covariance change. For serious small-sample research, consider and justify alternative degrees-of-freedom adjustments or bootstrap methods suited to the design. The model should identify which reference distribution its confidence interval assumes.

In this example both intervals exclude zero. That agreement is a fact about our data, not proof that heteroscedasticity is absent or irrelevant. A different dataset might change a decision at 5% when the uncertainty changes. Rather than treating statistical significance as a binary score, report the slope in meaningful units, both intervals, their assumptions and the sensitivity of conclusions to credible analyses.

13. HC0, HC1, HC2 and HC3: what is changing?

The family of HC estimators shares the same outer OLS matrices but differs in how it replaces unknown observation-level variance. HC0 uses each squared fitted residual directly. Yet residuals at high-leverage points tend to be shrunk because their own outcome influences the fitted value. HC2 and HC3 respond by dividing squared residuals by powers of \(1-h_{ii}\). HC1 instead rescales the overall HC0 matrix using the degrees-of-freedom factor \(n/(n-p)=8/6\). These modifications are particularly visible in a small sample such as ours.

For the slope in our example, the four standard errors are approximately: HC0 0.320719, HC1 0.370335, HC2 0.409047, and HC3 0.525145. They are not ordered this way by a universal theorem; this ordering reflects the particular residuals and leverages. The intercept SEs also differ. If a statistical package defaults to HC1 while another returns HC3, saying only "robust SE" is not a reproducible methods statement. Record the estimator's exact name and version, the fitted model, and any small-sample inference convention.

In practice, the purpose of the comparison is not to choose whichever SE produces the strongest result. It is to show how much the substantive inference changes across methods with defensible assumptions. A strong scientific conclusion should not require an undocumented chain of method shopping. If results vary substantially, emphasize uncertainty about the inference rather than hiding an inconvenient model. The larger methodological lesson is that robustness is always robustness against some specified vulnerability, not against all possible mistakes.

14. Weighted Least Squares targets a different operation

HC3 keeps the OLS coefficients and changes their covariance estimate. Weighted Least Squares (WLS) changes the fitting objective itself: minimize \(\sum_i w_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2\). If independent errors have known variances proportional to \(\sigma_i^2\), the theoretically efficient choice for a correctly specified linear mean is \(w_i\propto1/\sigma_i^2\). Smaller-variance observations receive higher weight because their outcomes contain more precise information about the same mean relationship.

WLS coefficient estimator
\[\widehat\beta_{WLS}=(X^\mathsf TWX)^{-1}X^\mathsf TWy,\qquad W=\operatorname{diag}(w_1,\ldots,w_n)\]

For demonstration only, suppose an external measurement protocol justified variances proportional to \((1+x_i/4)^2\) and errors remained independent. Then illustrative weights are \(w_i=1/(1+x_i/4)^2\), giving approximately 0.6400, 0.4444, 0.3265, 0.2500, 0.1975, 0.1600, 0.1322, and 0.1111. Refitting the same eight observations produces WLS intercept about 7.165566 and slope about 2.336425. Both coefficients differ from OLS because observations now contribute unequally to the fitting objective.

That assumed variance law is not estimated or validated by these eight data. It is a hypothetical condition that explains how WLS behaves. Without substantive justification, choosing inverse squared residuals from a tiny sample can be unstable and can give misleading precision. If variance weights are themselves estimated, their uncertainty and potential misspecification matter. Robust SE and WLS can sometimes be combined, but the correct covariance estimator must then reflect the weighted estimating equations and chosen target.

A key interpretation check: if you report WLS and HC3 side by side, you are not comparing two estimates of the same model with only a different SE. WLS changes coefficients and may change the target weighting of observations. HC3 on an OLS fit changes only the covariance estimate. Distinguishing these operations prevents the misleading claim that "we fixed heteroscedasticity and the slope moved" when the actual cause of the moved slope was a new fitting objective.

15. Distinguish variance changes from wrong means

Suppose expenditure actually curves upward with company size, but we fit a straight line. The residual graph might fan outward because the mean was misspecified, not because conditional error variance truly changes. No heteroscedasticity-consistent covariance formula makes \(\beta_1\) a correct summary of the true conditional mean at every x. If the mean is nonlinear, interpreting a single slope as a constant change in expected expenditure is already questionable.

A missing variable can similarly create both an apparent variance pattern and biased coefficients. If two industries have different average expenditure, size composition and dispersion, leaving out industry may make the residuals look heteroscedastic. Adding an appropriate group indicator may change the fitted mean, residuals, and coefficient interpretation. HC3 cannot recover the effect of a relevant omitted factor or prevent a confounding interpretation error. This connects to Guide 50 on correlation and causality, and to Guide 53 on interaction and nonlinear terms.

A diagnostic sequence therefore asks: Are the units independent? Is the conditional mean plausibly linear? Is the size index measured reliably? Is there a known group or time structure? Are extreme observations data mistakes or valid companies? Only after these questions should we decide whether uncertainty adjustment, model revision, weighting, or new data collection addresses the actual problem. A neat covariance formula cannot substitute for thinking about the mechanism that generated the observations.

16. Dependence, clusters, serial correlation, and what HC3 cannot fix

The diagonal-only sandwich used above treats distinct firms as conditionally uncorrelated. Now imagine four corporate groups, each owning two firms. Group-wide shocks could move both firms' expenditures together. Even if every firm's error variance is correctly estimated, off-diagonal covariances between firms inside a group would still enter the general \(\Omega\) matrix. Ignoring them often understates coefficient uncertainty because pairs contribute less independent information than eight unrelated companies.

Cluster-robust covariance estimators aggregate score contributions at the cluster level rather than assuming each observation is independent. Their validity depends on the number and independence of clusters, the design, and small-sample corrections. With four hypothetical corporate groups, large-cluster asymptotics would be hard to defend; a label such as "cluster robust" would not magically create enough independent clusters. The analyst may need a different design, additional clusters, or methods explicitly justified for limited clusters.

Repeated monthly observations on the same firm create a time dimension. Errors may be serially correlated, so nearby months share shocks even if their marginal variances differ. Heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) covariance estimators extend the idea of estimating more of the error covariance structure, but require choices about time ordering, lag decay and regularity. These extensions are acknowledged here, not taught as plug-in commands with universal guarantees. The statistical question determines which sources of dependence must enter the covariance calculation.

Boundary rule: HC3 addresses heteroscedasticity under suitable observation-level independence assumptions. It does not automatically handle clusters, panel structure, time dependence, causal confounding, bad measurement, or an incorrect mean specification. In each setting, identifying the sampling unit and covariance structure is more important than selecting an impressive-sounding option in a software menu.

17. Sensitivity analysis is a planned comparison, not a search for significance

A sensitivity analysis asks whether an interpretation survives credible changes in assumptions or analytic choices. For our firms, we can compare conventional and HC3 intervals, examine how the slope responds to a suspected influential observation, check model curvature, and consider the consequences of realistic grouping. Different comparisons probe different weaknesses. A leave-one-out analysis tests influence on estimates, not whether an observation should be excluded from the primary analysis.

Remove firm H temporarily as a diagnostic, then refit the remaining seven records. The new OLS slope is approximately 2.107143 and the intercept is 7.571429. Its HC3 slope SE is approximately 0.409701; with five residual degrees of freedom and a t critical value of about 2.57058, an illustrative 95% interval is [1.053972, 3.160314]. The change from slope 2.571429 to 2.107143 shows sensitivity to firm H; it does not establish that firm H is an error or that the seven-firm slope is more truthful.

Remove firm F temporarily instead. The remaining seven records give slope approximately 2.728261, intercept 6.021739, and HC3 slope SE approximately 0.455129. The slope moves in the opposite direction. This comparison demonstrates why reporting a single deletion result is inadequate: the assessment depends on which observation is removed and why. In a research report, provide the full-data estimate prominently and label leave-one-out fits as sensitivity checks, unless a documented data-quality error justifies an exclusion.

A careful sensitivity table also distinguishes quantities that did not change (the definition of x and y) from those that did (sample, fitted coefficients, residual degrees of freedom, leverage structure, and estimated covariance). Explaining the mechanism behind each movement is more informative than printing alternative p-values. Students should practice predicting the direction of a change before calculating it and then revisiting that prediction in light of the actual residual and leverage.

18. Reporting results without overclaiming

A clear applied statement might say: "In this illustrative eight-company sample, the estimated increase in monthly operating expenditure was 2.57 thousand dollars per unit of the operational-size index. The conventional OLS slope SE was 0.332 and the HC3 SE was 0.525. With a common illustrative t critical value, the corresponding 95% intervals were [1.76, 3.38] and [1.29, 3.86] thousand dollars per size-index unit. The stronger HC3 uncertainty reflects the observed residual and leverage configuration; this small synthetic dataset cannot establish a population variance law."

The report should additionally state the model specification, observation unit, sample size, HC variant, inference reference distribution, uncertainty interpretation and diagnostic limitations. Do not call the results "causal effects" without a credible identification design. Do not say the robust interval has guaranteed exact 95% coverage in an eight-record sample. Do not say the classical interval is necessarily false: its calculation is valid under its assumptions, whose plausibility is in question.

When an audience asks for a single preferred number, the most informative response is often an effect estimate accompanied by uncertainty and a brief statement of the assumptions under which it can be interpreted. Professional reporting is neither a pile of significance stars nor a blanket statement that all answers are unknowable. It identifies the target, estimates it transparently, and shows the vulnerability of important conclusions to reasonable analytic choices.

18A. Follow the weighted calculation rather than trusting a new coefficient

It is worth showing why the WLS slope from Section 14 is not merely a number printed by software. Under the explicitly hypothetical precision function \(w_i=(1+x_i/4)^{-2}\), define the weighted totals \(S_w=\sum_iw_i\), \(S_{wx}=\sum_iw_ix_i\), \(S_{wy}=\sum_iw_iy_i\), \(S_{wxx}=\sum_iw_ix_i^2\), and \(S_{wxy}=\sum_iw_ix_iy_i\). Weighted normal equations require \(\begin{pmatrix}S_w&S_{wx}\\S_{wx}&S_{wxx}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_0\\b_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}S_{wy}\\S_{wxy}\end{pmatrix}\). This directly parallels the unweighted equations, except that each observation contributes according to its assumed relative precision.

The weighted slope formula is \(b_{1,WLS}=(S_{wxy}-S_{wx}S_{wy}/S_w)/(S_{wxx}-S_{wx}^2/S_w)\); the intercept is \(b_{0,WLS}=(S_{wy}-b_{1,WLS}S_{wx})/S_w\). The denominator is a weighted version of the variation in x and must be positive for the intercept and slope to be separately identified. The numerator is a weighted centered cross-product between predictor and outcome. The formula shows conceptually why an observation's contribution depends on both its location and the proposed variance: a high-x firm can influence the fitted slope even when its inverse-variance weight is relatively small.

The weights are not probabilities and do not have to add to one. Multiplying every weight by the same positive constant leaves the WLS point coefficients unchanged, because both numerator and denominator of the weighted normal equations scale together. Their ratios determine the fitted line. By contrast, the standard error calculation depends on which variance scale is being assumed or estimated, and an estimated weight function may require additional uncertainty considerations. Using the WLS formula, the estimated slope of 2.336425 is lower than the unweighted value of 2.571429: that difference reflects less relative weight on large-x firms, particularly H, whose unusually high spending pulls the unweighted right end upward.

This worked comparison also explains why seemingly simple commands such as “fix the heteroscedasticity” are ambiguous. If the analyst seeks a stable estimate of the specified mean relationship and knows the measurement error variances, WLS might be appropriate. If the analyst lacks a credible variance function and primarily needs inference about the OLS mean-model coefficients under independent heteroscedastic errors, an HC covariance may be more transparent. If the mean model is wrong, neither choice is enough. The scientific justification must come before the numerical convenience.

18B. Changing measurement units does not make uncertainty disappear

Consider reporting expenditure in dollars rather than thousands of dollars. Every value of y would be multiplied by 1,000, while x remains the same. Because the OLS solution is linear in y, the new intercept becomes about 6,178.571 dollars and the new slope becomes about 2,571.429 dollars per size-index unit. Every fitted value and residual also multiplies by 1,000. Consequently SSE multiplies by \(1{,}000^2\), and every coefficient variance—including the classical and HC3 slope variances—multiplies by \(1{,}000^2\). Taking square roots, both standard errors multiply by 1,000.

The t ratio is invariant under this positive rescaling: multiplying a coefficient and its SE by the same factor cancels in the ratio. Confidence interval endpoints also multiply by 1,000. The substantive evidence should not change merely because an author changes a table from “USD thousands” to “USD.” This dimensional check is valuable because a missing factor of 1,000 can create an apparently dramatic change in results while leaving the mathematical method otherwise correct.

Now suppose the size index is replaced by \(z=x/2\), so one z unit corresponds to two original index units. Since x=2z, the model can be rewritten \(E(Y\mid z)=\beta_0+(2\beta_1)z\). The new slope and its standard error are both twice their original values, while fitted means, residuals and slope t statistics remain unchanged. The predictor-rescaling example illustrates that coefficients must always be read with their units; a coefficient can change numerically without a corresponding change in underlying fit or scientific evidence.

For the HC3 calculation, a consistent reparameterization transforms the design columns and covariance together. Leverage remains unchanged because the column space of the design matrix is the same. Therefore the corrected residual contributions continue to represent the same observations even though the individual coefficient entries use different numeric units. This is a deeper reason to emphasize dimensions rather than memorize isolated values from a software output.

18C. What “consistent” promises—and what it does not

Heteroscedasticity-consistent means that under an appropriate increasing-sample framework, an estimator of coefficient covariance approaches the relevant population covariance target in a specified sense. The idea is asymptotic: we imagine more observations collected under conditions ensuring no single observation dominates the design and error moments remain controlled. It does not imply that an HC3 estimator exactly equals the true covariance in a sample of eight, nor that a nominal 95% interval always covers in 95 out of every 100 possible tiny samples.

Why does leverage matter in that argument? Fitted residuals are constrained by the estimated model and generally differ from true population errors. At high leverage, the fitted line can track an observed value more closely, compressing its residual relative to the underlying disturbance. HC2 and HC3 seek to compensate for that compression by adjusting residual squares with leverage factors. HC3's squared denominator more strongly counteracts leverage effects, which can improve behavior in some small-sample configurations but also become unstable when leverage is extremely near one. It remains an approximation based on information in the observed sample.

Nor is consistency a guarantee against bad data or biased conditional means. If the mean function is wrong, the estimator may converge toward a best linear approximation rather than the causal or nonlinear parameter the analyst wanted. If errors are dependent, a diagonal-only covariance estimator can converge to the wrong variance target. If firms were selected because their expenditures had extreme values, the sampling mechanism itself requires examination. Strong methods statements therefore identify both the target estimand and the assumptions that connect the target to the fitted coefficients.

A good mental test is to distinguish three sources of uncertainty: random sampling, model specification, and measurement quality. HC3 addresses an aspect of the first under a particular variance structure. Model uncertainty needs comparison of credible specifications and perhaps validation data; measurement quality needs auditing or better data collection. A wider confidence interval is sometimes an honest signal of risk, not a flaw to be eliminated by switching procedures until a pleasing answer appears.

18D. A decision framework before reporting a robust result

Start by identifying what each row represents. Eight independent firms allow a different covariance justification from eight months of one firm or eight divisions of the same corporation. Next write the conditional mean you intend to interpret and mark the domain of observed x. Check whether a line is plausible and whether a straight slope meaningfully answers the business or scientific question. This step precedes the choice of standard error because an accurate variance estimate around an irrelevant model is not useful.

Then reconstruct the fitted outcomes and residuals. Look for patterns in their sign and magnitude and for valid high-leverage observations. State what the diagnostic plot can and cannot support at the sample size in hand. Under independent but potentially heteroscedastic disturbances, choose and specify an HC variant before interpreting its uncertainty. Compare it with the textbook covariance not to hunt for a preferred p-value, but to understand how much of the reported precision came from the equal-variance shortcut.

If externally supported precision weights exist, explain why WLS changes the objective and coefficients. If observations belong to clusters or repeat through time, revisit the off-diagonal error covariance instead of silently relying on HC3. If omitting a variable or using a straight line is doubtful, examine the mean model rather than only the covariance. Record sensitivity checks that answer identifiable questions: how strongly does H influence the slope, does a justified functional form alter the result, and is the conclusion stable across plausible uncertainty estimates?

Finally, communicate both the estimated association and the limits of its warrant. Name the estimator, sample unit, sample size, data provenance, predictor and response units, HC variant, interval convention, and unresolved limitations. Use a sentence about what further evidence would strengthen the conclusion—more firms near the upper range, independently documented variance information, or genuinely independent clusters. This chain of reasoning turns technical calculations into accountable statistical practice, which is the ultimate goal of the guide.

18E. A counterexample: when an equal-variance shortcut looks reassuring

It is tempting to think that an unusually small standard error means our estimate is especially trustworthy. But consider the contribution of company H to this sample. It has a positive residual of 3.25 thousand dollars and an extreme x value. The conventional estimator pools squared residuals into a single value, then distributes that common variance over the design geometry. In effect, the same variance estimate is used to summarize departures near the center and near the endpoints. In HC3, company H contributes through both its squared residual and its leverage adjustment. These procedures answer different uncertainty questions because their estimates of the unknown error covariance are different.

A useful counterfactual thought experiment is to keep the x values fixed while repeating data collection from the same eight kinds of firms. Suppose the conditional error SD for small firms were one thousand dollars but for large firms were five thousand dollars. We would expect repeated outcomes at the high-x end to move much more than outcomes at the low-x end. Because slope calculations attach substantial influence to departures far from the mean of x, uncertainty about the slope could be particularly affected. The constant-variance shortcut erases the location of that heterogeneity, while a heteroscedasticity-consistent covariance attempts to preserve it using observation-level information.

That story is not a fitted variance model for our table. We have not established a progression from SD one to SD five; we are using a hypothetical mechanism to explain why location and variance matter together. The distinction between a teaching counterexample and an empirical finding is essential. A student can imagine one large residual caused by chance even under homoscedasticity, or many apparently similar residuals under heteroscedasticity in a small sample. Diagnostic plots are imperfect evidence, and HC3's performance depends on the actual sample design and regularity conditions.

A second conceptual comparison asks what happens if the observations change but the estimated slope does not. Two different datasets might produce the same OLS slope of 2.571429 while distributing residuals differently across the design. The textbook covariance could even match if both had the same SSE. Nevertheless, HC3 could differ substantially because the products of squared adjusted residuals and centered predictor weights would differ. This explains why reporting only a fitted coefficient and an R-squared value is insufficient for inference: those summaries do not uniquely determine heteroscedasticity-robust uncertainty.

Check your own understanding: could you predict which observation matters more for HC3 slope uncertainty—one at x=4 with a residual of 3, or one at x=8 with a residual of 3? The high-x observation carries more centered-predictor weight and often more leverage. To establish the exact comparison, however, calculate each contribution using its actual h value and the squared centered x weight; do not infer the ranking only from a visual impression. This final comparison reconnects algebra to intuition and illustrates how to ask questions about estimated uncertainty without treating a robust formula as infallible.

19. Worked transfer problems — understanding through independent calculations

The following problems are not mere recall questions. Each alters one feature of the analysis or asks you to reproduce a key operation independently. Before reading a solution, write down what stays constant, what changes, and which assumptions make your calculation meaningful. All exercises refer to the synthetic eight-company dataset unless they explicitly introduce another hypothetical scenario.

Exercise 1 — Reproduce the OLS slope and its units

Problem. Given \(\sum x=36\), \(\sum y=142\), \(\sum x^2=204\), \(\sum xy=747\), and n=8, calculate the slope, intercept and predicted expenditure at x=5. Explain why a slope of 2.57 cannot be interpreted as a 2.57% increase. Identify the status of any causal claim.

Solution. Compute \(S_{xx}=204-36^2/8=42\) and \(S_{xy}=747-(36)(142)/8=108\). Thus \(b_1=108/42=2.57142857\), and \(b_0=17.75-2.57142857(4.5)=6.17857143\). At x=5, \(\widehat y=6.17857143+5(2.57142857)=19.0357143\) thousand dollars. The dependent variable is in thousands of dollars, not logarithms, so the slope is a change in currency, not percent. An observational mean association is not an intervention effect; common causes may affect firm size and expenditure. Check that 19.035714 is close to the observed y=19, giving a small negative residual.

Exercise 2 — Compute a residual and the residual degrees of freedom

Problem. Find the residual at x=6 and calculate the residual variance estimate. Why do you use n-p instead of n? Is the residual variance the same as a coefficient's standard error?

Solution. The fitted expenditure at x=6 is 6.17857143+6(2.57142857)=21.6071429, while the observed value is 18. The residual equals -3.6071429 thousand dollars, and its square is about 13.01148 in squared-thousand-dollar units. Add all eight squared residuals to obtain SSE=27.7857143. The intercept and slope consume two dimensions, so the residual degrees of freedom equal 8-2=6; therefore \(s^2=27.7857143/6=4.63095238\). This estimates a common residual variance under the classical model. A coefficient SE additionally incorporates the design matrix and has units appropriate to the specific coefficient. The two quantities cannot be substituted for one another.

Exercise 3 — Reconstruct the matrix inverse

Problem. Form \(X^\mathsf TX\), compute its determinant and inverse, and explain why the determinant is relevant. Describe the dimensions of the result.

Solution. With a column of ones and x values 1 through 8, \(X^\mathsf TX=\begin{pmatrix}8&36\\36&204\end{pmatrix}\). Its determinant is \(8(204)-36(36)=1632-1296=336\). For a 2×2 matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\), the inverse is \((ad-bc)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}\), when the denominator is nonzero. Thus \(B=(1/336)\begin{pmatrix}204&-36\\-36&8\end{pmatrix}\). This 2×2 matrix maps the two predictor cross-products into two coefficient estimates. A zero determinant would mean the columns do not supply two independent directions, so ordinary inversion cannot identify separate coefficients.

Exercise 4 — Derive the general covariance formula

Problem. Starting with \(y=X\beta+u\), establish a covariance formula that permits unequal variances, and show why the classical shortcut requires a special error structure.

Solution. Substitute y into the OLS expression to obtain \(\widehat\beta=\beta+B X^\mathsf Tu\), where \(B=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\). Conditional on X, the constant \(\beta\) has zero variance; the variance of a linear transformation gives \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta\mid X)=BX^\mathsf T\Omega XB\). Here \(\Omega=\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)\) can have distinct diagonal elements. If and only if the relevant model condition makes \(\Omega=\sigma^2I\), substitute to obtain \(BX^\mathsf T(\sigma^2 I)XB=\sigma^2B(X^\mathsf TX)B=\sigma^2B\). The simplification uses the common variance and no cross-observation covariance. This is an algebraic derivation of the covariance identity, not a demonstration that our sample satisfies its assumptions.

Exercise 5 — Hand-calculate HC3 at a high-leverage endpoint

Problem. For company H with x=8 and e=3.25, calculate h and q for HC3. Contrast it with company A, which has the same leverage but a smaller residual. What happens to the HC3 contribution if h approaches one?

Solution. At x=8, the centered predictor is 8-4.5=3.5, so \(h=1/8+3.5^2/42=0.4166667\). The adjustment denominator is \((1-h)^2=(7/12)^2=49/144\approx0.340278\). Therefore \(q_H=3.25^2/(49/144)=10.5625(144/49)=31.040816\). At x=1, h is the same, but \(q_A=1.25^2(144/49)=4.591837\). Thus larger squared residual magnitude yields a larger variance contribution despite identical leverage. If h approaches one, dividing by \((1-h)^2\) can yield a very large contribution; that is a sign of fragile estimation and a reason to inspect the design, not a mechanical error.

Exercise 6 — Calculate the classical slope SE

Problem. Use \(SSE=27.7857143\), n=8, p=2, and the bottom-right entry \(B_{22}=1/42\) to calculate the conventional slope SE. Explain each square root and degree-of-freedom choice.

Solution. The estimated common residual variance is \(s^2=27.7857143/6=4.63095238\). The slope sampling variance under the equal-variance shortcut is \(s^2B_{22}=4.63095238/42=0.11026077\). The standard error is its positive square root, \(0.33205537\), with units thousands of dollars per size-index unit. The square root returns variance to coefficient units; it does not change the estimated slope. An analyst who divided by n rather than n-p would use a different variance convention and generally understate residual uncertainty here.

Exercise 7 — Reproduce the HC3 slope variance two ways

Problem. Given the HC3 meat \(M=\begin{pmatrix}58.707523&382.204729\\382.204729&2737.486792\end{pmatrix}\) and \(B=(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\), find the slope variance. State an independent scalar calculation using x-centered weights.

Solution. Calculate \(BMB\) using the matrix entries from Exercise 3. The bottom-right component is approximately 0.27577753, giving \(SE_{HC3}=\sqrt{0.27577753}=0.52514525\). An independent calculation uses \(a_i=(x_i-4.5)/42\) and the eight q values from Section 10: \(\sum_i a_i^2 q_i=0.27577753\). Both forms estimate the same slope variance because the coefficient is a linear combination of y with known design weights. A mismatch indicates that q values, index centering, or matrix multiplication have been transcribed incorrectly.

Exercise 8 — Compare two interval procedures

Problem. Construct the classical and HC3 slope intervals using the same illustrative critical value 2.446912. Compare widths and state exactly which inference step remains only approximate.

Solution. The point slope is 2.571429. The classical margin is \(2.446912(0.332055)=0.812510\) to the displayed precision, yielding [1.758918, 3.383939]. The HC3 margin is \(2.446912(0.525145)=1.284984\), yielding [1.286444, 3.856413]. The HC3 interval is about 2.57 wide versus 1.63 for classical, but both center on the unchanged OLS coefficient. The HC3 covariance estimate is heteroscedasticity-consistent under suitable asymptotic conditions; using the same six-degree t cutoff in this eight-point example is a teaching convention, not an exact finite-sample coverage guarantee.

Exercise 9 — Distinguish HC variants

Problem. The observed HC0, HC1, HC2 and HC3 slope SEs are 0.320719, 0.370335, 0.409047 and 0.525145. Explain the different denominators and why it is not defensible to choose one after looking at which p-value crosses 0.05.

Solution. HC0 uses squared residuals directly. HC1 multiplies its full covariance matrix by n/(n-p)=8/6, which multiplies SE by \(\sqrt{8/6}\): \(0.320719\sqrt{8/6}=0.370335\). HC2 divides each squared residual by 1-h, while HC3 divides by its square, giving larger adjustments at higher leverage. None is a universal oracle. Picking the variant solely to obtain significance capitalizes on sampling variation and hides a consequential methodological choice. A reproducible analysis states HC3 or another specified variant before interpreting results and reports alternative reasonable choices as sensitivity information.

Exercise 10 — Separate WLS from HC3

Problem. Under a hypothetical externally justified variance function proportional to \((1+x/4)^2\), calculate the first and last weights. Interpret the WLS slope 2.336425 relative to OLS 2.571429. What additional evidence would you want before treating WLS as preferred?

Solution. At x=1, the inverse-variance-proportional weight is \(1/(1.25)^2=0.64\). At x=8, it is \(1/(3)^2=1/9\approx0.111111\). WLS gives relatively greater influence to the small-x observation because the hypothesized measurement variance is smaller there. Minimizing the weighted sum gives slope 2.336425 and intercept 7.165566; those differ from OLS because the fitting objective changed. HC3 would leave the original coefficient 2.571429 untouched. Before prioritizing WLS, seek a credible mechanism or external calibration for the variance law, verify independence, study sensitivity to misspecification, and report that the variance form was imposed rather than discovered from this toy sample.

Exercise 11 — Recognize when HC3 is insufficient

Problem. Reinterpret eight companies as four corporate groups containing two companies each. Group-wide demand shocks make errors positively correlated within groups. Explain using the general covariance formula why HC3 alone is not enough, and why an ordinary cluster-robust label may be fragile with just four groups.

Solution. The general covariance contains \(X^\mathsf T\Omega X\). Positive within-group dependence appears in off-diagonal elements \(\Omega_{ij}\) for companies sharing a group. The standard HC3 meat replaces only diagonal contributions derived from individual residual squares, so it omits these cross-products. A cluster covariance attempts to aggregate contributions within each independent group, but its usual large-cluster justification requires many independent clusters and appropriate regularity. Four groups do not meet that spirit. A defensible response is to acknowledge dependence, investigate designs or inference methods suitable for few clusters, and avoid pretending eight records give eight independent pieces of evidence.

Exercise 12 — Sensitivity to a valid high-x observation

Problem. Temporarily remove company H, refit OLS, and interpret the change in slope from 2.571429 to 2.107143. Does the change justify deleting H in the main report? State a defensible sensitivity conclusion.

Solution. Using firms A–G, the new estimated intercept is 7.571429 and the slope is 2.107143. Recomputed leverages and residuals give HC3 slope SE approximately 0.409701 and, under a five-degree t convention, interval [1.053972, 3.160314]. The slope decreases by about 0.464286 thousand dollars per index unit. That movement shows the full-data fit is sensitive to the last company; it does not show the company is erroneous. If H is a valid observation from the intended population, the primary result should still use it. Report both full and leave-one-out analyses clearly, investigate data quality, and discuss whether the high-x range requires more observations.

Exercise 13 — Diagnose a misspecified mean rather than a variance problem

Problem. Suppose a domain expert expects an expenditure curve \(E(Y\mid X=x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2\) rather than a straight line. The residual plot of a linear model fans outward. Would automatically reporting HC3 solve the main conceptual problem? Describe a principled workflow.

Solution. No. HC3 changes the estimated coefficient covariance of the specified linear regression; it does not make a straight line reproduce a curved conditional mean. The analyst should first inspect data provenance and a scatterplot, articulate a plausible mean relationship, estimate a quadratic model with the necessary additional predictor \(x^2\), check how residual shape changes, and avoid extrapolating a small sample. Robust SE may still be useful for uncertainty under plausible heteroscedasticity after improving the mean model. But the meaning of a single slope changes: in the quadratic model, the derivative \(\beta_1+2\beta_2x\) varies with x. Neither the revised model nor its covariance makes the observational association causal.

Exercise 14 — Final transfer: construct an accountable report

Problem. A student writes: "Robust regression proves the size index causes spending to increase by exactly 2.57 and fixes all modeling problems. The robust interval has exactly 95% coverage." Rewrite the paragraph so every major phrase is statistically defensible. Include numerical evidence and at least three limits.

Solution. A defensible replacement is: "For eight synthetic, independent-company observations, OLS estimates an association of 2.571 thousand dollars higher expected monthly expenditure per unit of the size index under a linear conditional-mean specification. The conventional slope SE is 0.332 and the HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent SE is 0.525. Using the same illustrative t critical value gives intervals [1.759, 3.384] and [1.286, 3.856]. HC3 adjusts the estimated uncertainty but does not refit the coefficients, establish causality, repair a misspecified mean, or account for arbitrary clustering. The sample is small, so the reference-distribution approximation and interval coverage require caution." This version states the target, units, method, results, conventions and major limits. The last exercise asks you to communicate the reason the calculations matter, not merely recite them.

20. Closing synthesis: choose the vulnerability before the method

You can now trace the whole argument rather than memorize a list of robust options. First define the conditional mean and observation unit. Fit the mean model and reconstruct its residuals. Ask whether the ordinary covariance shortcut \(s^2(X^\mathsf TX)^{-1}\) is justified by a common conditional error variance and absence of cross-unit covariance. If not, return to the general expression with \(\Omega\), and decide which unknown features can reasonably be estimated from the available design.

HC3 replaces unknown diagonal variance contributions with leverage-adjusted squared residuals while keeping OLS coefficients fixed. The point slope in our example stayed 2.571429, but the estimated slope SE changed from 0.332055 to 0.525145. WLS asked a different question by refitting with proposed inverse-variance weights, obtaining slope 2.336425 under an assumed variance law. Neither procedure resolved omitted causes, incorrect mean curvature, or correlated errors among observations. Sensitivity analyses were informative because they exposed which assumption or observation mattered, rather than because they produced a preferred significance result.

The final test is explanatory: if you had to teach a first-time student why a covariance matrix has bread, meat and bread; why HC3 squares \(1-h_{ii}\) in the denominator; why the interval can move while the line does not; and why clustered firms require a different analysis, could you reproduce the reasoning using the eight rows? If so, the guide has accomplished more than showing formulas. It has established a disciplined way to connect estimates, uncertainty, scientific assumptions and responsible communication.
