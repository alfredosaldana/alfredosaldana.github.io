1. Why an incomplete lifetime needs its own estimator

A compressor that fails after two months provides an observed failure time. A compressor still running when monitoring ends after three months does not provide a failure at month three; it only shows that its lifetime exceeds three months. Treating both observations as lifetimes of two and three months changes the scientific question and produces a downward bias. Dropping the running compressor also wastes genuine information: it survived for the first three observed months. Kaplan–Meier (KM), also called the product-limit estimator, is designed to use precisely this partial information.

Our event is the first confirmed compressor failure and the time scale is months since the study origin. We follow twelve synthetic compressor histories, first introduced as a data-design example in Guide 72. Some enter observation after time zero, and some leave without failing. We distinguish an event, a censoring time, and the moment when a unit becomes eligible to enter a risk set. This guide derives an estimate of the probability that a lifetime exceeds a specified time, describes its uncertainty, and explains what is and is not identified beyond observation.

The primary questions are operational, not merely computational: What fraction may still be functional at month nine? How certain is that estimate? Has the observed curve reached one-half, so that a median can be reported? How many failure-free months, on average, are accumulated up to a defensible horizon? Each answer uses a different construction, and each depends on a clearly identified population, event definition and censoring assumption.

\[S(t)=P(T>t)\]

2. Prerequisites, symbols and the estimand

You need elementary fractions, products, probability conditional on an event, square roots and the logarithm. The uncertainty section uses the large-sample normal approximation and a transformation of a confidence interval; neither requires matrix algebra. Guide 73 establishes the population survival function, while Guide 72 teaches time origin, right censoring and delayed entry. We reintroduce the definitions here so the guide stands on its own.

Let T be the nonnegative time from the common origin until first failure; S(t)=P(T>t) is the population survival probability. The symbol n_j means the count at risk immediately before distinct failure time t_j, after accounting for delayed entry; d_j is the number of observed failures at that time, and c_j is the number censored there. The hats on estimates distinguish quantities calculated from the sample from unknown population quantities. The product-limit estimator is a step function, not a fitted smooth hazard curve.

Use months for t, T and RMST; all survival estimates and confidence limits are dimensionless probabilities. A Greenwood variance estimate has squared-probability units, and its square root is a standard error in probability units. If time is rescaled from months to weeks, a survival ordinate at the equivalent physical age does not change, but its horizontal coordinate does. Stating units prevents false comparisons of rates, times and probabilities.

\[n_j=\#\{i:L_i<t_j\le Y_i\},\qquad d_j=\#\{i:Y_i=t_j,\delta_i=1\}\]

3. The complete synthetic data and its provenance

The dataset below is fully constructed for teaching, not measured at any industrial facility. Each row is one compressor. Entry L_i is the observed study time at which the unit first becomes eligible; exit Y_i is failure or last known follow-up; δ_i=1 denotes failure and δ_i=0 right censoring. Follow-up duration is Y_i−L_i, but event times and risk sets use the shared calendar-like origin, not an individual clock reset at entry. This distinction is essential for delayed entry.

At month two A fails while six units that entered at time zero are present; later G, H, I, J, K and L gradually enter at their recorded entry times. Follow-up is not equivalent to starting every unit at month zero. For example, G enters at 2.5 and fails at six, contributing 3.5 observed compressor-months, not six. Every unit has one row, one entry and one exit. A repeated measurement would not create another independently assigned unit.

We use the same complete twelve rows throughout the guide so that the risk table, staircase, standard errors, median and restricted mean can be checked against the original records. The purpose of a synthetic dataset is mathematical transparency. It does not demonstrate the performance or hazard distribution of real compressors; no causal or industry-general claim is justified by these invented observations.

4. What right censoring contributes

Censoring and failure have different logical content. If B is censored at month three, we know B did not fail in its observable interval before three, but we do not know its subsequent failure time. Its censoring does not reduce the Kaplan–Meier curve at month three. Instead B leaves future denominators. The same happens with D at seven, H at eight, F at ten, and J and L at twelve. Censoring marks may be placed on the flat part of the curve to display where follow-up stopped.

The estimator depends on a censoring assumption: conditional on the information defining the risk set, leaving observation should not systematically reveal a different latent failure risk from remaining under observation. A maintenance program that removes only severely damaged compressors from monitoring would violate this assumption. Then the observed KM steps can misrepresent the target population; arithmetic correctness does not repair selection bias.

Administrative study end and random loss to contact may both be recorded as right censoring, but their scientific credibility differs. The reason for censoring, time origin, entry criteria and availability of relevant prognostic variables should be reported. In a dataset with competing failure types, treating a competing event as ordinary censoring changes the estimand and may be inappropriate for absolute failure probability; cumulative incidence methods address that separate objective.

5. Derive the product-limit estimator

Imagine the probability of surviving through several consecutive failure times. The multiplication rule for conditional probabilities says that survival through all intervals equals the probability of surviving the first one multiplied by each subsequent conditional survival probability given survival until then. At failure time t_j, the empirical fraction that survives that instant among the n_j units at risk is (n_j−d_j)/n_j. Multiplying those fractions produces Kaplan–Meier.

For the first event, n_1=6 and d_1=1, so the estimated chance of crossing time two is 5/6. At month five the risk set is seven: five of the original units were still present just before month three, B left at three, and G, H and I entered before five. Specifically the risk set at five is C,D,E,F,G,H,I: seven units. The conditional factor there is 6/7. Hence S-hat(5)=(5/6)(6/7)=5/7≈0.714286. The product is a chain of conditional fractions; it is not 1 minus the cumulative number of failures divided by twelve.

Between failure times the function remains constant. If two failures occur at one time, use d_j=2 in a single factor; do not accidentally count the second failure in a denominator that was not defined consistently. In exact ties, enter those eligible at the time before counting failures according to a declared convention. Our data have no identical entry and event times, avoiding ambiguity in this illustration.

\[\widehat S(t)=\prod_{t_j\le t}\left(1-\frac{d_j}{n_j}\right)\]

6. Rebuild the risk sets before multiplying

Risk sets are the core bookkeeping task. At each distinct failure month, inspect all twelve rows and count units satisfying L_i<t_j≤Y_i. The first inequality excludes compressors not yet entered; the second retains units that are still observed immediately before the event. At month six, C,D,E,F,G,H,I are eligible, so n=7. G fails and the survival estimate multiplies by 6/7. J has not yet entered until month 5.5—therefore J actually is eligible at six too, while C failed at five and is no longer eligible. The correct seven are D,E,F,G,H,I,J. This explicit correction illustrates why counting from memory is unsafe.

At nine, E fails; the risk set comprises E,F,I,J,K,L, giving n=6. At ten, K fails while F is censored at ten; just before ten, F,I,J,K,L are present, n=5, d=1. Conventionally events are processed before censorings at exactly the same recorded time, so F is counted in the denominator but does not reduce the staircase. At eleven I fails and I,J,L form the risk set n=3. The final administrative censoring of J and L at twelve does not create another downward step.

We verify all risk counts in code from the raw rows rather than treating a preprinted table as unquestionable authority. The sequence 6,7,7,6,5,3 is not required to decline: delayed entry can increase the size of a later risk set. This is a particularly useful transfer point for students accustomed to closed cohorts with every subject under observation from time zero.

7. Interpret the entire risk table

The complete table connects time, n_j, d_j, the conditional factor and the cumulative product. Reading across one row is more informative than memorizing a final survival figure. At time nine, the preceding estimate is 0.612245; the new factor is 5/6=0.833333; the updated estimate is 0.510204. That means approximately 51% of the target lifetime distribution is estimated to exceed nine months, under the censoring assumptions and with sampling uncertainty. It does not mean that exactly 51% of our twelve individual compressors were observed alive at nine.

Because the estimate is multiplicative, intermediate fractions can be checked exactly. After the first two times, the factors (5/6)(6/7) cancel to 5/7; after six months another 6/7 yields 30/49=0.612245. Time ten produces 0.408163 and time eleven 0.272109. Students should check 0≤S-hat≤1 and that no event creates an upward jump. A step should occur only when d_j>0; a censoring mark is a statement about observation rather than failure.

For a patient-recovery application, the event might instead be recovery, and the curve would represent probability of remaining unrecovered. This interpretation reversal matters: survival is always “event has not yet occurred,” not automatically “healthy” or “doing well.” Always report the event definition directly next to the graph.

8. Why a staircase, not a smooth line

The product-limit estimate is right-continuous: at an event time the value after incorporating the event is assigned to that time, and the horizontal segment continues until the next failure. This convention is why the vertical jump at month two ends at 5/6 rather than leaving an intermediate ambiguous point. Interpolating linearly between month two and five would imply unobserved gradual evidence of failures during that interval. KM does not provide such evidence.

Place censoring ticks at their time on the current horizontal survival level. Mark an individual censoring time without reducing the ordinate. The number at risk below a graph should reflect the chosen time and delayed entries; showing only the initial sample size can obscure sparse tails. A curve may appear precisely known when few units remain, especially if plotted with the same heavy line weight throughout. Confidence bands and a risk table make that weakness visible.

For strict “time to recovery,” measurement may occur only at scheduled visits. An event observed at a visit can be interval-censored rather than exactly timed; naïve KM that pretends each visit is an exact recovery time is not the appropriate likelihood model. Our illustration assumes observed failure times are exact within the stated resolution, censoring times identify lower bounds, and follow-up and entry information are properly recorded.

9. Greenwood variance: uncertainty from risk sets

A step estimate is not a certainty. Greenwood’s formula estimates sampling variance by accumulating, across event times, the terms d_j/[n_j(n_j−d_j)] and multiplying the sum by S-hat(t)^2. The formula is motivated by uncertainty in each conditional survival fraction and a first-order large-sample approximation to the variance of the product. It is not an exact finite-sample variance and relies on independent observational units and appropriate censoring assumptions.

At month nine, event times two, five, six and nine have risk counts 6,7,7,6 with one event each. Their Greenwood terms are 1/(6×5)=1/30, 1/(7×6)=1/42, 1/42 and 1/30. The sum is 0.1142857. Multiplying by S-hat(9)^2=0.510204²≈0.260308 gives estimated variance about 0.02975; taking the square root yields SE≈0.17248. The standard error is measured in probability units, not in months.

At month ten add 1/(5×4)=0.05 to the cumulative term, giving 0.1642857; S-hat(10)=0.408163 and SE≈0.16544. Although the standard error happens to fall numerically, that does not mean month ten is intrinsically better supported. The relative uncertainty and effective sample sizes matter, and intervals can behave differently near zero or one. Greenwood is undefined at a risk set completely exhausted by events without modification; the tail deserves particular care.

\[\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\{\widehat S(t)\}=\widehat S(t)^2\sum_{t_j\le t}\frac{d_j}{n_j(n_j-d_j)}\]

10. A simple Wald interval and its defects

A first approximation to a pointwise confidence interval takes S-hat ± z×SE, with z≈1.96 for 95% nominal coverage under a normal large-sample approximation. At nine months, 0.510204±1.96(0.172480) gives about [0.1721,0.8483]. It is a pointwise interval: it attempts to cover the population survival probability at one prespecified time. It does not guarantee that an entire drawn survival curve is covered simultaneously at all months.

At ten, the same procedure yields 0.408163±1.96(0.165437)≈[0.0839,0.7324]. This can be communicated as uncertainty under the model assumptions, but the technique has a serious mathematical defect: near S=0 or S=1, the endpoints can fall outside [0,1], even though probabilities cannot. A symmetric interval on the probability scale is rarely a good default at boundaries.

The words “95% confidence” describe the long-run behavior of the interval-producing procedure over repeated studies under its assumptions. They do not mean a 95% posterior probability that the fixed, unknown parameter falls inside this particular observed interval. Guide 58 introduced Bayesian credible intervals, which have a different interpretation. Our next construction transforms the scale so that the endpoints always remain in the probability range.

\[\widehat S(t)\pm 1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\{\widehat S(t)\}\]

11. Derive and apply the log–log interval

For 0<S-hat<1, define g(S)=log[−log(S)]. The transformed quantity ranges over the entire real line, so a symmetric normal approximation on that scale maps back to legal probabilities. Its derivative is g′(S)=1/[S log(S)], which follows by the chain rule from introductory calculus. Combining the delta method with Greenwood yields estimated SE of g(S-hat) equal to sqrt(G)/|log S-hat|, where G is the accumulated Greenwood sum. This is the explicitly specified log–log transformation, not an unspecified generic “95% band.”

At nine months, S-hat=0.510204, G=0.1142857, and |log(S-hat)|≈0.67294, so SE_g≈sqrt(0.1142857)/0.67294≈0.5023. Let a=g(S-hat). Compute a±1.96SE_g and transform back with S=exp[−exp(a)]. Because this inverse is decreasing, the upper transformed endpoint becomes the lower survival endpoint and vice versa. We will calculate the precise endpoints with Python and print them in the table instead of silently guessing from a graph.

Intervals produced this way are pointwise confidence intervals. They may be wide in a sample of twelve, even when the plotted line looks smooth. A band for simultaneous coverage across all times requires a distinct procedure, such as a properly constructed Hall–Wellner or equal-precision band, not merely connecting pointwise endpoints. At S-hat=1 before the first event, the log–log expression is undefined and must be handled by boundary convention rather than blindly applying logs.

\[g(s)=\log[-\log(s)],\qquad \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_g=\frac{\sqrt{G(t)}}{|\log\widehat S(t)|}\]

12. What confidence intervals can—and cannot—say

Pointwise confidence intervals do not protect against all forms of uncertainty. Greenwood accounts for sampling uncertainty under the KM model, not errors in event coding, incompatible entry definitions, informative censoring, calendar changes or variation among sites. A plausible interval from a misspecified observation process can be systematically misleading. A sensitivity audit should therefore accompany a confidence band, especially in observational survival analysis.

Uncertainty often expands late in follow-up because fewer units remain at risk. The Greenwood sum increases with each event; but plotting standard errors alone may obscure asymmetric uncertainty and the fact that the sample is small. Reporting n_j alongside intervals is therefore not cosmetic. When only three units are at risk at month eleven, a sharp downward step is based on one observed failure among three, not on a large, stable estimate of the population tail.

The estimates here are descriptive associations under a censoring and delayed-entry model. No random treatment was assigned, and the synthetic failure times do not support any causal effect of maintenance policy. To compare actual treatments, a separate design and analysis would be necessary. If the audience asks “What is the chance the next individual compressor will fail by exactly month ten?”, be careful: KM estimates a group survival distribution; individual calibrated prediction requires specifying covariates, target population and appropriate validation.

13. Median survival and the case of no median

The median lifetime is the earliest time when the survival function is at or below 0.5, given the usual definition of a median with possible jumps. Inspect our table: at month nine S-hat≈0.510204 is still slightly above one-half; at month ten S-hat≈0.408163 falls below it. Therefore the Kaplan–Meier median estimate is ten months. We do not interpolate between nine and ten, because no event occurred between those observed times and the curve is flat there.

The median is a quantile of the estimated distribution, not the arithmetic median of the observed exit times. The latter mixes events and censorings; it might change if the monitoring program stops earlier without any change in the underlying failure distribution. For incomplete data, treating all exit times as actual failures estimates the wrong thing. The time at which half of the observed devices have had documented failure is also not equivalent to the KM median when entry and censoring vary.

If the KM curve never drops to 0.5 or below during adequately supported follow-up, then the median is not estimable from this dataset. Report “median not reached” or “not estimable,” along with follow-up and risk counts; do not extrapolate to a fabricated crossing time or substitute the largest observed event. A hypothetical curve with S-hat(last event)=0.68 illustrates this case. Reporting a restricted mean up to a well-supported horizon can often be more informative than pretending the median exists.

\[\widehat m=\inf\{t:\widehat S(t)\le 0.5\}=10\ \text{months}\]

14. Restricted mean survival time (RMST): the area

An ordinary mean lifetime integrates the survival curve all the way to infinity. With incomplete follow-up, the right tail is usually not identified without a model, so an unrestricted mean may be impossible to estimate nonparametrically. Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) instead chooses a finite, clinically or operationally meaningful horizon τ and integrates S(t) from zero to τ. It has units of time and answers “How many event-free months are expected during the first τ months?”

For τ=11, the KM function is constant on intervals [0,2), [2,5), [5,6), [6,9), [9,10) and [10,11). The widths are 2,3,1,3,1,1 months; the survival heights are 1,5/6,5/7,30/49,25/49 and 20/49. Multiply width by height in each interval: 2(1)+3(5/6)+1(5/7)+3(30/49)+1(25/49)+1(20/49)=7.969388 months. This is an exact finite sum up to rounding, not an extrapolation beyond the study.

Why use eleven instead of an arbitrary longer horizon? Events are observed through eleven, and follow-up continues through twelve for two censored units; the choice τ=11 is transparently supported by the collected data. Choosing τ should be done before comparing groups when possible and should reflect shared, adequate follow-up. If τ is changed after inspecting which result looks favorable, that is another form of post hoc outcome selection. RMST does not require the median to be reached.

\[\widehat{\mathrm{RMST}}(\tau)=\int_0^\tau\widehat S(t)\,dt=\sum_k(t_{k+1}-t_k)\widehat S(t_k)\]

15. Distinguish RMST from mean observed follow-up

Our dataset also records 64 observed compressor-months across twelve units. Divide 64/12≈5.333 months and you obtain mean observed follow-up per enrolled compressor, not mean event-free time in the target population through eleven. The total recorded exposure uses entry and exit and is a descriptive sample summary. It cannot substitute for integrating a survival estimator, because some units contributed little follow-up and censoring is not failure.

The estimate RMST(11)≈7.969 months might initially seem inconsistent with 5.333 months of observed follow-up per unit. The two numbers answer different questions. The first estimates area under the population survival curve under risk-set and censoring assumptions; the second is a direct average of observed monitoring durations. In a study with substantial delayed entry, their difference can be pronounced. Neither is automatically “the average lifetime” without specifying the estimand.

The restricted failure-time mean also differs from an incidence rate. Dividing six observed events by 64 compressor-months gives 0.09375 observed failures per compressor-month, an aggregate event-per-exposure rate; it is not S(11), and it is not the same as RMST(11). Translating 0.09375 into an exponential mean would additionally assume a constant hazard model, which KM does not assume. Keeping definitions near numbers prevents accidental substitution of one summary for another.

16. Estimation support, tail behavior and entry assumptions

After the last observed failure at month eleven, the KM estimate stays at 0.272109 through the last observed follow-up. That horizontal continuation says “no additional observed failure,” not “we know the population has a 27% probability of surviving indefinitely.” At month twelve J and L leave; there is no further follow-up. Drawing the line beyond the last supported time would be a graphical extension without event information.

The tail is especially fragile because only three units were at risk at eleven. A confidence interval conditional on the model may still be computable, but it can be broad and does not justify projections to twenty or thirty months. Scientific reporting should state the latest event, last observed contact, number at risk at prespecified times and the horizon τ used for RMST. The largest observed time is not evidence that survival equals zero afterward.

With delayed entry, there is another assumption beyond censoring: the sampled units entering later must represent the relevant event-time distribution conditional on being eligible and surviving to entry. If entry is chosen based on imminent failure or if the data only include unusually durable compressors, the estimate is vulnerable to selection bias. The choice of time scale also matters: calendar month and operating age are not interchangeable. For real cohorts, establish this design before calculation.

17. Ties, time resolution and ordering conventions

When several failures occur at exactly the same recorded time, all are included in d_j and n_j is the count immediately before the shared time. Suppose hypothetically five units were at risk and two failed together: the conditional factor would be (5−2)/5=3/5. Splitting into sequential event counts of 4/5 and 3/4 also multiplies to 3/5 when the risk sets are updated consistently, but the Greenwood calculation and interpretation require a declared tied-time convention.

If censoring and event happen at the same reported time, many implementations place event before censoring. This is why F, censored at ten, belongs to n=5 at the time K fails. A dataset reported in whole months may conceal which event actually happened first within that month. Precision limitations should be documented; applying a particular tie rule does not magically recover unknown within-month order.

Never treat delayed entry at exactly t_j as automatically in the risk set without checking the chosen open/closed interval convention. Our rule L_i<t_j≤Y_i is explicit; a different time-origin convention should be translated consistently across tables, code and estimand. One row per unit is important in this simple single-event analysis. Multiple episodes, recurrent events or clustered machinery require other risk-set constructions and variance methods.

18. Risk-table audits and computational reproducibility

A good independent check starts from the row-level data, not from the final six numbers. For every event time t, filter records whose entry is earlier than t and whose exit is at least t; count those records. In another operation count events with exit equal to t. Construct the factor 1−d/n and accumulate the product. Separately accumulate d/[n(n−d)]. Compare the resulting survival and Greenwood standard error with the hand-derived table to a stated numerical tolerance.

We use Python and SciPy only to verify—not replace—the reasoning. The code in the companion verification file reads the complete CSV and independently computes the six risk sets. The hand table remains interpretable without running code. If numerical output disagrees, investigate entry inequalities, censor-event ties, off-by-one time indexing and whether a software library expects left-truncated data in a different representation. Agreement between two tools that share the same mistaken preprocessing does not establish validity.

For a published HTML guide, the plotted staircase must be constructed from the exact same risk-table values as the text. Axes should label time in months and survival as probability; the figure caption should identify the population and invented data provenance. Tables that need additional horizontal space should scroll within their container on 360-pixel screens rather than force the whole page to overflow.

19. Communication and decision under uncertainty

A useful report does not say only “KM at eleven is 27.2%.” It identifies the first-failure event, common time origin, twelve synthetic units, right censoring, delayed entry, risk sets, and the 95% pointwise interval method. It distinguishes a median reached at ten from a mean restricted at eleven. It notes that just three units remain at risk at eleven and that no evidence supports a model-free prediction beyond last follow-up.

For maintenance planning, one might reserve replacement units based on estimated survival through ten months. But the wide intervals and tiny sample would argue for cautious, monitored decisions rather than fixed claims. A hazard-based predictive model, condition monitoring and external validation may eventually refine predictions; they are not established by this descriptive KM analysis. The synthetic dataset does not contain maintenance treatment assignment, so no causal recommendation is identified.

If a competing outcome like equipment removal for reasons unrelated to failure prevents the target failure, decide whether to define a composite event or use competing-risk methods. If censoring is likely informative, investigate why units exited; if the horizon is too long for reliable support, shorten it prospectively. Statistical precision cannot overcome a badly defined event or a biased observation process. This is the final interpretation students should be able to defend, including the limitations rather than only the attractive headline number.

20. Where Greenwood comes from: a delta-method bridge

It is reasonable to ask why a sum of fractions can estimate uncertainty in a product. Take logarithms of the Kaplan–Meier estimate: log S-hat(t) is the sum of log(1−d_j/n_j) over event times. Under an idealized independent conditional event mechanism, the variance of a sum is easier to approximate than the variance of a product. A first-order expansion of the log terms gives a risk-set contribution of approximately d_j/[n_j(n_j−d_j)]. The variance of log S-hat is therefore approximated by the cumulative Greenwood sum G(t).

Now apply the derivative of exp(x): a small change in log S becomes about S times that change in survival. Squaring the multiplier yields Var(S-hat)≈S-hat² Var(log S-hat), exactly the structure of Greenwood. This is a Delta Method argument, not a claim that the discrete failures themselves are normally distributed. The approximation is motivated by a sufficiently large, appropriately sampled cohort with noninformative censoring and adequate risk sets.

At month nine the log-scale variance approximation G=4/35 is not small, because only a few units support the estimate. The delta method still provides a conventional uncertainty description, but its normal-reference intervals should be interpreted cautiously. The relationship is valuable for transfer: nonlinear transformations alter standard errors through their derivatives, and computational agreement does not overcome a small-sample or censoring-design weakness.

\[\log\widehat S(t)=\sum_{t_j\le t}\log(1-d_j/n_j),\quad \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\log\widehat S)\approx G(t)\]

21. Pointwise intervals for a median or an RMST are different tasks

The log–log intervals derived earlier refer to survival at a fixed time. It is tempting to reuse the numerical endpoints as if they were a confidence interval for median lifetime, but the target parameter has changed from a probability to a time. A median interval requires inverting a confidence set for the survival curve or another appropriate quantile procedure, with explicit conventions when the limits do not cross 0.5. Large-sample methods such as Brookmeyer–Crowley address this different inference problem. We do not claim to have calculated a median interval by merely reporting S-hat(10)±SE.

RMST uncertainty is another task. The RMST estimator is an area under an entire estimated curve, so its variance must account for dependence between survival ordinates at different times. Multiplying the Greenwood standard error at one horizon by eleven months would be incorrect: the area includes an integrated covariance structure, not just the final ordinate. Valid RMST intervals can use a derived influence-function variance or an appropriate resampling approach, while respecting the observation design and keeping the horizon fixed.

In the current twelve-unit example we provide KM pointwise log–log intervals, a descriptive median estimate, and a descriptive RMST estimate. That is deliberate transparency: no median or RMST confidence interval is certified without implementing and checking its distinct inferential method. In a real comparative study, report interval methods and sampling assumptions separately for each estimand instead of claiming one general Greenwood calculation answers every uncertainty question.

22. Two competing plots and the danger of visual shortcuts

Imagine two KM plots with identical ordinates at month ten but very different sample sizes. In the first, 500 independent devices have extensive follow-up; in the second, only five remain at risk just before an important event. The same height of 0.4 does not imply the same uncertainty. Without a risk table or confidence interval, the viewer may infer false equivalence. This is why every substantive figure should carry numbers at risk and clear censoring marks, not just an attractive stair-step curve.

Plotting confidence endpoints as a filled region can also mislead if the reader assumes it has simultaneous 95% coverage. Our curves display pointwise log–log limits, so their caption explicitly says pointwise. Connecting those limits into lines helps visualization but does not change their statistical guarantee. Likewise, extending a final horizontal segment to infinity can suggest that the right tail is known. We stop the display at the observed twelve-month follow-up and do not imply infinite survival.

A responsible graph title states the event and population; the vertical axis says estimated event-free probability and the horizontal axis says months from the specified origin. If the event is recovery, “survival” means remaining unrecovered and should be labeled accordingly. These presentation decisions are scientific requirements because a correctly calculated curve can still transmit the wrong conclusion through an ambiguous title or visually missing denominators.

23. A practical data-quality checklist before interpreting KM

Before estimation, audit the raw histories. Verify that every unit has a unique identifier, the event code belongs to {0,1}, the entry precedes the exit and the event definition is consistent across records. Check whether any machine could be observed as failed before being enrolled, whether downtime was misclassified as first failure, and whether a recorded administrative censoring date was accidentally filled with a future event time. Such mistakes can produce plausible survival curves while silently changing the estimand.

Next audit the risk sets. At every event time, compare the count from row-level inequalities with the reported n_j. A one-unit discrepancy at the tail can alter the survival estimate dramatically. In our data, at eleven the legitimate risk set is I,J,L. If J were wrongly excluded, n would change from three to two, so the conditional factor would fall from 2/3 to 1/2. That would change S-hat(11) from approximately 0.2721 to 0.2041 without any new event—a substantial scientific error created solely by bookkeeping.

Finally audit inferential assumptions. Document reasons for censoring, how units were selected and how late entries arise. Ask whether observation intensity differs between units and whether event times are exact or interval detected. State whether the table represents a single-event population or competing outcomes. These checks belong before statistical polish: more decimals, a denser graph or a sophisticated confidence transformation cannot rescue an invalid time origin or miscounted risk set.

24. A miniature counterexample to treating censorings as failures

Consider three imaginary units all entering at zero. One fails at time two; another is censored at time three; the last fails at time four. The correct KM value after two is 2/3. The censoring at three does not change that value, but it removes one subject from the future risk set. At time four, only the unit that actually fails remains at risk, so the next KM factor is (1−1)/1=0 and the estimate falls to zero. This simple example shows both the effect of censoring on later denominators and the discontinuity caused by exhausting the risk set.

Now imagine incorrectly coding the censoring at three as a failure. After time two the estimate is 2/3; at three the risk set is two and d=1, producing a false decline to 1/3. At four the last failure still pushes survival to zero, but the entire intermediate survival history was misstated. The wrong curve would assert an event in a month where none was observed. Even when two curves coincide at the final recorded time, they may imply very different probabilities and RMST over earlier horizons.

The counterexample also explains why simply setting all censor indicators to one is not a harmless shortcut. The KM likelihood and event-time staircase depend on which exit times contribute failures; a censoring time contributes a right-side bound instead. Before using statistical software, inspect the status coding and check that it agrees with the clinical or mechanical event definition. Correct event coding has priority over every numerical refinement.

25. Choose the RMST horizon before viewing outcomes

RMST requires a decision that may seem purely technical: the choice of the upper integration limit τ. But τ changes the estimand itself. RMST(6) summarizes the first six months, whereas RMST(11) summarizes the first eleven. We can compute the former from our staircase: area from zero to two is two; from two to five is 3×5/6=2.5; from five to six is 1×5/7≈0.714286. Total RMST(6)=5.214286 months. The two restricted means are not competing estimates of the same population quantity; they answer questions about different follow-up windows.

For maintenance operations, a six-month warranty and an eleven-month replacement schedule might justify different predetermined horizons. If the question is “How much useful operation should we expect within the first six months?”, reporting RMST(11) alone fails to answer it even though its mathematical value is correct. Similarly, two treatment groups must be compared using the same τ if a difference in restricted means is to have a coherent interpretation. A shorter common horizon may be necessary when one group has sparse follow-up beyond a certain time.

Choosing the horizon after seeing that one curve looks favorable at a particular date introduces selection. Prespecify a scientifically meaningful τ, inspect whether both groups have adequate support, and then calculate the corresponding area. RMST is particularly useful when survival curves cross or medians are not observed, but it does not repair informative censoring or confounding. Its interpretive strength comes from defining the period clearly and resisting unsupported extrapolation.

\[\widehat{\mathrm{RMST}}(6)=2+3\left(\frac56\right)+\frac57=5.214285714\]

26. Why left truncation is not just another censored observation

Right censoring limits what we know about a lifetime after an observed exit. Left truncation is different: a subject can only appear in the dataset if it survived long enough to enter. At the shared origin, a compressor G that enters at 2.5 is not an observed member of the initial risk set. Pretending it contributed two and a half months from zero would enlarge the denominator at month two without legitimate observation. In our table that error would turn the first step from 5/6 into an incorrectly calculated factor such as 6/7.

Independent delayed entry requires careful interpretation. The estimator describes a lifetime distribution under the assumption that entry, conditional on eligibility and measured factors, does not select a hidden, systematically different event-risk process. If only long-lived machines are ever enrolled, correcting the counting rule alone does not identify the lifetime distribution of all new machines. The risk-set adjustment addresses the observable selection structure, not every possible hidden cause of enrollment.

A concrete audit is to list each delayed-entry subject and ask which failure times it could legitimately have experienced while under observation. G entered at 2.5 and can contribute to risk at five and six, but not two; J entered at 5.5 and contributes at six, nine, ten and eleven until censoring at twelve. These eligibility statements come directly from L<t≤Y, and they can be checked by hand for every event. This is why a common time origin and accurate entry dates are indispensable.

27. Prepare the bridge to comparisons without skipping assumptions

Once one KM curve has been reconstructed, it is natural to ask whether two groups differ. Two curves can be compared descriptively by displaying their survival estimates, risk tables and uncertainty; however, a visible separation does not automatically constitute evidence of a treatment effect. Study 75 will introduce the log-rank test, whose null hypothesis concerns the survival experience across the event times and whose calculation uses observed and expected event counts under a defined comparison design. The current guide does not replace that analysis.

Before comparing groups, verify common definitions of time zero, eligibility, endpoint and censoring. A group enrolled later or monitored more intensely may display different apparent survival even without a true difference in the intended event distribution. A comparison based on RMST likewise requires a common τ, consistent event definition and appropriately estimated uncertainty of the difference. Multiple unplanned cutoffs selected after visual inspection raise multiplicity concerns already encountered in Guide 46.

For now, the transferable achievement is more fundamental: reconstruct an honest single-group survival curve and state exactly what it estimates. With that foundation, differences between curves can be considered without confusing censor marks with events, standard errors with causal evidence or final plateaus with immortal survivors. Those distinctions carry forward to the log-rank test and proportional-hazards models, where the risk-set concept remains central.

28. Exercise 1 — Compute the first two KM steps

Problem and data

Using the records, compute the survival estimate after two and after five months. Explain why the denominator at five is seven rather than the initial twelve.

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

At t=2, eligible units are A–F: n=6, d=1, so factor=5/6 and S-hat(2)=0.833333. At t=5 the eligible units are C,D,E,F,G,H,I: n=7; B was censored at three, A failed at two and J enters only at 5.5. The new factor is 6/7 and the cumulative product (5/6)(6/7)=5/7=0.714286. The denominator is the current risk set, not the overall recruited sample. This interpretation is valid only if entry and censoring assumptions support representing the intended population.

\[\widehat S(5)=\frac56\cdot\frac67=\frac57\]

29. Exercise 2 — A censoring at a non-event time

Problem and data

B is censored at three months. Does the curve fall at three? What changes in the risk table between the events at two and five?

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

No event is observed at month three, hence d(3)=0 and there is no downward KM step. B exits observation and cannot be counted in subsequent risk sets. Between two and five, G enters at 2.5, H at 3.5 and I at 4.5. Because entries can replace exits in the count, risk n at five is seven while n at two was six. A censor tick would appear at three at the then-current survival height of 5/6. It is a follow-up mark, not a failure probability.

30. Exercise 3 — Reconstruct the step at month six

Problem and data

List each eligible unit immediately before month six. Calculate the conditional survival factor and new cumulative estimate.

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

The correct set is D,E,F,G,H,I,J. C failed at five, J entered at 5.5 and K does not enter until 6.5. Thus n=7, d=1 because G fails. Conditional survival=6/7 and accumulated S-hat(6)=(5/7)(6/7)=30/49≈0.612245. If C were wrongly retained, the denominator would be eight, changing both survival and Greenwood. Checking membership against each (entry,exit,event) record is the essential reproducibility step.

\[\widehat S(6)=\frac57\cdot\frac67=\frac{30}{49}\]

31. Exercise 4 — Event and censoring tied at ten

Problem and data

At month ten, K fails and F is censored. Reconstruct n and d and explain why F must not create a second drop.

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

Immediately before ten, F,I,J,K,L are observed and eligible. Hence n=5, d=1. The KM factor is (5−1)/5=4/5. Survival just before ten is 25/49≈0.510204, so after ten it is (25/49)(4/5)=20/49≈0.408163. F enters the denominator under the declared events-before-censor convention but never the numerator of failures. Counting F as d=2 would falsely label a running compressor as failed and exaggerate failure probability.

\[\widehat S(10)=\frac{25}{49}\cdot\frac45=\frac{20}{49}\]

32. Exercise 5 — Compute Greenwood at nine

Problem and data

Calculate the cumulative Greenwood sum, variance and standard error at nine months, showing the individual terms.

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

The observed failure times through nine are two, five, six and nine, with n values 6,7,7,6 and d=1 each. G=1/30+1/42+1/42+1/30=4/35≈0.114286. S-hat(9)=25/49≈0.510204, so Var≈(25/49)²(4/35)=0.029749 and SE≈0.172480. The result has probability units and is a large-sample standard error, not months. As a sanity check, variance is nonnegative and the standard error is less than one.

\[G(9)=\frac1{30}+\frac1{42}+\frac1{42}+\frac1{30}=\frac4{35}\]

33. Exercise 6 — Compare Wald and log–log intervals

Problem and data

At month nine use S=25/49, SE=0.172480 and G=4/35 to calculate two pointwise 95% confidence intervals. What makes their endpoints different?

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

Wald: 0.510204±1.96×0.172480 gives approximately [0.1721,0.8483]. For log–log, first compute a=ln(−ln(0.510204)) and SE_g=sqrt(4/35)/|ln(0.510204)|. Form a±1.96SE_g, then apply exp(−exp(x)) with reversed order for the survival limits. Both target the same S(9), but the second imposes a nonlinear transformation that respects the probability range and generally yields asymmetry. Neither is a simultaneous band. The exact numerical log–log endpoints appear in the reference table.

34. Exercise 7 — Identify and interpret the median

Problem and data

Locate the first failure time at which KM reaches 0.5 or lower. Why is the answer not a value interpolated between two steps?

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

At nine months S-hat=25/49≈0.510204>0.5, so median has not yet been reached. At ten S-hat=20/49≈0.408163≤0.5, so median=10 months. The estimated survival remains flat between observed failures; linear interpolation would invent a gradual decline that is absent from the KM estimator. The result estimates the time when estimated probability of remaining without failure first becomes at most one-half, conditional on study assumptions.

35. Exercise 8 — A median that cannot be estimated

Problem and data

A separate cohort has observed KM values 1, 0.90, 0.82, 0.68 and then only censoring. What is its KM median?

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

No value in the supported event-time curve reaches 0.5. Thus the KM median is not estimable from these data. Reporting 0.68 as a time, using the last follow-up as a median or extrapolating a decline to 0.5 would be wrong. Report “median not reached,” the last event and follow-up times, and the remaining risk count. A well-specified RMST within observed follow-up is a legitimate alternative, but its numerical value requires the actual event-time step widths, which were not supplied in this question.

36. Exercise 9 — Compute RMST(11) from rectangles

Problem and data

Compute the restricted mean up to eleven months from the exact KM fractions and justify the units.

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

Break the area into [0,2),[2,5),[5,6),[6,9),[9,10),[10,11). Multiply each width by the survival height: 2×1+3×5/6+1×5/7+3×30/49+1×25/49+1×20/49 = 7.969387755 months. The units are months because probability is dimensionless and the integration axis is time. This is an estimated expected event-free time within the first eleven months, not the unrestricted mean lifespan. Extending the final height to infinity would create an unjustified infinite area.

\[\widehat{\mathrm{RMST}}(11)=7.969387755\ \mathrm{months}\]

37. Exercise 10 — Separate exposure and RMST

Problem and data

Contrast the total follow-up 64 compressor-months, its mean per subject, the observed event rate and RMST(11).

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

The directly observed exposure is 64 months summed across rows. Mean monitoring duration is 64/12=5.3333 months per unit. Six failures divided by exposure give 6/64=0.09375 failures per compressor-month, a crude incidence density. RMST(11) is the area under KM, 7.969388 months. The four quantities have different definitions and cannot substitute for one another. In particular, 1/0.09375=10.6667 months would be a constant-rate exponential-model transformation, not a nonparametric Kaplan–Meier mean.

38. Exercise 11 — Two failures at a tied time

Problem and data

In a hypothetical study five units are at risk and two fail simultaneously. Calculate the KM factor and Greenwood contribution.

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

Conditional survival is (5−2)/5=3/5=0.6. Greenwood contribution is d/[n(n−d)]=2/[5(5−2)]=2/15≈0.133333. This is not the same as counting only one failure; ignoring the tie would overstate survival. To update a previous cumulative estimate, multiply it by 0.6, and to update Greenwood, add 2/15 to its accumulated sum. The time-resolution convention must be declared, particularly if observations were rounded.

\[1-\frac25=\frac35,\qquad \frac{2}{5(5-2)}=\frac2{15}\]

39. Exercise 12 — Censoring sensitivity and incomplete support

Problem and data

Suppose the two units censored at month twelve were actually systematically removed for early signs of failure. Identify what can and cannot be fixed by recalculating Greenwood.

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

The arithmetic risk sets and Greenwood variance could be recomputed perfectly from the recorded exit times, but informative censoring means the KM estimand may be biased for the intended lifetime distribution. Greenwood quantifies random variability conditional on a suitable observation process; it does not adjust selection bias. A credible remedy requires investigating the removal mechanism, capturing relevant prognostic factors and possibly using justified inverse-probability weighting or a sensitivity analysis. Without such information, report the limitation rather than attaching more decimals to the same estimate.

40. Exercise 13 — Interpret a recovery curve instead of a failure curve

Problem and data

Suppose the event is first documented recovery rather than mechanical failure. If S-hat(6)=0.60, what does this say and what does it not say?

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

With recovery defined as event, S-hat(6)=0.60 estimates the proportion that has not yet experienced documented recovery by time six; the complement 0.40 estimates event occurrence by that time under the KM assumptions. It does not mean 60% are healthy or that 40% of all patients must recover eventually. Censoring, competing outcomes and interval-ascertainment can complicate the interpretation. Always specify the event, clock, population and completeness of follow-up in the figure caption.

41. Exercise 14 — Final transfer: produce a defensible maintenance memo

Problem and data

Use the twelve-unit analysis to advise a maintenance manager. Report nine- and ten-month survival, median, RMST(11), pointwise uncertainty, sample support and at least two threats to interpretation.

Complete worked solution, verification and interpretation

A defensible memo says: first-failure KM survival is 25/49≈51.02% at nine and 20/49≈40.82% at ten; the median estimate is ten months because the curve first reaches ≤0.5 there.
