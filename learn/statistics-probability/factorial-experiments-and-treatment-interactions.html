01. The manufacturing question: why study two treatments together?

A manufacturer produces temperature-resistant panels. Engineers can set the curing temperature to Low or High and select Standard or Enhanced material. They want to improve strength, measured in megapascals (MPa), without claiming that a material which performs well at one temperature must work equally well at the other. A study that tests temperature alone while fixing material cannot answer whether the preferred temperature changes when material changes. A study that tests material alone similarly misses a possible temperature-by-material interaction.

A factorial experiment deliberately crosses the levels of factors: every temperature is tested with every material. There are two factors, each with two levels, hence a 2 × 2 experiment with four treatment combinations, sometimes called cells. Every panel is manufactured under exactly one combination. The outcome is strength after a standardized curing and testing protocol. The numbers in this guide are entirely synthetic, chosen to expose the arithmetic; they are not observed factory quality data, product specifications, or evidence of a real material effect.

The central decision is not simply “does heat help?” It is “how much does heat help with Standard material, how much with Enhanced material, and is the difference large enough to make one universal temperature recommendation misleading?” The third question motivates interaction. We will construct all four cell means, identify three different meanings of an effect, fit an equivalent regression model, reproduce a two-factor ANOVA, interpret uncertainty, and finish with decisions that respect experimental design and statistical limitations.

02. Prerequisites, objectives and vocabulary

This lesson assumes familiarity with averages, subtraction, basic randomization (Guide 59), the one-way ANOVA idea and contrasts (Guide 48), and regression interactions (Guide 53). Those guides are helpful, but no prior knowledge of factorial notation is assumed. We will reintroduce each relevant formula. You should finish able to design a simple randomized 2 × 2 experiment, calculate all four means, explain conditional versus marginal effects, calculate a difference-in-differences interaction, reproduce its sum of squares and F test, and justify a practical recommendation.

A factor is an experimentally varied explanatory variable. A level is an allowed setting of that factor. A treatment combination is a unique pair of levels. An experimental unit is the independently randomized object: here, a newly manufactured panel. A replicate is a distinct panel, not three measurements of the same panel. A response is the measured strength in MPa. A main effect summarizes an average difference across the levels of the other factor. A simple effect compares two levels of one factor while the other is fixed. An interaction describes how the simple effect changes across levels of the second factor.

A point worth anticipating: main effect is not synonymous with “the effect that matters most,” and interaction does not imply a chemical explanation. These are mathematical descriptions of systematic differences in mean responses under a chosen design. Physical explanation requires materials science, process knowledge and additional experiments. Our aim is to link the mathematics to what random assignment can and cannot support.

03. The treatment map and the experimental unit

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[(A,B)\in\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\},\quad N=2\cdot2\cdot4=16\]

Name factor A curing temperature: A=0 means Low and A=1 means High. Name factor B material: B=0 means Standard and B=1 means Enhanced. The ordered pairs are (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), and (1,1). Each pair represents a separate treatment combination. To avoid ambiguous phrases such as “group 1,” always include both settings when recording a result. The complete factorial grid is a two-by-two table whose rows are temperatures and columns are materials.

Suppose the factory has sixteen eligible new panels. Before curing, assign four distinct panels randomly to each of the four cells, using a documented procedure with equal allocation probability across all balanced assignments. Each panel receives the assigned material and curing schedule, then one independently recorded destructive strength test. The outcome is continuous in MPa. An investigator who measures one panel four times would have repeated measurements, not four experimentally randomized replicates. That mistake would inflate precision and could invalidate an F test.

Complete randomization assigns all sixteen panels across four cells without a block restriction. It differs from the blocked randomization in Guide 59: there blocks constrained assignments; here temperature and material are both manipulated treatment factors, and the cells exhaust their cross-classification. A production shift is not a third randomized factor merely because the panels happen to be tested on different days. If shifts influence both treatment allocation and strength, randomization should distribute or block shifts deliberately rather than ignoring them.

04. Complete fictional dataset, with outcomes and units

The sixteen deliberately constructed panel strengths are as follows. Low temperature with Standard material gives 48, 50, 52, 50 MPa. High temperature with Standard gives 54, 56, 58, 56 MPa. Low with Enhanced gives 52, 54, 56, 54 MPa. High with Enhanced gives 70, 72, 74, 72 MPa. Every number is a different fabricated panel. The table under this section prints the individual panel identifiers, assignments and values, so none of the observations is hidden inside a summary.

Because we want a fully reproducible example, all units are alike except for their assigned factor levels, outcomes are assumed measured without missing values, the assignment is considered genuinely randomized, and the error structure is examined later rather than assumed proven. These choices are illustrative assumptions, not empirical facts. We intentionally made within-cell variation easy to calculate, although real strength measurements can be asymmetric, heteroscedastic, or affected by batches.

Before computing any F ratio, ask what the rows permit us to compare. Four observations within each cell estimate local measurement and production variability. The four cell means compare the intended treatments. Four cells suffice to estimate both main effects and an interaction because the combinations are crossed; replicates within those cells provide independent information about unexplained variation. A single panel per cell could reproduce the means, but without a separate residual estimate it could not support the ordinary replicated two-factor ANOVA used here.

05. The four cell means, calculated without software

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\begin{aligned}\bar Y_{00}&=50,&\bar Y_{10}&=56,\\\bar Y_{01}&=54,&\bar Y_{11}&=72,\\\bar Y_{\cdot\cdot}&=\tfrac{50+56+54+72}{4}=58\end{aligned}\]

Calculate the Low–Standard mean as (48+50+52+50)/4 = 200/4 = 50 MPa. High–Standard gives (54+56+58+56)/4 = 224/4 = 56 MPa. Low–Enhanced gives (52+54+56+54)/4 = 216/4 = 54 MPa. High–Enhanced gives (70+72+74+72)/4 = 288/4 = 72 MPa. Do not compress these into a single grand mean yet: the four values reveal the scientific shape of the response.

The Low row has means 50 and 54, while the High row has 56 and 72. These are mean strengths, not probabilities. The four cell sample sizes are all four, so the marginal mean for Low is (50+54)/2=52 MPa, and for High it is (56+72)/2=64 MPa. For Standard material, the average across temperatures is (50+56)/2=53 MPa; for Enhanced it is (54+72)/2=63 MPa. The grand mean is (50+56+54+72)/4=58 MPa, matching the sum of all sixteen strengths divided by sixteen.

This equality of simple and count-weighted averages is a consequence of balance. If one cell had ten panels and another had two, taking an unweighted mean of cell means would describe an equal-cell estimand, not automatically the mean over observed panels. Balance simplifies both statistical orthogonality and communication. In later sections we contrast balance with the choice of target population rather than treating equal weights as a universal natural law.

06. Simple effects: the temperature question has two answers

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\begin{aligned}\widehat\tau_{A\mid B=0}&=56-50=6\\\widehat\tau_{A\mid B=1}&=72-54=18\end{aligned}\]

A simple effect of temperature is High minus Low while holding material fixed. With Standard material, High–Standard minus Low–Standard equals 56 − 50 = 6 MPa. With Enhanced material, High–Enhanced minus Low–Enhanced equals 72 − 54 = 18 MPa. Both comparisons describe moving from the same lower temperature setting to the same higher one. Yet the second is three times as large. The effect of temperature therefore depends on the material setting in the fabricated data.

For material, the analogous simple effects are Enhanced minus Standard. At Low temperature, the change is 54 − 50 = 4 MPa; at High, it is 72 − 56 = 16 MPa. The two pairs of results tell the same interaction story from opposite directions. Changing temperature has a different effect depending on material, and changing material has a different effect depending on temperature. That symmetry is a useful check, not two separate interactions.

The signs matter. Positive MPa differences indicate greater mean strength for the named higher setting; they do not say whether higher strength is worth the energy cost, equipment stress or price of enhanced material. If greater strength were undesirable because of brittleness, even the preference direction could change. A recommendation requires a defined utility and threshold, not merely a statistically nonzero coefficient. Keep the direction and units attached to each subtraction.

07. Marginal effects: averaging can conceal important conditions

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\widehat\tau_A=\tfrac12[(56-50)+(72-54)]=12\;\mathrm{MPa}\]

The marginal temperature effect is the difference between average strengths across material settings: 64−52 = 12 MPa. Equivalently, the average of its two simple effects is (6+18)/2=12 MPa. The marginal material effect is 63−53=10 MPa, equivalently (4+16)/2=10 MPa. The equal-weight averaging represents a target in which the two settings of the other factor are equally important, consistent with this balanced design.

A manager could state “higher temperature increases strength by 12 MPa on average,” and that sentence would describe the constructed marginal mean contrast. It would be incomplete if used to predict what happens for Standard material, where the local difference is 6 MPa. Similarly, “enhanced material adds 10 MPa” is an average, not the 4 MPa increase at Low temperature. The statistical importance of interaction is precisely that a main-effect headline can omit the condition needed to act responsibly.

A marginal effect is not intrinsically wrong when an interaction exists. It answers a different question: what is the effect averaged over a stated mixture of the other factor? It is useful if an organization actually operates both material settings at those proportions. To change the mix, calculate a weighted average explicitly. For example, if 80% of production uses Standard and 20% Enhanced, the average temperature effect is 0.8×6+0.2×18 = 8.4 MPa, not 12 MPa. This is a target-mix calculation, not an additional experimental observation.

08. Interaction as a difference of differences

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\boxed{\widehat\Delta=(72-54)-(56-50)=12\;\mathrm{MPa}}\]

Compute the difference between the temperature effects: 18−6=12 MPa. Equivalently, take the difference between the material effects: 16−4=12 MPa. Written using the four means, interaction is (72−54)−(56−50)=12 MPa, or (72−56)−(54−50)=12 MPa. Both expressions must agree because ordinary subtraction is associative in this arrangement. The number has the same MPa units as the outcome.

The technical phrase difference of differences here means a contrast of four randomized factorial cell means. It quantifies departure from additivity: if an extra 6 MPa from temperature were independent of material, the Enhanced material would also show 6 MPa from temperature. In that additive hypothetical, the High–Enhanced mean predicted from 50 plus a 6-MPa temperature increment plus a 4-MPa material increment would be 60 MPa. Its actual constructed cell mean is 72 MPa. The additional 12 MPa is precisely the positive interaction.

Do not confuse this with an observational difference-in-differences causal design using before/after time comparisons and a parallel-trends assumption. The algebraic contrast has a similar shape, but the justification of causal interpretation is different. Here both factors are randomized interventions, and validity additionally needs correct implementation, no interference and well-defined treatments. The four-cell contrast by itself does not establish causality in an observational dataset.

09. Interaction plots: what parallel lines would mean

An interaction plot places temperature on the horizontal axis and separate lines for the two materials. Standard rises from 50 to 56 MPa, a slope across categories of +6 MPa. Enhanced rises from 54 to 72 MPa, a change of +18 MPa. Because the changes differ, the two lines are not parallel. Their separation widens from 4 MPa at Low to 16 MPa at High; the increase in separation is 12 MPa, matching the arithmetic interaction contrast.

These are two categorical temperature settings, so the drawn line merely joins the displayed means. It does not demonstrate that strength changes linearly at temperatures between Low and High. A smoother curve would require more tested levels and a model for dose response. Nor does an attractive line chart provide a sampling-uncertainty analysis: the raw means may vary across randomized experiments. We need within-cell replication to quantify precision and an appropriate inferential model to test interaction.

If the lines were parallel, the estimated interaction contrast would be zero. Parallelism in the sample would not prove the true interaction is exactly zero, and visually converging lines need not correspond to a statistically significant interaction. Graphs answer “what pattern?” while interval estimates and tests answer “how uncertain is it?” The useful order is: inspect four means, compute the contrast, compare the graph, then analyze error. This prevents the graphic from becoming decorative or a substitute for arithmetic.

10. Two complementary model languages

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[Y_{ijk}=\mu+\alpha_i+\beta_j+(\alpha\beta)_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ijk}\]

One way to express a two-factor study is a cell-means model: each observation equals the mean for its assigned combination plus a residual error. In symbols, for temperature i, material j and panel k, Y_ijk = μ_ij + ε_ijk. There are four μ values, one for each treatment combination. It is the most direct representation of the four-cell dataset and makes no assertion that temperature and material effects are additive.

An alternative effects formulation writes Y_ijk=μ+α_i+β_j+(αβ)_ij+ε_ijk, with constraints so that parameters are identifiable. It separates the grand level, the average temperature component, the average material component, and the interaction component. A third formulation uses 0/1 indicator coding and an explicit product term in regression. All three can reproduce the same four fitted means in a complete 2 × 2 design with interactions.

A model coefficient is meaningful only together with its coding. The interaction cannot simply be called a “12-unit main effect”; its interpretation is how a conditional contrast changes. We will derive the 0/1-coded coefficients from the four means before fitting anything with a package, and then show why classical ANOVA and regression return the same interaction evidence under ordinary balanced-model assumptions.

11. Derive the indicator regression coefficient by coefficient

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\boxed{\widehat{E}(Y\mid x,z)=50+6x+4z+12xz}\]

Set x=1 for High temperature and 0 for Low; set z=1 for Enhanced material and 0 for Standard. Consider E(Y|x,z)=β₀+β_A x+β_B z+β_AB xz. At Low–Standard, x=z=0, so the fitted cell mean is β₀=50. At High–Standard, x=1,z=0, giving β₀+β_A=56 and therefore β_A=6. At Low–Enhanced, x=0,z=1, giving β₀+β_B=54 and β_B=4.

At High–Enhanced, x=z=1. Substitute the first three coefficients: 50+6+4+β_AB=72; thus β_AB=12. The fitted response is 50+6x+4z+12xz, in MPa. Verify every row: (0,0)→50, (1,0)→56, (0,1)→54, (1,1)→72. This four-way substitution is a necessary consistency check. If any predicted cell mean disagreed, the contrast or coding would be wrong.

Notice what β_A=6 means: it is the temperature effect when z=0, not the overall 12-MPa main effect. At z=1, temperature effect is β_A+β_AB=6+12=18. Likewise β_B=4 is the material effect when x=0; at x=1, material effect is β_B+β_AB=4+12=16. In an interaction model, never report a coefficient without naming the reference condition.

12. The common effect-coding alternative

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\widehat{E}(Y\mid a,b)=58+6a+5b+3ab,\quad a,b\in\{-1,+1\}\]

Another valid coding uses a and b equal to −1 at Low/Standard and +1 at High/Enhanced. Write E(Y|a,b)=γ₀+γ_A a+γ_B b+γ_AB ab. Under this coding, the grand mean is γ₀=58, the temperature coefficient γ_A=12/2=6, the material coefficient γ_B=10/2=5, and the interaction coefficient γ_AB=12/4=3. Check Low–Standard: 58−6−5+3=50; High–Enhanced: 58+6+5+3=72.

Why does the interaction coefficient change from 12 to 3 even though the observed interaction does not? Each indicator moves by 1 under 0/1 coding but by 2 under −1/+1 coding. The product-based interaction contrast therefore carries a scaling factor of 4. Neither coefficient is “more correct.” The invariant scientific quantity is the specified four-mean contrast, which remains 12 MPa. A coefficient with an unspecified coding convention cannot be compared directly across reports.

Effect coding can make the coefficient of a factor look like half its equally weighted marginal effect. Reference coding makes coefficients direct differences at the reference setting. Both reproduce fitted means and predictions exactly. This example also explains why reading regression output without examining the design matrix can produce seemingly contradictory numbers. Always reconstruct at least one cell from the reported parameters and check the implied conditional comparison.

13. Residuals: what remains unexplained within each cell?

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\mathrm{SSE}=4(4+0+4+0)=32,\quad\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{32}{12}=\frac83\]

The fitted value for each panel in the full interaction model is its cell mean. For Low–Standard, observed strengths 48,50,52,50 around fitted 50 give residuals −2,0,+2,0 MPa. High–Standard around 56 gives the same residual pattern; Low–Enhanced around 54 and High–Enhanced around 72 also give the same pattern. There are four cells, each with squared residuals 4+0+4+0 = 8 MPa².

The sum of squared errors (SSE) is therefore 4×8=32 MPa². The number of residual degrees of freedom is 16 observations minus four fitted cell means = 12; each cell contributes 4−1=3 degrees of freedom. The pooled residual mean square is MSE=32/12=8/3≈2.6667 MPa², and its square root is about 1.633 MPa. This is an estimate of residual strength variability, not the expected effect of either treatment.

Residuals provide the denominator for factorial F tests and standard errors. Their variation depends on the measurement unit: if the same strengths were expressed in kPa, numerical sums of squares would scale by one million, but properly calculated F ratios would remain unchanged. If one cell had much larger scatter, a single pooled MSE might be inappropriate. Our constructed equal residual patterns make a textbook ANOVA transparent; real experiments require residual diagnostics rather than claiming model assumptions from visual neatness.

14. Decompose variation around the grand mean

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\mathrm{SST}=4(64+4+16+196)+32=1152\]

The overall mean is 58 MPa. The total sum of squares equals the sum of (individual strength −58)². It can be decomposed into within-cell variation SSE=32 and between-cell variation. The four cell means are 50,56,54,72, whose deviations from 58 are −8,−2,−4,+14. Square and weight each by four panels: 4×(64+4+16+196)=1120 MPa². Therefore SST=1120+32=1152 MPa².

The two-factor balanced ANOVA allocates the 1120 between-cell sum of squares into temperature, material and interaction. This decomposition is algebraically exact here because the balanced design makes the corresponding contrast columns orthogonal. If cells are unbalanced, sums of squares can depend on the chosen hypothesis and adjustment strategy, and simple formulas should not be copied blindly.

Do a direct check using the four cell totals 200,224,216,288: total 928, and 928/16=58. When an ANOVA table omits the data or cannot reproduce the grand mean, a student has no way to verify the result. We therefore keep both raw values and summary totals visible. The next sections derive each between-cell contribution rather than merely reporting output from software.

15. Temperature sum of squares from marginal means

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\mathrm{SS}_A=8(52-58)^2+8(64-58)^2=576\]

The mean strength at Low temperature is 52, and at High is 64; the grand mean is 58. There are eight panels at each temperature level. The temperature sum of squares is 8×(52−58)²+8×(64−58)² = 8×36+8×36 = 576 MPa². The temperature factor has 2−1=1 degree of freedom. Its mean square is MS_A=576/1=576 MPa².

This sum of squares measures variation explained by the equally weighted average contrast between temperature levels; it does not mean every Standard panel becomes 12 MPa stronger when heated. The marginal effect is 12 MPa even though the two conditional temperature effects are 6 and 18 MPa. Orthogonal decomposition tells us how variability is partitioned under balance, not which conditional scientific mechanism should be ignored.

A useful computational shortcut for a balanced 2 × 2 design with r replicates per cell is SS_A = r×(marginal temperature difference)². Here r=4 and the difference is 12: 4×12²=576. Verify this equals the long calculation. This formula will reappear for material, and a related factor of one-quarter appears for the interaction. We prefer to derive the sums once so the numerical shortcuts do not become unexplained rules.

16. Material sum of squares and its meaning

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\mathrm{SS}_B=8(53-58)^2+8(63-58)^2=400\]

The Standard marginal mean is 53; the Enhanced marginal mean is 63. Each is based on eight panels. Therefore SS_B=8×(53−58)²+8×(63−58)²=8×25+8×25=400 MPa². There is one degree of freedom, so MS_B=400 MPa². The difference 63−53=10 MPa also gives the shortcut SS_B=4×10²=400 MPa².

A larger sum of squares does not necessarily indicate a better investment. The material may cost far more than the extra strength is worth. Nor is SS_B the causal effect in MPa: it has squared MPa units, whereas the material contrast has MPa units. Explicit units provide a rapid check against confusing an ANOVA component with a directly interpretable treatment effect.

With interaction, report both the 10-MPa marginal material contrast and the conditional contrasts of 4 MPa at Low temperature and 16 MPa at High. If managers always operate at High temperature, the 16-MPa figure may be closer to their operational decision; if the target population mixes both levels equally, the marginal 10-MPa figure may answer the policy average. Neither is universally the “correct” number without a defined comparison.

17. Interaction sum of squares from a four-mean contrast

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\boxed{\mathrm{SS}_{AB}=\frac{r\widehat\Delta^2}{4}=\frac{4(12)^2}{4}=144}\]

Our difference-of-differences contrast is Δ=(72−54)−(56−50)=12 MPa. In a balanced 2 × 2 with r independent panels per cell, SS_AB=rΔ²/4. Substitution gives 4×12²/4=144 MPa². It has one degree of freedom, so MS_AB=144 MPa². The one-quarter factor is not arbitrary: the four contrast coefficients are +1,−1,−1,+1, and their squared coefficients add to four.

We can check this result without taking a formula on faith. The additive fitted mean for Low–Standard is grand 58 minus half temperature effect 6 minus half material effect 5 =47. Low–Enhanced is 57, High–Standard is 59, and High–Enhanced is 69. Compared with observed cell means 50,54,56,72, the departures are +3,−3,−3,+3 MPa. Squaring and multiplying by four panels per cell produces 4×(9+9+9+9)=144 MPa².

The two routes agree, so the interaction sum of squares represents variation the additive model cannot capture. If the true interaction were zero, the four population means would obey the additive equality μ11−μ01=μ10−μ00. An observed nonzero Δ could still arise by chance; significance assessment uses the residual MSE and the degrees of freedom, rather than declaring causality solely because 144 is positive.

18. The complete two-way ANOVA table

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\begin{aligned}F_A&=576/(8/3)=216\\F_B&=400/(8/3)=150\\F_{AB}&=144/(8/3)=54\end{aligned}\]

The decomposition is now fully specified: temperature SS 576 with 1 df; material SS 400 with 1 df; interaction SS 144 with 1 df; residual SS 32 with 12 df; total SS 1152 with 15 df. Check addition: 576+400+144+32=1152, and degrees of freedom 1+1+1+12=15, which equals N−1.

An F statistic divides a factor's mean square by the residual mean square when the tested model and assumptions make that comparison appropriate. Since MSE=8/3, the temperature F is 576/(8/3)=216, material F is 400/(8/3)=150, and interaction F is 144/(8/3)=54. All three have 1 numerator and 12 denominator degrees of freedom. Under the usual independent Normal equal-variance errors null model, these are compared with an F(1,12) reference distribution.

The interaction question should come first when deciding how to communicate a factorial experiment. A strong interaction means that summaries averaged over the other factor may not describe practical choices well. All three F tests may be significant simultaneously, but a small p-value for a marginal factor does not override a meaningfully different conditional effect. 19. Hypotheses, p-values and evidence

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\Delta)=12\pm t_{0.975,12}\sqrt{\frac{4(8/3)}4}\approx[8.44,15.56]\]

The interaction null hypothesis is H₀: Δ=0 in the population of eligible manufacturing conditions. The alternative is Δ≠0. The F statistic of 54, with (1,12) df, yields a very small p-value under the stated error assumptions. A p-value is the probability, assuming the null and model, of a statistic at least as extreme as the observed one. It is not the probability that Δ=0, nor the probability that a future panel will match its predicted cell mean.

An interval estimate is often more interpretable. The estimated Δ is 12 MPa. Its standard error under independent equal-variance cell observations is √[4MSE/r] = √[4×(8/3)/4] = √(8/3) ≈ 1.633 MPa. A 95% t interval with 12 df uses approximately 2.1788 as critical value: 12 ± 2.1788×1.633 ≈ [8.44,15.56] MPa. The interval refers to the interaction contrast; it is not a prediction interval for an individual strength.

Because the interaction has one degree of freedom, the squared t ratio equals the F statistic: (12/1.633)² ≈ 54. We have thus checked the inferential result through both ANOVA and regression-contrast logic. These inferential statements are conditional on randomization, independent units and a defensible error model. They are illustrative given the synthetic data; they should never be presented as experimentally established performance figures.

Independent software check: interaction F(1,12) = 54 and p = 8.8723193e-06.

20. The additive model is a scientific restriction

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[F_{\mathrm{partial}}=\frac{(176-32)/(13-12)}{32/12}=54\]

If an interaction term is omitted, the model asserts that changing temperature has the same expected effect for Standard and Enhanced material. This is an additivity restriction, not just a shorter mathematical expression. With the main effects estimated from our balanced data, the restricted fitted cell means are 47,59,57,69 MPa in Low–Standard, High–Standard, Low–Enhanced and High–Enhanced order. They differ systematically from the observed means 50,56,54,72 by ±3 MPa.

The additive model's residual sum of squares equals full-model SSE 32 plus the interaction SS 144 = 176 MPa². Removing one parameter changes the residual df from 12 to 13. The partial F test comparing restricted to full is [(176−32)/(13−12)]/(32/12)=144/(8/3)=54. This exactly matches the interaction F in the balanced ANOVA table. We did not need to fit a second dataset; the nested-model comparison uses the same sixteen observations.

It would be a mistake to omit the product term merely because a software summary makes the main-effect coefficients easier to read. If interaction is physically important, the reduced model averages away an essential conditional pattern. Conversely, in a noisy small sample, a nonsignificant interaction test does not prove additivity. Decisions about simplification should consider uncertainty, study power, scientific relevance and prespecified modeling goals.

21. Randomization supports causal claims only under conditions

Because temperature and material are both deliberately assigned, a well-conducted experiment can support causal interpretations of comparisons between treatment combinations. Imagine each eligible panel has four Potential Outcomes, one strength under each temperature–material pair. Only one is observed per panel. Random assignment makes the chosen treatment independent of the vector of potential outcomes in the design's probability mechanism, allowing the average cell comparisons to estimate causal contrasts over the eligible units.

That argument needs assumptions. The treatment settings must be sufficiently well-defined and delivered as assigned. No interference means one panel's treatment does not alter another panel's outcome; shared ovens could violate it through heat spillover. Consistency means the outcome under the assigned version corresponds to the specified treatment. Comparable testing protocols and no differential missing results are also needed. Randomization does not repair a defective strength instrument or treatment cross-contamination.

The numeric difference of differences is not automatically causal in observational data. If the factory merely records which temperatures operators choose, material choice, production shift, batch and expected quality might all affect assignment. The same contrast can then reflect confounding or selection. A parallel-trends premise belongs to certain observational time-based difference-in-differences analyses; it is not what identifies our randomized factorial contrast. Keep the algebra and design justification distinct.

22. Balance, replication and why sample size matters

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\Delta)=\sqrt{\frac{4\sigma^2}{r}}=\frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt r}\]

With four independent panels per cell, N=2×2×4=16. A contrast of four independent cell means with common residual variance σ² has variance 4σ²/r. Its standard error therefore falls with the square root of replication: SE(Δ)=2σ/√r. If all else stayed the same and replication increased from four to sixteen panels per cell, expected standard error would halve. This precision gain is a property of independent replication, not of taking repeated instrument readings from the same panel.

Balance also makes main-effect and interaction contrasts orthogonal in the elementary fixed-effects ANOVA. This simplifies interpretation and calculation: sums of squares add without ambiguity. It does not ensure perfect random baseline equality, and it cannot prevent unexpected process failures. A strong experimental plan includes practical constraints, run order, blinding of the strength assessor where possible, calibration and an outcome measurement protocol.

Four observations per cell suffice to illustrate mathematics, but they are not a universal sample-size recommendation. A real experiment should define a minimum relevant interaction in MPa, reasonable residual variation, desired precision or power and production costs before collecting data. Designing only for a main effect may leave poor power for the interaction that actually drives the decision. Our example's clean signals are intentionally pedagogical and should not be mistaken for typical industrial precision.

23. Randomization schemes and nuisance variables

Completely randomizing sixteen panels among four cells is simple when all panels can be made and tested under comparable circumstances. But factories operate across shifts, batches, furnaces and technicians. If one furnace always runs at High temperature and another always at Low, furnace and temperature are confounded; measuring many panels in each furnace does not magically create independent temperature assignments. The experimental unit may actually be the furnace run, not the panel.

A randomized complete block factorial design could allocate all four combinations within each of several production batches, if the process permits. That design requires a model reflecting blocks and the correct residual structure. A split-plot may be necessary when temperature changes are expensive and applied to whole runs while material varies across panels inside a run. Then tests of temperature cannot use panel-to-panel error as if temperature were randomized independently to every panel.

These are extensions, not substitutions to make without revisiting the assignment mechanism. The central guide intentionally analyzes the fully randomized replicated 2 × 2, where each panel is an independent treatment unit. Learning to recognize when that assumption fails is itself a transferable skill. A valid calculation on the wrong assignment unit is an invalid scientific inference.

24. Diagnostics: what the classical F test assumes

The classical fixed-effects factorial ANOVA model assumes residual errors with mean zero, independence across experimental units, equal variance across the four treatment combinations and, for exact small-sample F reference distributions, Normal errors. Randomization supports assignment-based causal comparisons, but it does not by itself establish Normality or constant error variance. Observed residuals should be studied by cell and by run order.

In this example, all cells share residuals −2,0,2,0 MPa. This is compatible with equal sample variances by construction, but it is not proof that underlying variances are equal. A plot of residuals against fitted cell means can expose changing spread, and a Q–Q plot can help inspect severe deviations from Normal shape. With four values per cell, formal diagnostics have limited ability to detect problems. Replication counts, physical measurement processes and outlying observations remain relevant.

If variances differ markedly or measurements cluster by batch, classical pooled-MSE uncertainty may be misleading. Heteroscedasticity-robust approaches, randomization-based methods or hierarchical models may be more suitable depending on assignment and sample size. None should be applied mechanically without understanding what quantity they test. Small-sample robust methods can also behave poorly. The design should guide inference before a convenient software setting does.

25. Decisions under interaction: choose combinations, not slogans

Among our four observed cell means, High–Enhanced is highest at 72 MPa, followed by High–Standard 56, Low–Enhanced 54 and Low–Standard 50. If the only objective were to maximize expected strength and implementation were feasible, High–Enhanced would be the sample-based preferred combination. But the higher temperature and enhanced material could impose energy, production time, emissions or component costs that are absent from our strength dataset.

Suppose increased temperature costs an additional $8 per panel and enhanced material costs $10 per panel. Those are new hypothetical costs, not observed manufacturing data. A utility function would need to convert strength improvements to comparable economic or safety value. If each extra MPa were worth $1, High–Enhanced increases mean strength by 22 MPa versus Low–Standard but costs $18 more, yielding a hypothetical net gain of $4; Low–Enhanced gains 4 MPa for $10, yielding −$6. The preferred choice depends on the value assigned to performance and reliability.

An equally important decision question is generalizability. A single set of synthetic panel means does not establish strength across product lots, temperatures within the categories, or long-run durability. Repeat experiments across relevant batches with valid assignment, confidence intervals and clear decision thresholds. In an interaction study, the scientific conclusion should explicitly name the combinations being compared.

26. Communicate factorial findings without misrepresenting causality

A good report contains the experimental unit, number randomized to each cell, exact treatment definitions, all four cell means with units, conditional effects, an interaction contrast with uncertainty, an interaction plot and model assumptions. State whether any panels were missing, whether the planned randomization was followed, and whether sensitivity analyses changed conclusions. If the reader cannot reconstruct the 12-MPa interaction from the displayed means, the report has hidden its most important operation.

Distinguish estimated effect size from statistical significance. A 12-MPa contrast may be scientifically large or small depending on process tolerances, and a very small p-value is not a business-utility measure. State the target population: eligible panels under the specified temperature and material settings, not all possible materials and temperatures. Report factor labels and coding when presenting regression coefficients so a reference-condition effect is not misread as a marginal average.

The results in this guide are mathematical facts about a deliberately fabricated table, not findings about existing manufactured products. The verified identities provide a reusable analysis workflow, but genuine scientific conclusions require measured data, a credible design and transparent limitations. The following exercises require the student to re-derive rather than merely recognize the formulas.

27. Exercise 1 — reconstruct four means from raw panels

Problem. Using the sixteen strengths shown in Section 4, compute every cell total and mean, the grand mean and the residual squared sum for Low–Standard. Explain the sample unit and the units of every result. Solution. Low–Standard totals 48+50+52+50=200; 200/4=50 MPa. High–Standard totals 54+56+58+56=224; mean 56 MPa. Low–Enhanced totals 52+54+56+54=216; mean 54 MPa. High–Enhanced totals 70+72+74+72=288; mean 72 MPa. Overall 200+224+216+288=928, and 928/16=58 MPa. Low–Standard residuals are −2,0,2,0, giving 4+0+4+0=8 MPa².

Why this works. Independent panels, rather than repeated instrument readings, contribute one observation each. Each cell is equally replicated, so the average of the four cell means also equals the grand mean. A squared residual is measured in MPa², not MPa. Verification. The four cell totals add to the overall 928 and the residuals within Low–Standard sum to zero. If these checks fail, later sums of squares must be redone. Transfer. With unequal cell sizes, replace the simple average of cell means by a count-weighted average when the target is the observed-panel grand mean.

28. Exercise 2 — two simple temperature effects

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\widehat\tau_{A\mid B=0}=6,\qquad\widehat\tau_{A\mid B=1}=18\]

Problem. Calculate temperature effects separately for Standard and Enhanced materials. Can one 12-MPa marginal claim replace both? Solution. Standard: 56−50=6 MPa. Enhanced: 72−54=18 MPa. The equal-weight marginal contrast is (6+18)/2=12 MPa. That average correctly summarizes a 50/50 material target but predicts neither condition-specific change exactly. With all panels using Standard, expect the relevant contrast to be 6 MPa, not 12.

Reasoning. A conditional effect answers “what changes at this material?” while a marginal effect averages over materials. The two quantities are different because the four means are not additive. Check. The marginal temperature means 64 and 52 differ by 12, agreeing with the average of the two simple effects. Interpretation. The chart should have a shallower Standard line and steeper Enhanced line. The effect is a difference in mean MPa, not a percent. A decision rule that specifies only “increase temperature” ignores the mechanism-dependent context.

29. Exercise 3 — compute interaction in two orders

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\widehat\Delta=(72-54)-(56-50)=12\]

Problem. Derive the difference-of-differences contrast by temperature first and by material first. Solution. Temperature comparisons give 18−6=12 MPa. Material comparisons give (72−56)−(54−50)=16−4=12 MPa. Equivalently, 72−54−56+50=12. This agreement comes from applying the same four coefficients +1,−1,−1,+1 to the cell means.

Check. Under an additive hypothesis with baseline 50, Standard temperature gain 6 and Low-temperature material gain 4, High–Enhanced should equal 50+6+4=60. Actual 72−60=12 MPa. Interpretation. A positive interaction means the combined High–Enhanced combination exceeds what the additive model predicts by 12 MPa. It does not imply that 12 MPa is a new stand-alone treatment or that the experiment reveals a particular molecular pathway. In an observational dataset, the same arithmetic would not prove a causal synergy.

30. Exercise 4 — compute main effects with a different target mix

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[0.8(6)+0.2(18)=8.4\;\mathrm{MPa}\]

Problem. If a future production line uses Standard 80% and Enhanced 20%, what is the average temperature effect? Solution. First identify the target weights: w_S=0.8, w_E=0.2, summing to 1. Multiply each conditional temperature contrast by its weight: 0.8×6=4.8, 0.2×18=3.6, total 8.4 MPa. This is not an estimate from newly observed data; it is a reweighting of the existing conditional means to the stated production mix.

Verification. Weighted Low strength =0.8×50+0.2×54=50.8 MPa. Weighted High strength =0.8×56+0.2×72=59.2 MPa. Their difference is 59.2−50.8=8.4 MPa. Interpretation. The 12-MPa equal-weight average is not automatically relevant for an 80/20 operating mix. A report should identify which target distribution defines any marginal effect. Generalizing beyond the two tested temperatures still requires data at additional levels.

31. Exercise 5 — reconstruct the regression without a package

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\widehat Y=50+6x+4z+12xz\]

Problem. Fit a saturated 0/1 indicator model and interpret β_A and β_AB. Solution. The model is β₀+β_A x+β_B z+β_AB xz. From Low–Standard, β₀=50. High–Standard implies β_A=56−50=6. Low–Enhanced implies β_B=54−50=4. High–Enhanced implies β_AB=72−(50+6+4)=12. Therefore E(Y|x,z)=50+6x+4z+12xz MPa.

Verification. Substitute all four (x,z) pairs to recover 50,56,54,72, respectively. Interpretation. β_A=6 is the temperature effect for Standard material, since z=0; β_A+β_AB=18 is its effect with Enhanced material. β_AB=12 is a change in temperature contrast, not an additional 12 MPa automatically added whenever temperature alone rises. A model without the xz term would impose equal conditional temperature effects and fail to reproduce all four cell means.

32. Exercise 6 — change contrast coding without changing the science

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\widehat Y=58+6a+5b+3ab\]

Problem. With a,b∈{−1,+1}, determine coefficients γ in γ₀+γ_A a+γ_B b+γ_AB ab. Solution. γ₀ equals the equal-cell grand mean 58. The equally weighted temperature effect is 12, so γ_A=6. Material effect is 10, so γ_B=5. Interaction contrast is 12 and the coding scaling factor is four, so γ_AB=3. The fitted model is 58+6a+5b+3ab.

Verification. Low–Standard gives 58−6−5+3=50; High–Standard gives 58+6−5−3=56; Low–Enhanced gives 58−6+5−3=54; High–Enhanced gives 58+6+5+3=72. Lesson. β_AB=12 and γ_AB=3 are both correct under their stated codings. The physically meaningful four-cell contrast remains 12 MPa. Before comparing published coefficients, recover the model's coding and reconstruct the same mean comparison.

33. Exercise 7 — calculate SSE and residual degrees of freedom

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\mathrm{SSE}=32,\quad df_E=12,\quad\mathrm{MSE}=8/3\]

Problem. Compute the pooled residual mean square of the saturated interaction model. Solution. Each cell has residuals −2,0,2,0 about its fitted mean. Squared residuals sum to 4+0+4+0=8 MPa² in each cell. Across four cells, SSE=4×8=32 MPa². Sixteen independent panel observations and four fitted cell means leave 16−4=12 residual df. Thus MSE=32/12=2.6667 MPa² and root MSE≈1.633 MPa.

Verification. Each cell has three residual df, giving 4×3=12. The residuals within a cell sum to zero as least-squares fitting with a cell intercept requires. Interpretation. MSE measures residual variation around fitted cell means and supports uncertainty calculations. It is not an effect measured in MPa; its units are MPa². If one panel had been measured four times, the independent experimental-unit count would not automatically quadruple.

34. Exercise 8 — reconstruct factorial sums of squares

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\mathrm{SS}_A+\mathrm{SS}_B+\mathrm{SS}_{AB}+\mathrm{SSE}=576+400+144+32=1152\]

Problem. Determine SS_A, SS_B and SS_AB and verify their partition. Solution. Temperature marginal difference is 12; with r=4, SS_A=r×12²=576 MPa². Material difference is 10; SS_B=4×10²=400 MPa². Interaction Δ=12; SS_AB=r×12²/4=144 MPa². Within-cell SSE=32, so total =576+400+144+32=1152 MPa².

Independent check. The cell means deviate from grand 58 by −8,−2,−4,+14. Their between-cell SS is 4×(64+4+16+196)=1120 MPa²; add SSE 32 to obtain 1152 again. Interpretation. Main effects and interaction partition explained squared deviations, not direct gains in strength. Do not read 576 as 576 MPa of extra strength. The clean additive partition relies on a balanced orthogonal design.

35. Exercise 9 — test the interaction and compute its interval

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[F_{AB}=\left(\frac{12}{\sqrt{8/3}}\right)^2=54\]

Problem. Calculate the interaction F, the equivalent t and a 95% interval. Solution. Interaction MS=144/1=144 MPa²; MSE=32/12=8/3 MPa². Hence F=144/(8/3)=54, with (1,12) degrees of freedom. SE(Δ)=√(4MSE/r)=√(8/3)=1.63299 MPa. The t ratio is 12/1.63299≈7.34847; squaring gives 54.

Using t_{0.975,12}≈2.17881, margin≈2.17881×1.63299≈3.558; 95% CI≈[8.44,15.56] MPa. Interpretation. Under the classical independent Normal equal-variance error model, this interval excludes zero and the F test finds strong evidence of an interaction. This is a claim about a population interaction contrast, not the probability that a specific panel gains 12 MPa. The data are fictional and cannot support a real industrial efficacy claim.

36. Exercise 10 — compare full and additive models

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\frac{(176-32)/(13-12)}{32/12}=54\]

Problem. Show how removing interaction changes SSE and compute a nested-model F statistic. Solution. The full model fits the four means and has SSE=32, residual df 12. The additive restricted model predicts cell means 47,59,57,69, resulting in a systematic ±3 MPa cell-level discrepancy. Weighted squared difference is 4×(3²+3²+3²+3²)=144 MPa². Thus restricted SSE=32+144=176; restricted residual df 13.

The partial F ratio is [(176−32)/(13−12)]/(32/12)=144/(8/3)=54. Check. It equals the direct interaction F; the comparisons use identical observed panels. Interpretation. The additive model cannot capture the disproportionate benefit at High–Enhanced. Removing a term is a scientifically testable restriction, not merely cosmetic simplification. This result does not authorize fitting a highly complex model with far more factors than the design can estimate.

37. Exercise 11 — detect pseudoreplication

Problem. Four panels per cell are each tested three times. A technician treats all 48 readings as independent panel replicates. Explain why this fails. Solution. There are still 16 independently randomized panels: treatment was assigned once to each panel. The three instrument readings within a panel can share the panel's microscopic defects and calibration drift. Taking their average produces one panel-level strength and preserves independent unit count 16 for simple ANOVA.

Check. If each panel's repeated readings were identical, tripling every data row should plainly provide no new independently assigned treatment evidence. Yet a naive 48-row ANOVA would pretend it did, artificially reducing uncertainty. Transfer. If a whole furnace run receives one temperature, then even different panels within that run may not be independent temperature units. A split-plot or clustered model is needed. Interpretation. Correct uncertainty follows the assignment mechanism, not merely the number of cells in a spreadsheet.

38. Exercise 12 — make and defend a conditional recommendation

Problem. An operations manager reports only “heat adds 12 MPa and enhanced material adds 10 MPa,” then chooses Enhanced material at Low temperature expecting +10 MPa. Correct this decision. Solution. At Low temperature the relevant material contrast is 54−50=4 MPa, not the 10-MPa equal-weight marginal contrast. At High temperature it is 72−56=16 MPa. Similarly, heat adds 6 MPa for Standard and 18 MPa for Enhanced. The high–enhanced cell mean of 72 MPa is the highest constructed mean, but costs, failure thresholds, process stability and uncertainty are not included.

Verification. The manager's two marginal effects average correctly, but a single-setting prediction requires the corresponding simple effect. Reconstruct the four fitted means using 50+6x+4z+12xz before stating any gain. Limit. Randomized factorial assignment can identify causal contrasts for eligible panels when interventions are defined, implemented and noninterfering. It cannot turn synthetic outcomes into real plant evidence or establish a continuous temperature-response curve between tested settings. Student test. A learner should be able to explain both why the 12-MPa interaction is mathematically correct and why its validity in a factory depends on actual randomization and measurement.

39. Intervals for simple effects and why they differ from the interaction interval

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\tau_{A\mid B})=\sqrt{\frac{2(8/3)}4}=\sqrt{\frac43}\approx1.1547\]

The interaction interval describes a difference between two simple effects. It should not be confused with either simple-effect interval. With four independent observations per cell and pooled MSE=8/3, the variance of a difference between two cell means is 2MSE/r. Its standard error is √[(2×8/3)/4]=√(4/3)≈1.1547 MPa. At Standard material the estimated temperature effect is 6 MPa, so its classical 95% interval is 6±2.1788×1.1547≈[3.48,8.52] MPa. At Enhanced material the effect is 18 MPa and the corresponding interval is approximately [15.48,20.52] MPa.

The simple effects differ by 12 MPa. Their difference has variance 4MSE/r, twice the variance of one simple effect, because it combines four independent cell means instead of two. That gives the larger interaction standard error 1.633 MPa and interval [8.44,15.56]. Comparing whether two separate 95% intervals overlap is not the correct general test of interaction. Their exact covariance structure, the four-cell contrast and the appropriate standard error must be used.

These individual 95% intervals are not automatically simultaneous 95% coverage statements for all effects. If a study examines many simple effects, interactions and subgroup contrasts, reporting only those that appear significant creates a multiplicity and selection problem. Prespecify priority contrasts and consider appropriate multiplicity adjustments where the inferential family demands them (Guide 46). Distinguish the nominal coverage of each interval from joint coverage across multiple intervals.

40. The logic of a factorial randomization test

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[|\Omega|=\frac{16!}{(4!)^4}\]

The classical interaction F test uses a pooled residual variance and a model-based F reference distribution. A randomization test instead derives a reference distribution from the actual treatment-allocation mechanism under a sufficiently specified null hypothesis. For the sixteen-panel completely randomized experiment with exactly four panels assigned to each labeled cell, there are 16!/(4!4!4!4!) balanced allocations. An assignment-based algorithm could enumerate or randomly sample those allocations, recompute a chosen interaction statistic, and compare the observed value with its assignment distribution.

There is an important conceptual obstacle: the null statement that the average interaction is zero does not specify every panel's missing Potential Outcomes. A simple Fisher randomization test requires a sharp null, such as no effect of any of the four assigned combinations on any panel, under which the observed outcome would remain fixed under every reassignment. That sharp null is stronger than a zero average interaction and tests more than absence of interaction. Permuting labels under an additive-but-nonzero-effect null without further modeling is generally not justified merely by calling it a permutation test.

Thus the randomized design strengthens causal identification, but it does not make all possible null hypotheses equally easy to test exactly. For this guide we use the transparent classical F approach and show how it relates to a regression contrast. A defensible design-based analysis would state the sharp or weak null, assignment mechanism and test statistic separately. This distinction prevents a misleading claim that any shuffled p-value is automatically a model-free causal interaction test.

41. Design power: the interaction is often the hardest target

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\Delta)\approx\frac{2(4)}{\sqrt r}=\frac8{\sqrt r}\]

A practical experiment is planned around a minimum scientifically relevant interaction rather than the difference that happens to appear after inspecting results. Suppose engineers regard a 3-MPa change in temperature effect between materials as the smallest interaction worth detecting, and suppose a pilot experiment suggests a residual standard deviation of 4 MPa. With r independently assigned panels per cell, the interaction standard error would be approximately 2×4/√r=8/√r MPa, under the common-variance model.

For example, r=4 would give an anticipated standard error of 4 MPa, so a true interaction of 3 MPa would have a signal-to-standard-error ratio of only 0.75. That is weak information, not a basis for promising a reliable significance result. Increasing r to 16 would reduce anticipated SE to 2 MPa and raise the ratio to 1.5, still not a guarantee of high power. Formal power analysis must use the intended test, significance level, alternative effect and relevant degrees of freedom; this rough ratio is an intuition check, not a complete sample-size calculation.

The fabricated main example has unusually small residual variation and a large interaction; it is engineered to make the steps observable, not to demonstrate that real interactions are easy to detect. A design with insufficient replication may conclude “not statistically significant” despite scientifically important uncertainty. An ethical report then states imprecision rather than interpreting non-rejection as evidence that two treatments behave identically.

42. A counterexample: large main effects without interaction

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\Delta=(57-47)-(50-40)=0\]

Consider another hypothetical 2 × 2 set of population means, distinct from the sixteen-panel dataset: Low–Standard=40, High–Standard=50, Low–Enhanced=47, High–Enhanced=57 MPa. Temperature effect is 50−40=10 MPa with Standard and 57−47=10 MPa with Enhanced. Material effect is 47−40=7 MPa at Low and 57−50=7 MPa at High. The interaction contrast is (57−47)−(50−40)=10−10=0 MPa.

Here both main effects are substantial, yet the response is exactly additive. The fitted 0/1 model has intercept 40, temperature coefficient 10, material coefficient 7 and interaction coefficient 0. The two lines in an interaction plot would be parallel. This case shows that observing benefits from both factors does not imply their interaction is positive. Combined benefit and statistical interaction answer different questions. An additive model can favor a combined High–Enhanced choice even when the interaction contrast is zero.

Compare that case with the main dataset: there, High–Enhanced exceeds the additive prediction by 12 MPa. A classroom reader should be able to construct both graphs and explain why a large combined level does not determine whether the lines are parallel. What determines interaction is the difference in slopes across factor levels, not the tallest cell mean or the number of individually positive contrasts.

43. A second counterexample: crossover changes the preferred setting

Core equation · reconstruct the contrast
\[\Delta=(65-70)-(75-60)=-20\;\mathrm{MPa}\]

Now consider a separate fictional four-mean table: Low–Standard=60, High–Standard=75, Low–Enhanced=70, High–Enhanced=65 MPa. The temperature effect with Standard is 75−60=+15 MPa, while with Enhanced it is 65−70=−5 MPa. The interaction is −5−15=−20 MPa. The temperature marginal mean for Low is (60+70)/2=65 and for High is (75+65)/2=70, giving an average effect of +5 MPa.

A manager who sees only the +5-MPa marginal gain could recommend High temperature for every panel. That recommendation is wrong for Enhanced material, where High reduces mean strength by 5 MPa. This pattern is called a crossover interaction because which temperature is preferred depends on the other factor. In the interaction graph, the Standard line rises and the Enhanced line falls; the lines cross between the two displayed settings when connected illustratively.

There are no raw individual replicates in this additional table, so no defensible residual standard error, confidence interval or F test can be calculated from these means alone. The example is only about interpreting the signs and decisions implied by a four-mean pattern. It reinforces why replication and uncertainty matter: a crossover in observed means can reflect a real qualitative interaction or sampling noise. The rigorous next step would be to design and replicate an experiment that estimates each condition-specific effect with acceptable precision.

44. Further scientific questions and disciplined extensions

A real team might next ask whether the interaction remains stable across supplier batches, whether curing energy has a nonlinear temperature relationship, or whether a third factor such as cooling speed changes the effect of material. Those are meaningful follow-up experiments. Adding more factors expands the design: three binary factors have 2³=8 combinations, and a fully crossed study with four independent replicates per combination would have 32 experimental units. The new three-way interaction compares how a two-way interaction changes across the third factor's levels; it should not be interpreted without specifying all conditions.

Fractional factorial designs intentionally test a subset of combinations, often to control cost, but can alias effects. Random-effects and split-plot designs address different forms of replication and assignment. Their analysis requires additional assumptions and is outside the full derivation of this introductory guide. A reader should not reuse the 12 residual degrees of freedom or the simple sums-of-squares identities when a future design changes its experimental unit or balance.

The reusable workflow is to name the four interventions, randomize eligible independent units, record complete outcomes, compute cell means, calculate conditional contrasts, inspect interaction graph, derive the regression and ANOVA decomposition, quantify uncertainty, examine assumptions, and state an action under an explicit objective. This sequence is more valuable than memorizing an isolated p-value. It applies to A/B product features, agriculture, manufacturing and medicine only when each domain's randomization and outcome definitions are valid.
