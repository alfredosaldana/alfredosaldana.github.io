01Why an estimate changes while the population stays fixed

Two random samples can give different averages without any student changing a score. The variation comes from which students were selected. A Sampling Distribution describes this variation by assigning probabilities to the possible values of a statistic under a specified sampling procedure. It is the bridge between a calculation on one sample and reasoning about how that calculation would behave across other samples.

This guide builds that bridge explicitly. We will enumerate every sample in a small population, calculate several statistics on each, and construct their exact probability laws. We will then derive their centers and spreads, compare sampling with and without replacement, and use simulation to approximate a law we already know. The point is to understand what varies, what remains fixed, and why a sampling design is part of every probability statement.

The complete teaching population contains four fictitious students with quiz scores 60,70,80,and 90 points. These are constructed values, not records from a school. Their small number lets us inspect every selection and verify formulas independently. We keep the same population throughout most of the guide so that changes in results can be traced to a statistic, sample size, or design rather than an unexplained change of context.

The prerequisites are sampling design from Guide 04, dependence from 13, Normal distributions from 21, and Monte Carlo reasoning from 24. Necessary definitions and algebra are recalled locally. Guide 26 will examine estimator quality more systematically, Guide 27 will deepen Standard Error, and Guide 28 will develop the CLT. Here an exact finite distribution must not be called Normal merely because it looks symmetric.

02State the complete population and its fixed parameters

Label the students A=60, B=70, C=80, and D=90. Population size is N=4; the population total is τ=300 points and its mean is μ=300/4=75 points. In design-based reasoning, these four scores are fixed. Randomness enters through selection of identities, not through inventing a new score for a selected student.

Population variance requires a named convention. The distribution of a uniformly selected individual gives mass 1/4 to each score, so its variance uses divisor N:

σ²=[(60−75)²+(70−75)²+(80−75)²+(90−75)²]/4.=(225+25+25+225)/4=125 points².

Its SD is√125≈11.18034 points. Survey-sampling formulas also use the fixed finite-population quantity SN² with divisor N−1. Here SN²=500/3≈166.66667 points². These are two definitions of population spread, not two estimates from different samples. They satisfy SN²=Nσ²/(N−1).

Define success as a score at least 80. Students C and D qualify, so the fixed population proportion is p=2/4=.5. This threshold is a classification rule for this example, not a claim about a school's passing standard.

03Make the sampling procedure part of the model

Our principal design selects two distinct students uniformly without replacement. Every unordered pair has probability 1/C(4,2)=1/6. This is Simple Random Sampling Without Replacement, abbreviated SRSWOR. The sample size n=2 counts students within one selected sample; it is not the population size N or the number of simulated repetitions B.

One implementation draws a first identity uniformly from four and a second uniformly from the remaining three. Each ordered sequence of distinct identities has probability(1/4)(1/3)=1/12. An unordered pair combines two possible orders, so its probability is 2/12=1/6. Because our statistics do not depend on order, six unordered pairs are the convenient sample space.

Within a sample, the two selections are dependent. After selecting A, the chance of selecting A next is zero. Before the first draw, each student is equally likely in either draw position; this equal marginal law does not imply independence. We will derive how the dependence changes variance.

Between hypothetical repetitions, restore the full population before selecting another pair. A student can appear in several different hypothetical samples without appearing twice within any one sample. Repeated sampling is a thought experiment describing the design, not a demand to collect six disjoint pairs from four students.

A convenience rule such as “choose the two highest scores” gives a different distribution: there is only one selected pair. Equal probabilities belong to the stipulated random design, not to the mere existence of a list of possible samples.

04Treat a statistic as a function of a random sample

Before sampling, X1 and X2 denote the scores selected by the random procedure. A statistic is a specified function of these sampled values. It can vary because the input sample varies. After selection we write lowercase x1,x2 and a realized statistic; the calculation is then fixed even though it arose from a random mechanism.

For a general sample of size n, define the sum T, mean X̄, success proportion p̂, and sample variance S²:

T=ΣXi; X̄=T/n; p̂=Σ1(Xi≥80)/n.S²=Σ(Xi−X̄)²/(n−1), n≥2.

The notation 1(condition) means 1 when the condition is true and 0 otherwise. It lets us represent the count of qualifying students as a sum of indicators. Mean and sum measure points, proportion is dimensionless, and sample variance measures points squared. A sample SD S=√S² again measures points, but describes a different quantity from the mean's Standard Error.

For realized pair A,D with scores 60 and 90, T=150, x̄=75, p̂=.5, and s²=[(60−75)²+(90−75)²]/1=450. Its SD is√450≈21.21320 points. None of those realized numbers alone lists the probability distribution of the corresponding statistic.

The sample mean 75 happens to equal μ, but this does not make the sample a census or guarantee that every statistic equals its population counterpart. Selecting B,C also produces mean 75, while its sample variance is only 50. A statistic summarizes specified information; different samples can share one summary and differ sharply in another.

05Keep population, observed sample, and sampling law separate

The population distribution describes a uniformly selected student's score. Its four values are 60,70,80,and 90 with probabilities .25 each. The empirical distribution of the observed sample A,D describes the two selected scores, 60 and 90, with relative frequencies .5 each. A sampling distribution instead describes the statistic's possible values over repetitions of the selection rule.

For the two-student mean without replacement, the possible values will be 65,70,75,80,and 85. The population has four score values, the observed sample contains two scores, and the mean's sampling law has five values. There is no reason their supports or probabilities should agree: they are distributions of different objects.

The figure uses the same horizontal point scale to prevent a change in units from creating an artificial visual contrast. The vertical quantities are probability mass for the population and sampling law, and empirical relative frequency for the one observed sample. All are dimensionless, but they answer different questions. A stem at 75 in the sampling panel concerns an average, not a student who necessarily scored 75.

An important practical consequence follows. A histogram of one sample is not automatically a histogram of repeated sample means. To construct the latter, a mechanism must generate possible samples and a statistic must be recalculated on each. In real applications the full population law is usually unknown, so theoretical assumptions, design information or approximation methods are needed. Our complete teaching population lets us see the distinction without pretending that one sample reveals everything.

There are C(4,2)=4·3/(2·1)=6 distinct unordered pairs. List each once and assign probability 1/6. The probability belongs to a pair of identities, even if two students in another example happened to share the same score. Counting distinct score patterns instead of identity selections could give incorrect weights when scores repeat.

07Build the probability law of the sample mean

To construct the mean's distribution, collect all sample-space probabilities leading to the same mean. The value 75 occurs for A,D and B,C, so it receives 2/6=1/3. Each other mean occurs once and receives 1/6. Equiprobable samples do not imply equiprobable statistic values.

A general finite formulation makes the aggregation precise. If ω indexes a possible sample and t(ω) is its statistic, then P(T=t) is the sum of P(ω) over all samples satisfying t(ω)=t. The same operation works when samples have unequal probabilities, provided the correct weights are used. Counting outputs alone is sufficient only when sample-space outcomes are equiprobable.

For the observed mean 75, the sampling probability is 1/3. This is a pre-sampling statement about the design and the fixed population. It is not a probability that the unknown population mean equals 75 after seeing the data; our teaching population mean is already fixed and known. Reversing the direction would require a separate inferential framework.

The distribution is discrete. Its five masses sum to 1, and values such as 72 or 90 are impossible for this particular without-replacement n=2 mean. A smooth Normal curve would assign probability to ranges containing unsupported means and would not be this exact law. Later approximation theory may justify other calculations in other settings, but symmetry and a familiar bell-like outline cannot change the sample space we have enumerated.

08Calculate the center and spread from the exact law

Expectation weights every possible statistic value by its probability. We can use the five distinct means and their masses, or the six equiprobable pair rows. Both represent the same law. Using the rows makes the arithmetic particularly transparent:

E[X̄]=(65+70+75+75+80+85)/6=450/6=75.Var(X̄)=[(65−75)²+(70−75)²+0²+0²+(80−75)²+(85−75)²]/6.=(100+25+0+0+25+100)/6=125/3 points².

The SD of this sampling distribution is called the Standard Error of the sample mean: SE(X̄)=√(125/3)≈6.45497 points. Here it is known exactly because the population and design are completely specified. It describes variation between possible sample means, not spread between individual scores in one sample.

As an independent arithmetic check, E[X̄²]=34000/6=17000/3. Subtract(E[X̄])²=75²=5625 to recover 125/3. The raw second moment is much larger than the variance because it includes the squared location of the distribution. Forgetting to center would confuse location with sampling fluctuation.

For realized pair A,D, the observed mean is 75 and its realized error x̄−μ is zero. Nevertheless its sampling procedure has SE 6.45497. A zero realized error does not imply zero uncertainty before sampling. Conversely, pair A,B has realized error−10, which is larger in magnitude than one SE but remains a possible outcome. A standard deviation is not a hard upper bound on every draw.

09Translate a question into an event about the statistic

A question about a sample mean must be evaluated using its sampling law. For example, “the two-student average is at least 80” includes mean 80 from B,D and mean 85 from C,D. Their disjoint probabilities add:

P(X̄≥80)=1/6+1/6=1/3.

This differs from P(X≥80)=2/4=.5 for one uniformly selected student. The threshold uses the same point units but applies to a different random object. The finite law tells us directly which samples contribute; there is no need to substitute a Normal approximation.

A second question asks whether the mean misses μ by at least 10 points. Its event is |X̄−75|≥10, which includes 65 and 85 and therefore also has probability 1/3. By contrast, |X̄−75|≤5 includes 70,75,and 80 with total probability 1/6+1/3+1/6=2/3. Endpoints matter: “at least” includes equality and “less than” excludes it.

These are probabilities before choosing a random sample from the stated population. After observing a particular pair, its sample mean is no longer an unknown output of that selection. Saying that the population mean has a certain probability of being near the observed average would reverse the direction of the calculation. Confidence intervals and Bayesian statements will require their own frameworks later.

A useful check is to identify the event twice: once as allowed statistic values and once as the original pairs producing them. If those two lists yield different probabilities, the mapping, boundary, or sample weights need correction.

10Explain the negative dependence without replacement

Every draw position has the population score distribution, with mean 75 and variance 125. But the positions are not independent. If the first selected score is 60, the remaining scores average 80. If the first is 90, the remaining scores average 70. Removing a low score raises the remaining average; removing a high score lowers it.

We can derive the covariance generally by considering a random ordering of all N fixed population members. Every position has variance σ², and exchangeability gives the same covariance c for any two distinct positions. The full sum is the fixed population total, so its variance is zero:

0=Var(X1+⋯+XN)=Nσ²+N(N−1) c.c=Cov(Xi,Xj)=−σ²/(N−1), i≠j.

For N=4, c=−125/3. This calculation uses random ordering of a complete finite population, not an assumption that every dependent process has negative covariance. The sampling design creates this particular relation.

For a sample of n positions, there are n variance terms and n(n−1) ordered covariance contributions. Dividing by n² for the average gives:

Var(X̄)=σ²/n+[(n−1)/n] c.=(σ²/n)(N−n)/(N−1).

At N=4,n=2 this is(125/2)(2/3)=125/3, matching enumeration. Assuming independence would omit the negative covariance and give 125/2 instead, an overestimate for this design. The reduction has a reason: distinct selections cover more of the fixed population rather than allowing the same member to occupy both positions.

11Reconcile the two finite-population correction formulas

The Finite Population Correction, FPC, modifies the variance or standard error when selecting without replacement from a fixed finite population. With σ² defined using divisor N, the variance factor is(N−n)/(N−1). The SE factor is its square root. A variance factor and an SE factor cannot be substituted for one another.

Using SN²=Nσ²/(N−1), the same variance can be written:

Var(X̄)=(σ²/n)(N−n)/(N−1).=(SN²/n)(1−n/N).

The apparent difference between(N−n)/(N−1) and 1−n/N disappears once the variance convention is included. Here(125/2)(2/3) equals[(500/3)/2](1/2)=125/3. Comparing the correction fractions alone while silently changing the variance definition would create a false contradiction.

At n=N, every sample contains the entire fixed population and X̄=μ, so the sampling variance is zero. At n=1 the formula returns σ², the variance of one uniformly selected member. Both endpoints check the design logic. For a very small sampling fraction in a large population, the correction may be close to 1, but the exact expression remains available; no universal population-size rule justifies ignoring it in every problem.

This is a design effect for a finite-population target. A census may still have measurement error, missing records or uncertainty about a different future population. Zero selection variance does not eliminate those other sources. Likewise, no FPC should be inserted merely because a dataset has a finite number of rows; the target population and actual sampling mechanism must warrant it.

12Change the design and reconstruct the sample space

Now replace the selected student before the next draw. The two draw positions are independent and uniform over the four identities. There are 4²=16 ordered sequences, each with probability 1/16. Repeated identities such as A,A become possible. This replacement design is a mathematical comparison, not a recommendation to count a student's repeated score as a second independent real person.

For statistics that ignore order, A,B and B,A give the same output but remain two equally likely sequences. A,A has only one order. Therefore the ten unordered multisets are not equiprobable: the four repeated-identity multisets each have mass 1/16, while the six distinct pairs each have 2/16. Treating all ten equally would change the sampling law incorrectly.

13Construct the sampling distribution of a proportion

Define a success as score≥80 and let H be the number of successful students selected. Without replacement, H can be 0,1,or 2. A,B is the only zero-success pair, C,D the only two-success pair, and the other four pairs have one success. Dividing H by n=2 gives p̂=0,.5,or 1.

P(p̂=0)=1/6; P(p̂=.5)=4/6; P(p̂=1)=1/6.E[p̂]=0(1/6)+.5(4/6)+1(1/6)=.5.Var(p̂)=.25(1/6)+0(4/6)+.25(1/6)=1/12.

The SE is√(1/12)≈.288675, in fraction units. The probability of selecting at least one success is 5/6, while the population success proportion is .5. Those are different events; a proportion's expectation is not the probability that its numerator is positive.

The success count follows the Hypergeometric mechanism: choosing h of the two successes and 2−h of the two failures gives P(H=h)=C(2,h) C(2,2−h)/C(4,2). We have derived its small case by listing samples rather than requiring a formula without explanation.

With replacement the two success indicators are independent Bernoulli(.5), so H is Binomial(2,.5). Its masses are .25,.5,.25, and Var(p̂)=p(1−p)/2=.125, larger than 1/12. The without-replacement formula is[p(1−p)/n](N−n)/(N−1). The same dependence correction applies because a proportion is an average of binary population values.

14Distinguish a sample sum from a population-total estimate

The sample sum T=X1+X2 equals 2 X̄ for our fixed n=2. Scaling a statistic scales its sampling distribution: its possible sums are 130,140,150,160,and 170 with the same probabilities as the corresponding means. The center and variance follow the affine rules:

E[T]=2 E[X̄]=150 points.Var(T)=2² Var(X̄)=500/3 points².

Its Standard Error is√(500/3)≈12.90994 points. That number describes fluctuation of sums between two-student samples. It is not the expected sum, a student SD, or the error of one observed total. Changing n changes the sum's meaning as well as its variability.

The population total is τ=300, so T itself does not estimate that total without an adjustment. Under this uniform design, τ̂=N X̄=4 X̄ estimates the whole-population total. Its expectation is 4(75)=300, variance 16(125/3)=2000/3, and SE≈25.81989 points. For observed pair A,D, both the sample sum 150 and expanded total estimate 300 are valid calculations of different quantities.

This short comparison anticipates estimation without replacing Guide 26's fuller treatment. Its purpose is to identify the target before interpreting a statistic. A report that calls the selected scores' sum “the estimated population total” has silently changed the estimand. To verify the transformation, map each possible mean to 2 times and 4 times that value, preserve its probability, and check the squared multiplier in the variance.

15Give sample variance its own sampling distribution

Sample variance is itself a statistic, so it has a distribution across samples. With two scores, the deviations from their mean are opposite and have magnitude half their difference. Therefore S²=(X1−X2)²/2. Possible differences 10,20,and 30 yield variances 50,200,and 450.

The three adjacent-score pairs A,B; B,C; C,D produce 50, giving probability 3/6=.5. A,C and B,D produce 200, probability 2/6=1/3. A,D produces 450, probability 1/6. Its expectation is:

This equals SN², the finite-population variance with divisor N−1. It does not equal the individual-score distribution variance σ²=125. That distinction is correct for SRS without replacement and the conventions we declared. Copying an iid expectation formula without checking the design would create an apparent inconsistency.

The distribution is not determined by the mean's distribution alone. The two pairs giving mean 75 have variances 50 and 450, so one statistic cannot recover the other. Neither this three-point law nor the finite mean distribution is a continuous Chi-square or Normal distribution. Such theoretical families arise under other assumptions and transformations, developed later. For now, constructing the finite law lets us identify exactly what “the sample variance varies” means, including its support, masses, expectation and squared-point units.

16Explain why the variance denominator depends on the target

Centering observations at their own sample mean removes some variation. The algebraic identity below makes that statement precise without relying on a slogan about degrees of freedom. Write each Xi−μ as(Xi−X̄)+(X̄−μ), expand the square, and sum. The cross term vanishes because Σ(Xi−X̄)=0.

Σ(Xi−X̄)²=Σ(Xi−μ)²−n(X̄−μ)².E[Σ(Xi−X̄)²]=nσ²−nVar(X̄), when E[X̄]=μ.

Under iid sampling with replacement, Var(X̄)=σ²/n. The expected numerator becomes(n−1)σ², so dividing by n−1 gives E[S²]=σ². Dividing the same numerator by n would instead give expectation[(n−1)/n]σ². The denominator corrects a particular expectation; it does not guarantee that every realized variance equals the population value.

Under SRS without replacement, insert the FPC variance. The numerator's expectation becomes(n−1) SN², so E[S²]=SN². For our n=2, calculate 2(125)−2(125/3)=500/3. Under replacement the analogous calculation gives 250−125=125. These match the two exact enumerations.

The condition Σ(Xi−X̄)=0 also explains the phrase “one constraint among the deviations”: once n−1 deviations are known, the last is fixed by their sum. But that description alone does not establish every variance formula under arbitrary dependence. The expectation identity and the sampling design supply the missing argument. At n=1, S² with divisor n−1 is undefined; treating it as zero would silently invent an estimate of spread from one observation.

17Separate SD, SE, estimated SE, and realized error

Several spread measures here use points, but shared units do not make them interchangeable. Population SD√125≈11.18034 describes individual-score variation. Sample SD s describes variation among the selected scores; for A,D it is 21.21320. The true design SE of the two-student mean is 6.45497, obtained from the whole sampling law. The realized mean error for A,D is zero.

If the full population is unknown, a sample may provide an estimate of SE rather than its true value. Under SRS without replacement, a usual variance estimate for the mean replaces SN² in its formula by the sample variance:

Estimated Var(X̄)=(1−n/N) S²/n.

For A,D, substitute n=2,N=4,s²=450: estimated variance=(1/2)(450)/2=112.5, with estimated SE√112.5≈10.60660 points. It differs from the true 6.45497 because one sample's s² differs from the fixed SN². The variance estimate is not the same object as the random mean, and taking its square root does not create an exactly unbiased SE estimate.

Another useful contrast concerns averaging sample SDs. From the three-point variance law, E[S]=(.5)√50+(1/3)√200+(1/6)√450=25√2/3≈11.78511. But√E[S²]=√(500/3)≈12.90994. Averaging and taking square roots do not commute.

Guide 27 will develop Standard Error more fully. The immediate skill is to name which quantity varies across what repetitions. Never label a data SD as the uncertainty of an average merely because both are reported in points.

18Increase sample size within the same finite population

Hold the four scores fixed and change n under SRS without replacement. At n=1 the sample mean is an individual's score, with variance 125. At n=2 its variance is 125/3. At n=3 the four possible samples omit one student each; their means are 70,220/3,230/3,and 80, each with probability 1/4.

For n=3, deviations from 75 are−5,−5/3,+5/3,+5. Their squared average is[25+25/9+25/9+25]/4=125/9. The FPC formula independently gives(125/3)(1/3)=125/9, with SE≈3.72678 points. The center remains 75 while the possible means and probabilities change.

19Compare sample-size effects under replacement

Under independent sampling with replacement, increasing n does not turn n=4 into a census. Four draws may repeat A several times and omit other identities. The mean's variance is 125/n, so it remains 31.25 at n=4 rather than zero. The selection mechanism determines whether population coverage is guaranteed.

For any n in this replacement model, there are 4n equiprobable ordered sequences. The exact distribution can be built by counting their sums and then dividing each sum by n. For n=8 there are 65536 sequences, but a repeated convolution of four possible scores produces the same law without storing every sequence. Each convolution adds one possible draw to the accumulated sum and updates its count.

The displayed laws for n=1,2,4,and 8 all have center 75. Their variances are 125,62.5,31.25,and 15.625. The supported mean grid becomes finer: spacing 10/n points. Connecting the discrete masses or viewing many values together must not be mistaken for an exact continuous Normal distribution.

A symmetric distribution can concentrate as n grows without the LLN alone describing its shape. The CLT will later address a rescaled limit under appropriate assumptions. Here every plotted mass is obtained exactly from the finite replacement model. The sample-size comparison answers two questions separately: how widely means vary and which means are possible. A formula for variance supplies the first, but not by itself the full probability law needed for the second.

Because the mean grid becomes finer, the probability of one exact central value need not increase as SE decreases. A narrower spread can coexist with smaller individual point masses when more nearby values share the probability. Compare the whole distribution or the probability of a fixed interval, rather than treating the tallest stem as a direct measure of precision.

21Simulate complete samples rather than isolated means

Enumeration gives the exact law, but larger sample spaces may make simulation useful. One replication must recreate the sampling procedure, then compute one statistic. For our principal design, use random.sample(population, 2) to select two different identities, calculate their mean, store that one mean, and repeat B times. Here n=2 is fixed within every replication, while B controls how accurately the simulation approximates the sampling law.

The full population is available at the start of each replication. Python's sample operation returns a selection without modifying the input list. There is no need to remove students permanently between replications. Doing so would change the experiment and eventually exhaust the population. Sampling with replacement would instead use separate choices or an explicitly appropriate replacement method.

Our numerical experiment uses Python Random with seed 2501 and B=20000 complete samples. A seed controls reproducibility of the pseudo-random computation, not the truth of the sampling model. Generate the stream once, not anew with the same seed inside every replication. Repeated identical samples would make a stable but incorrect approximation.

The resulting empirical law uses frequency/B for each possible mean. It approximates the exact law; it does not replace the original unknown population with newly observed real students. Our values are constructed and completely known, so this simulation is a numerical learning experiment. Guide 24 supplied its LLN rationale; here the important new issue is identifying which distribution the repeated statistic is approximating.

22Run and check the empirical sampling distribution

The second standalone script generates 20000 size-two samples without replacement. It prints counts and empirical masses for each supported mean, followed by the empirical center, spread and frequency of the event mean≥80. Predict a center near 75, spread near 6.45497 and event fraction near 1/3, with nonzero numerical discrepancies.

23Distinguish sampling uncertainty from Monte Carlo error

The original statistic X̄ varies between hypothetical samples of n students. Its sampling SE belongs to that variation. The simulation adds a second layer: finite B produces an approximate description of the sampling distribution. Increasing B reduces error in that description, while holding n and the original sampling law unchanged.

For example, define Jb=1 if simulated sample b has mean≥80. The exact event probability is q=1/3. Under independent ideal replications, the estimated event mass has MCSE√[q(1−q)/B]. At B=20000 this is 1/300≈.00333333 in probability units. It is not the 6.45497-point SE of the original sample mean.

If we average the B simulated means to estimate the sampling distribution's center, that Monte Carlo average has SD√[(125/3)/B]≈.04564355 points at 20000. This small numerical scale says we can approximate 75 accurately; it does not make an actual two-student mean have SE .04564. The target of each average must be named before using a denominator.

Changing n modifies the sampling distribution. Changing B modifies how well a computational experiment approximates that distribution. A histogram becoming smoother with more replications does not show that the original estimator became more precise. Conversely, increasing n in a well-designed sample can shrink its sampling uncertainty even if no simulation is run. Keep N for population size, n for observations per sample, and B for numerical replications throughout the report.

24Recognize an exact Normal sampling distribution

Consider a different, explicitly stated model: X1,…,X9 are independent Normal scores with mean 75 and variance 144. This is a continuous teaching model, not the four-student finite population. It permits values outside a bounded quiz scale, so suitability for an actual score distribution would require scrutiny. We use it to isolate an exact family property.

Independent Normal sums are Normal, with means and variances added. The sum has mean 9(75)=675 and variance 9(144)=1296. Dividing the sum by 9 gives:

X̄∼Normal(75,144/9)=Normal(75,16).SE(X̄)=4 points; SD(Xi)=12 points.

Normal notation uses variance as its second parameter here. No large-sample approximation is involved: n=9 is enough for exact Normality because the inputs are independent Normal variables. Center and variance alone would not establish this distribution for arbitrary non-Normal inputs.

For a fixed interval 71 to 79, standardize using the mean's SE 4: lower z=(71−75)/4=−1 and upper z=(79−75)/4=1. Therefore P(71≤X̄≤79)=Φ(1)−Φ(−1)≈.68268949. The individual-score probability uses SD 12 and is only≈.26111732. The same interval contains more probability for the less variable average.

These are probabilities for a random statistic in a fixed interval, not confidence statements assigning probability to an unknown mean. The finite-population case remains discrete and requires its own design law. Keeping the two examples separate prevents a theoretical Normal model from silently replacing an enumerated finite distribution.

25Check independence before using the Normal mean formula

Normal marginal distributions do not by themselves imply the independent-sampling formula. Let one variable Z follow Normal(75,144), then set every observation equal to Z. Each marginal observation is Normal with the stated mean and variance, but X̄=Z regardless of n. Its variance is 144, not 144/n.

This is the same distinction between individual laws and joint mechanism encountered in sampling without replacement. The dependence is now positive and extreme rather than negative. The variance-of-a-sum formula captures both situations: covariance terms reduce or increase the spread, depending on how observations move together.

A practical study can also change its sampling law through clustering, unequal selection probabilities, nonresponse or a convenience rule. A familiar formula should be tied to the conditions under which it was derived. Merely obtaining nine rows, observing approximately Normal marginal histograms, or labeling a procedure “random” does not verify independence of the modeled units.

The correction is to specify the actual design and relevant dependence, then derive or estimate uncertainty accordingly. This guide does not provide general complex-survey or correlated-data formulas. It provides the reason those formulas cannot be replaced automatically by σ/√n. Under valid independent Normal sampling, the exact result in the previous section remains correct. Under the copied construction, the average has the same law as one score. Naming both mechanisms makes the difference auditable rather than a mysterious exception.

26Understand what one observed sample does not reveal

Our exact law is available because every population value and every selection probability is known. An actual sample of two scores would generally not provide that information. Observing 60 and 90 does not establish that the other population values are 70 and 80, nor does it establish the mean distribution's five masses.

For example, another four-person population could contain 60,60,90,90. The observed values 60 and 90 remain possible, but the without-replacement mean law is now 60,75,90 with probabilities 1/6,4/6,1/6. Its center is 75 and its variance 75, rather than 125/3. The same observed two-value sample is compatible with different population structures and sampling uncertainties.

To reason beyond the sample, we need design knowledge and a way to address unknown population features. That may involve a specified probability model, variance estimation, theoretical sampling results or later resampling methods. A Bootstrap distribution, for instance, is an approximation built from observed data under its own assumptions, not the exact full-population distribution magically revealed by one sample. Guide 35 will develop that method.

This limitation is not a reason to abandon inference. It explains why assumptions and uncertainty estimates are necessary. In the teaching example we intentionally know the population so that the mechanics are visible. When moving to real data, state which parts are known by design, which are estimated, and which are assumed. Repeated simulated samples from an assumed population add numerical detail conditional on that assumption, not new evidence about unseen students.

27A stable statistic can still target the wrong population

Suppose the selection rule always chooses the two highest-scoring students, C,D. Every repetition gives mean 85. The sampling distribution under that deterministic rule is a point mass at 85, with variance zero. Yet the intended population mean is 75. Absence of variation is not the same as accuracy for the target population.

A related mistake assigns equal weight to all values of a statistic merely because they are distinct. That would put probability 1/5 on each of 65,70,75,80,85 in the original problem, ignoring that 75 has two generating pairs. A plausible center or a visually symmetric graph might survive the error while variance and event probabilities change.

A sampling-law audit therefore begins before plotting: identify the population and target, list the actual random mechanism, attach correct probabilities, and only then aggregate statistic values. Random Sampling concerns selection from a population; Random Assignment concerns intervention allocation. A correct sampling distribution of quiz means does not establish a causal effect of a teaching method.

Guide 26 will formalize bias and precision, but this concrete contrast already shows why one cannot choose a procedure solely because its output barely changes. Our main SRS design has positive SE yet centers on 75; the convenience rule has zero variation and centers on 85. Statements about uncertainty should preserve both the source of variation and the population to which the result is meant to apply.

28Write a complete sampling-distribution explanation

A defensible explanation names the fixed population, sampling design, sample size, statistic, probability law and interpretation. For our principal case: “From the four fixed scores 60,70,80,90, select two distinct students uniformly. The mean takes 65,70,75,80,85 with probabilities 1/6,1/6,1/3,1/6,1/6. Its expectation is 75 and its variance 125/3, so its Standard Error is about 6.45497 points.”

Add the observed sample separately: “For A,D, the observed mean is 75 and sample SD 21.21320. The observed mean equals the population mean in this constructed example, but the procedure's SE is not zero.” This prevents the data summary and the repeated-sample law from being presented as one undifferentiated number.

If simulation is used, report its role and budget: “With n=2 fixed, B=20000 seeded replications approximate the exact law. The event mean≥80 occurred 6712 times, giving .33560 versus 1/3 exact. The probability approximation has MCSE about .00333333; more B improves numerical approximation rather than changing the original mean's SE.”

A practical report must also distinguish known values from assumptions. Our full population is constructed and known; real score data would require provenance, sampling-frame information, missingness checks and uncertainty estimation. Neither an exact combinatorial calculation nor a reproducible simulation certifies those empirical conditions. A complete conclusion retains limits while stating the result clearly, rather than ending with a formula whose random object and target remain unspecified.

29Exercises that rebuild the law and challenge its assumptions

Attempt the exercises before opening the solutions. Begin with the sample space and probabilities, not with a memorized SE formula. For every answer, identify whether the random object is a score, a sample mean, a sum, a proportion, a variance, or an approximation produced by simulation.

The sequence includes complete enumeration, a changed design, a changed sample size, an exact Normal case and a final report audit. Correct units, weights and interpretation are part of the solution. The final exercise retains the same mathematical and explanatory detail as the first rather than becoming a checklist of unexplained corrections.

A. Identify parameters and observed statistics

For the complete population 60,70,80,90 and observed pair 60,90, calculate μ, σ², SN², x̄, s² and s. State which values would change if another pair were selected.

Complete worked solution

The fixed population mean is 300/4=75. Its squared deviations sum to 500, so σ²=500/4=125 and SN²=500/3. The first variance describes a uniform individual's score distribution; the second is the finite-population convention used in the SRS variance formula. Both are fixed parameters of the stated population.

The observed pair gives x̄=(60+90)/2=75, s²=(225+225)/(2−1)=450, and s=√450≈21.21320 points. The mean and SD use points, while the variances use points squared. Another pair can change these sample statistics but does not change any population parameter.

Equality x̄=μ in this particular pair does not eliminate the procedure's sampling variation. Pair 60,70 instead gives 65 and s²=50. The distinction is between a function evaluated on one selected sample and a fixed description of the complete population. Before sampling, X̄ and S² are random; afterward their observed values are numerical summaries of that one selection.

B. Reconstruct the mean law from sample probabilities

List all size-two SRS samples without replacement. Obtain the five-point mean law and P(X̄≥80). Explain why assigning probability 1/5 to each distinct mean is wrong.

Complete worked solution

The six equiprobable unordered pairs are 60/70,60/80,60/90,70/80,70/90,80/90. Their means are 65,70,75,75,80,85. Each pair has probability 1/6, so the mean 75 receives 2/6 and the other means 1/6 each. The masses sum to 1.

The event X̄≥80 corresponds to 70/90 and 80/90, giving 2/6=1/3. It is not the individual-score event X≥80, whose probability is 2/4=.5. Identifying the original pairs checks the statistic-level event and its endpoints.

Assigning 1/5 to each distinct mean would ignore that two pair outcomes map to 75. In that incorrect law, P(X̄≥80) would become 2/5 rather than 1/3. Symmetry would still keep its center 75, so checking only the expectation would not catch the error. Correct probability weights are needed for the whole law, not just for a plausible average.

C. Verify the mean variance by two independent routes

Compute E[X̄], Var(X̄) and SE under the principal design. Verify variance by the raw second moment and by FPC. Compare realized error for pair 60,90 with SE.

Complete worked solution

Using the six pair means, E[X̄]=450/6=75. The centered squared deviations sum to 250, giving Var(X̄)=250/6=125/3. Independently, E[X̄²]=34000/6=17000/3; subtract 75² to obtain the same variance. These are expectations over all possible samples, not variance estimates from six observed studies.

The design formula gives(σ²/n)(N−n)/(N−1)=(125/2)(2/3)=125/3. Equivalently,(SN²/n)(1−n/N)=[(500/3)/2](1/2). Therefore SE=√(125/3)≈6.45497 points. The variance is in points squared; the square root restores point units.

For pair 60,90, x̄=75 and realized error x̄−μ=0. This does not make SE zero: SE characterizes the random procedure before observing a pair. Another allowed pair can have error±10. An error larger than one SE is not impossible, because a standard deviation is a spread measure rather than a deterministic bound.

D. Construct a proportion law under both designs

Define success as score≥80. Derive the without-replacement law of p̂, its mean and variance. Compare with two independent replacement draws and calculate P(p̂>0).

Complete worked solution

Without replacement, pairs A,B and C,D yield proportions 0 and 1 respectively; four mixed pairs yield .5. Thus probabilities are 1/6,4/6,1/6. Their expectation is .5 and their centered second moment is(.5²)(1/6)+(.5²)(1/6)=1/12. The corresponding SE is√(1/12)≈.288675.

With replacement, each success indicator is independent Bernoulli(.5). Two draws give proportions 0,.5,1 with probabilities .25,.5,.25 and variance .125. The without-replacement variance is smaller by factor(N−n)/(N−1)=2/3, matching(.125)(2/3)=1/12.

For at least one success, P(p̂>0)=5/6 without replacement and .75 with replacement. Neither equals the population proportion .5: the event concerns the sample containing any qualifying student. The threshold and the count-to-proportion transformation should be stated before applying a Hypergeometric or Binomial name. Different designs produce different joint indicator laws even when each draw's marginal success probability remains .5.

E. Verify the sample-variance law and its target

Find the sampling law of S² for n=2 without replacement and its expectation. Compare with replacement. What is the expectation if the centered sum is divided by n rather than n−1?

Complete worked solution

For two values, S²=(X1−X2)²/2. The six distinct pairs yield 50 three times, 200 twice and 450 once. Its law therefore has masses 1/2,1/3,1/6 and E[S²]=25+200/3+75=500/3. This equals SN², not σ²=125, under the stated finite-population conventions.

With replacement, the 16 ordered sequences give variances 0,50,200,450 with counts 4,6,4,2. Their weighted mean is(0+300+800+900)/16=125, agreeing with the iid property E[S²]=σ². The target changes with the sampling design, rather than with arithmetic preference.

Using divisor n=2 halves S² in this example. Its expectation becomes 250/3 without replacement and 62.5 with replacement. Neither operation should be justified by simply calling a denominator “population” or “sample” without identifying what is being estimated. The expectation identity in the guide explains the correction; the exact finite distributions verify it independently. A single observed s² can still be far from its target.

F. Compare sample sums and expanded population totals

For n=2 without replacement, derive the laws, expectations and variances of T=2 X̄ and τ̂=4 X̄. Interpret both for observed pair 60,90.

Complete worked solution

Multiplying each mean by 2 gives sample-sum values 130,140,150,160,170 with masses 1/6,1/6,1/3,1/6,1/6. Thus E[T]=2(75)=150 and Var(T)=4(125/3)=500/3. The sum counts selected scores, so it should not be compared directly with the full total 300 as though they were the same target.

Multiplying by 4 instead gives expanded-total values 260,280,300,320,340 with the same masses. Its expectation is 300 and variance 16(125/3)=2000/3. The scaling is squared for variance, not merely multiplied by 4. SE values are respectively√(500/3)≈12.90994 and√(2000/3)≈25.81989 points.

For pair 60,90, the observed sum is 150 and the expanded estimate is 300. Their numerical relationship comes from known N/n=2. Uniform selection justifies expansion here; unequal-probability designs can require other weights. A correct interpretation states whether a statistic summarizes selected units or estimates a population quantity before reporting its variability.

G. Change sample size and identify the census boundary

Enumerate n=3 means without replacement and check their variance. What happens at n=4? Compare n=4 with replacement and explain why increasing B is a different change.

Complete worked solution

The four size-three subsets have means 70,220/3,230/3,80, each with probability 1/4. Their mean is 75. Centered deviations−5,−5/3,5/3,5 give variance 125/9, so SE≈3.72678. The FPC formula gives(125/3)(4−3)/(4−1)=125/9 as an independent check.

At n=4 without replacement, the only sample is the whole population and the mean equals 75 with probability 1. Variance and SE are zero, while individual-score spread remains positive. With replacement, four draws may repeat identities; variance is 125/4=31.25 rather than zero. Four stored values do not establish a census unless the design guarantees coverage.

Increasing B holds n and its sampling law fixed while improving the numerical approximation to that law. It does not reproduce the same change as increasing students per sample. A report should say whether precision improved through a different data-collection design or merely through more computation on a fixed assumed model.

H. Calculate a probability under an exact Normal mean law

Nine independent observations follow Normal(75,144), with variance as the second argument. Find the exact law of their mean and P(71≤X̄≤79). What if all nine are copies of one observation?

Complete worked solution

The independent sum is Normal with mean 675 and variance 1296. Dividing it by 9 gives mean 75 and variance 1296/81=16. Thus X̄ is exactly Normal(75,16), with SE 4, for this n=9; no CLT approximation is needed.

Standardize the event endpoints with 4: lower−1 and upper 1. The probability is Φ(1)−Φ(−1)≈.68268949. The individual-score calculation uses SD 12 and gives Φ(1/3)−Φ(−1/3)≈.26111732. Using 12 for the independent mean would confuse the population distribution with the sampling distribution.

If every observation is the same Z, then X̄=Z and its variance stays 144. In that copied model the interval probability is .26111732. Each marginal can still be Normal; independence is the condition that justified summing variances without covariances. These are fixed-interval probabilities of random quantities, not confidence statements about an unknown μ.

I. Separate simulation accuracy from sampling spread

The prescribed B=20000 simulation gives 6712 means at least 80. Compare the event estimate with its exact probability and MCSE. Explain why neither MCSE nor simulated empirical SD replaces the true sampling SE.

Complete worked solution

The simulated event fraction is 6712/20000=.33560. The exact probability is 1/3, so signed Monte Carlo discrepancy is .00226667. Under independent ideal replications, MCSE=√[(1/3)(2/3)/20000]=1/300≈.00333333. The realized discrepancy is about .68 of that theoretical scale; it is not the same quantity.

The sampling SE of a two-student mean remains√(125/3)≈6.45497 points. The simulation's empirical SD≈6.45648 points approximates it from finite B, while event-mass MCSE has probability units. If we instead average simulated means to approximate their center, the Monte Carlo SD is√[(125/3)/20000]≈.04564355 points.

Increasing B improves the approximation of masses, center and spread without changing the n=2 selection law. The 20000 pairs are computations from the four constructed scores, not 40000 newly observed independent students. A close empirical SD supports numerical verification; it does not turn an assumed population into empirical knowledge or make the original procedure's uncertainty disappear.

J. Replace a misleading sampling-distribution report

A report says: “The histogram of our pair 60,90 is the sampling distribution. Every distinct mean is equally likely. SE is 21.2132, and observing mean 75 eliminates uncertainty. Without replacement still uses 125/n. A million simulated pairs makes the actual mean more precise and proves the population is Normal.” Diagnose and replace it.

Complete worked solution

The pair's histogram is its empirical data distribution, not the statistic's law over samples. The five mean values are not equiprobable because 75 has two generating pairs. The number 21.2132 is the observed sample SD; the true design SE is√(125/3)≈6.45497. Observing a zero realized mean error in this constructed population does not remove the pre-sampling variation of the procedure.

The correct without-replacement variance includes FPC: (125/2)(4−2)/(4−1)=125/3. A million replications would improve the numerical approximation to that law while leaving n=2 and its SE unchanged. No simulation can turn the fixed four-score population into a continuous Normal population or validate a model for a real school.

A replacement report is: “For the fixed constructed population 60,70,80,90, select two distinct identities uniformly. Six pairs each have probability 1/6. The mean law is 65,70,75,80,85 with masses 1/6,1/6,1/3,1/6,1/6; E[X̄]=75 and SE≈6.45497 points. The observed pair 60,90 gives mean 75 and sample variance 450. Simulation approximates the stated law and has separate Monte Carlo error.” Add that applying this reasoning to real data requires the actual sampling design and unknown population quantities to be addressed. The corrected explanation preserves target, design, units, law and limits through its final sentence.

30Connect the exact construction to later inference

A sampling distribution belongs to a statistic under a design or model. The construction begins with possible samples and their probabilities, maps each sample through a specified calculation, and combines probabilities for repeated outputs. Expectation, variance and event probabilities are then properties of that resulting law. A population histogram and one observed sample cannot be substituted for this construction.

Our four-student example made the logic inspectable. The same fixed scores produced different laws when replacement, n or the statistic changed. Negative dependence explained finite-population correction, and the sample-variance example showed why a denominator and its target must be stated together. Simulation introduced a separate approximation layer whose accuracy depends on B, while sampling uncertainty belongs to the original n-sized design.

The next guides build on this distinction. Point estimation asks which statistic should estimate a target and how its errors behave. Standard Error examines the spread of estimators and how to estimate it. The CLT addresses the shape of standardized fluctuations under appropriate conditions. None of these questions can be answered just by saying that a sample is large or a histogram appears smooth.

For transfer, choose a statistic and write a small complete sampling design that would let you verify its law. Explain what changes if observations are dependent or selection is unequal. Then identify what would be unknown in an actual application. Being able to separate a numerical result from the mechanism and assumptions supporting it is the main value of this guide, and the foundation for responsible inference from one observed sample.
