1. The question after fitting a regression

A fitted regression line or plane is a mathematical summary, not a certificate that its assumptions are satisfied. Imagine an economist studying how monthly household expenditure varies with income and household size. An ordinary least squares (OLS) fit can produce an impressive coefficient of determination while one unusual household substantially changes a coefficient, a patterned residual reveals a missing effect, or highly redundant predictors make individual coefficients unstable. These are different scientific problems. To decide what to trust, we need regression diagnostics: deliberate checks of the recorded observations, the fitted model and the sensitivity of its conclusions.

The fundamental question is not, “Which rows can I remove until the scatterplot looks attractive?” It is, “What does this feature of the data reveal about measurement, model design, uncertainty and the population to which I wish to generalize?” We shall fit a fully visible synthetic household dataset, calculate an extreme residual, leverage and Cook's distance, refit after a justified hypothetical exclusion, and inspect a separate example of multicollinearity. Throughout, we will distinguish diagnosis from automatic deletion and association from causation.

2. Prerequisites and a map of the learning journey

This guide builds on multiple linear regression (Guide 52) and nonlinear terms and interactions (Guide 53). We assume comfort with averages, squared deviations, least-squares coefficients, predictions, residuals and the elementary idea of a matrix. To remain self-contained, the next sections reintroduce each symbol before using it. A student who has seen derivatives but not projection matrices can follow the calculations by interpreting the matrix formulas as efficient ways to combine several predictors; a brief derivation explains what additional linear algebra means.

Our sequence follows a real analyst's workflow: define an observation and units; audit the data; fit the stated mean model; examine residual magnitude and pattern; inspect the unusual positions in predictor space; measure how much conclusions depend on individual rows; and finally investigate whether predictors can be distinguished from one another. The final exercises require a decision and a limitation, not merely a number. This ordering matters: a large residual is not the same as high leverage, a large leverage is not necessarily influential, and multicollinearity can occur even when every residual looks innocuous.

3. Variables, population, units and the synthetic case

The outcome \(Y\) is monthly nonhousing household expenditure measured in hundreds of dollars. Predictor \(X\) is monthly household income in hundreds of dollars, and \(Z\) is household size, measured in people. These units are deliberately aligned: an income increase of one unit in \(X\) means $100 more income in one month, not one dollar or one percentage point. We use twelve artificial households; the values are synthetic teaching data, not observations collected from families, a government survey or an experiment. Nothing in the table licenses a statement about actual household behavior.

Our starting mean equation is \(E[Y\mid X,Z]=\beta_0+\beta_1 X+\beta_2 Z\). The intercept predicts spending at zero income and zero household members and is outside the realistic domain; it is useful algebraically but should not be narrated as an economic reality. The income slope is a conditional association: the predicted difference in expenditure for one additional $100 of monthly income while household size is held fixed. Household size is not manipulated or randomly assigned, so even a precise coefficient would not establish a causal income effect. All twelve units are retained in the first fit, including the unusual one, to prevent discretionary exclusion.

5. A reproducible least-squares fit with all twelve households

OLS chooses coefficients minimizing \(\mathrm{SSE}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-\widehat Y_i)^2\). Each predicted value follows \(\widehat Y_i=b_0+b_1X_i+b_2Z_i\). The full-data fit, independently checked by numerical linear algebra, gives \(b_0=2.55199\), \(b_1=0.62356\), and \(b_2=1.20161\) after rounding. Thus, at a fixed number of people, the model associates an extra \(100 in income with an extra \)62.36 in monthly nonhousing spending. At fixed income, one additional household member is associated with $120.16 more spending. Both interpretations are model-conditional and descriptive.

Key equation
\[\widehat Y=2.55199+0.62356X+1.20161Z\]

The fit has \(\mathrm{SSE}=96.426\) in squared units of hundreds of dollars, residual degrees of freedom \(9\), and residual mean square \(s^2=96.426/9=10.714\). Its residual scale is \(s=\sqrt{10.714}\approx3.273\) hundreds of dollars. Do not interpret this as a standard deviation of household income; it is a scale for unexplained spending under the fitted model. The coefficients look tidy, but the fit alone does not tell us whether one row dominates the income slope. That requires diagnostics.

6. How OLS combines observations: the design matrix

Write the \(12\times3\) design matrix \(\mathbf X\) with first column ones, second column recorded income, and third column recorded household size. The response vector is \(\mathbf y=(23,28,25,36,34,40,38,43,49,46,54,92)^\mathsf T\). The OLS objective becomes \((\mathbf y-\mathbf X\mathbf b)^\mathsf T(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\mathbf b)\). Differentiating with respect to the three coefficients and setting the gradient to zero produces the normal equations \(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X\mathbf b=\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf y\).

Key equation
\[\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf y\]

This inverse formula assumes the three columns are linearly independent. In actual computation, QR or singular-value decomposition is usually more numerically stable than explicitly computing the inverse. The formula is pedagogically important because it shows that changing the predictors changes the geometry of the fit even if every \(Y\) value remains the same. The resulting hat matrix \(\mathbf H=\mathbf X(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\mathsf T\) maps observed outcomes into fitted outcomes. Its diagonal will measure leverage; it does not use observed spending directly.

7. Reproduce a prediction and residual by hand

Consider household 12 with \(X=125\), \(Z=6\), and observed spending \(Y=92\). Substitute its predictor values into the full fitted equation: \(\widehat Y_{12}=2.55199+0.62356(125)+1.20161(6)\approx87.706\). Its raw residual is \(e_{12}=92-87.706\approx+4.294\) hundreds of dollars. A positive residual means this household spent more than the fitted conditional mean, not that income caused the extra spending. It is tempting to label a deviation of about $429 “large,” but large relative to which scale and which observation geometry?

Key equation
\[e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i\]

For comparison, household 8 has \(X=60\), \(Z=3\), observed \(Y=43\), fitted value about \(43.570\), and a raw residual near \(-0.570\); these approximations should be checked against the final full-precision calculation rather than combined carelessly. A raw residual is measured in outcome units and is immediately interpretable, but raw residual variances differ across rows because each observation influences its own fit to a different degree. That motivates standardizing by residual scale and leverage rather than treating a common absolute residual cutoff as universal.

8. Residual-versus-fitted plot: what question does it answer?

A residual-versus-fitted graph places each \(\widehat Y_i\) on the horizontal axis and \(e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i\) on the vertical axis, with a horizontal reference at zero. If the conditional mean is adequately specified, the points should not trace a strong systematic U-shape, stair-step, or trend. A curved cloud suggests the model may be missing a nonlinear term; a widening funnel suggests the error variance may increase with fitted spending. Neither pattern by itself proves a specific cause. Differences in groups, repeated observations and data errors can produce misleading patterns.

A useful reasoning exercise is to ask what would change if we replaced \(X\) by its logarithm or added \(X^2\). The shape of the conditional mean changes, but simply adding terms after examining the same data also increases model-selection uncertainty. Diagnose before deciding; compare the amended equation with domain knowledge and ideally validate it on fresh data. A residual plot is exploratory evidence about model adequacy, not a significance test. In a small sample of twelve, absence of an obvious pattern is especially weak evidence of absence of misspecification.

9. Residual-versus-predictor plots and omitted structure

Looking only at residuals against fitted values can conceal important relationships. Plot residuals separately against income, household size, time of observation, and relevant categories. With a correctly fitted OLS model containing an intercept, residuals are algebraically orthogonal to the included predictor columns, so the linear sample correlation between residuals and any included predictor is zero. That mathematical identity does not establish a correct conditional mean. Residuals can still trace a curve against income or differ systematically by a missing group.

A subtle example is a quadratic omission: residuals may be positive at low and high income but negative in the middle; their positive and negative linear cross-products can cancel. Inspecting a curve rather than only a correlation coefficient identifies the problem. Similarly, an omitted interaction can yield a different pattern within small versus large households even if a combined residual graph looks balanced. When observations are ordered over time, positive runs of residuals suggest possible dependence. A diagnostic workflow therefore examines each scientifically meaningful predictor and the design structure, not merely the one plot most convenient to generate.

10. Normal Q–Q plots and the normality question

A normal quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plot sorts standardized residuals and compares them with theoretical quantiles of a normal distribution. If the distribution is roughly compatible with the reference after allowing for estimation and sample size, plotted points often fall near a straight line. Strong tail departures, isolated large observations or pronounced asymmetry may warrant investigation. A Q–Q plot is not a plot of residuals against income; it addresses distributional shape, not mean-function curvature or changing error variance.

For classical exact small-sample \(t\) and \(F\) inference, the assumption concerns the conditional errors given the design, commonly modeled as independent normal random variables with common variance. Residuals are estimated, constrained by the fitted regression and correlated with one another; they are not identical to unobserved errors. In larger samples, some inferential procedures may be approximately robust to nonnormal errors under additional conditions. A neat Q–Q line alone cannot demonstrate independence, random sampling, causality or homoscedasticity. With \(n=12\), visual judgments are especially uncertain; state that uncertainty instead of declaring an assumption verified.

11. Heteroscedasticity and dependence are separate threats

Homoscedasticity means \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i\mid\mathbf X)=\sigma^2\) does not change across predictor combinations. Household expenditure variability could increase with income even when average spending follows a linear relationship. A funnel-shaped residual pattern would then suggest heteroscedasticity. Under conditional mean exogeneity, OLS slopes may remain unbiased in the usual fixed-design model, but conventional homoscedastic standard errors and their associated small-sample tests can be inappropriate. Robust standard errors are developed in the next guide, not a magic correction for an incorrectly specified mean.

Independence is another requirement: two households within the same extended family or sampled from the same building might share unobserved circumstances. A sequence of repeated monthly records for one household would require methods that account for within-household dependence. Neither a robust standard error nor deleting the largest residual automatically solves confounding or dependence. We must first identify the sampling unit, clustering, timeline and measurement mechanism. Diagnostics are coherent only when connected back to how observations were collected.

12. What exactly is leverage?

Leverage measures how unusual a row is in the predictor space, not in the outcome variable. For a design vector \(\mathbf x_i=(1,X_i,Z_i)^\mathsf T\), its leverage is the \(i\)th diagonal element of the hat matrix: \(h_{ii}=\mathbf x_i^\mathsf T(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf x_i\). Under full column rank, leverages lie between zero and one, and their sum equals the number of fitted coefficients, \(\sum_i h_{ii}=p=3\). Therefore their average is \(p/n=3/12=0.25\) in the household example.

Key equation
\[h_{ii}=\mathbf x_i^\mathsf T(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf x_i,\qquad\sum_i h_{ii}=p\]

Household 12 has \(h_{12,12}\approx0.735\): substantially above the sample average because income \(125\) is distant from the others. This is a property of its \(X\) and \(Z\) values and of the entire design; a household with the same predictors but a different spending value would have the same leverage. A leverage guideline such as \(2p/n=0.50\) or \(3p/n=0.75\) can direct attention, but these are context-dependent screening rules, never universal definitions of an error. High leverage means potential to affect the fitted surface, not necessarily actual influence.

13. Why leverage changes the variance of a residual

Let the model be \(\mathbf y=\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) with independent homoscedastic errors having variance \(\sigma^2\). The residual vector is \(\mathbf e=(\mathbf I-\mathbf H)\mathbf y=(\mathbf I-\mathbf H)\boldsymbol\varepsilon\). Because \(\mathbf H\) is symmetric and idempotent, \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf e\mid\mathbf X)=\sigma^2(\mathbf I-\mathbf H)\). Consequently \(\operatorname{Var}(e_i\mid\mathbf X)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})\).

Key equation
\[\operatorname{SD}(e_i\mid\mathbf X)=\sigma\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}\]

Notice the counterintuitive result: high-leverage observations can have smaller raw residual variance because the fitted plane is pulled toward them. For household 12, \(1-h_{12,12}\approx0.265\), so the residual's standard deviation under the model is only about \(0.515\sigma\). A modest-looking raw residual near a high-leverage row can therefore be unusually large after accounting for its limited residual variance. The formula explains why simply ranking absolute raw residuals is not enough to identify unusual observations. It also depends on the stated error assumptions; the same algebra is not a guarantee under heteroscedasticity or dependence.

14. Internally standardized and externally studentized residuals

An internally studentized residual is \(r_i=e_i/[s\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}]\), where \(s^2=\mathrm{SSE}/(n-p)\) uses all observations. For household 12, substitute \(e_{12}\approx4.294\), \(s\approx3.273\) and \(h\approx0.735\): \(r_{12}\approx4.294/[3.273\sqrt{0.265}]\approx2.548\). This changes the diagnostic question from an outcome difference in hundreds of dollars to a dimensionless departure relative to the row's expected residual scale.

Key equation
\[r_i=\frac{e_i}{s\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}}\]

An externally studentized residual recomputes the variance estimate after removing observation \(i\), usually denoted \(s_{(i)}\), while keeping the geometric correction. Its formula is \(t_i=e_i/[s_{(i)}\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}]\). Under classical normal-error assumptions, the externally studentized statistic can be compared with a \(t\) reference distribution with \(n-p-1\) degrees of freedom for individual diagnostics. Multiple screening, model choice and small samples complicate naive \(p\)-value interpretations. Neither \(|r_i|>2\) nor \(|t_i|>3\) is a legal instruction to delete a row; they are prompts to check provenance, measurement and model fit.

15. Three distinct labels: outlier, leverage and influence

An outlier in the response direction is a row with an unusual outcome relative to the model, often visible through a large studentized residual. A high-leverage point has an unusual predictor combination and can potentially determine where the fitted surface passes. An influential point materially changes one or more fitted results when altered or removed. These labels refer to different properties. A household with ordinary income but extraordinary spending may have a large residual yet relatively little power to change the income slope. A household with extremely high income that sits exactly on the fitted line may have high leverage and near-zero residual, producing little observed influence.

In influence diagnostics, both predictor geometry and discrepancy matter. The twelfth household combines unusual predictor position with substantial departure relative to its residual scale, so it is a strong influence candidate. Yet calling a household “influential” does not mean it is fabricated or should disappear from the population. If the research question includes high-income households, excluding it solely because it changes the coefficient could remove important real variation. The next computations quantify sensitivity first; substantive rules about inclusion must be made separately and disclosed.

16. Cook’s distance: a combined sensitivity measure

Cook's distance \(D_i\) measures how much the fitted coefficient vector changes when observation \(i\) is omitted, normalized using the estimated residual variance and the design. In the standard OLS setting with \(p\) parameters it can be calculated without refitting every row: \(D_i=[e_i^2/(p s^2)] [h_{ii}/(1-h_{ii})^2]\). Using the internally studentized residual gives the equivalent expression \(D_i=(r_i^2/p)[h_{ii}/(1-h_{ii})]\).

Key equation
\[D_i=\frac{r_i^2}{p}\frac{h_{ii}}{1-h_{ii}}\]

For household 12 the numbers are \(r_{12}\approx2.548\), \(p=3\), \(h\approx0.735\): \(D_{12}\approx(2.548^2/3)(0.735/0.265)\approx6.00\). It is much larger than a convenient screening line such as \(4/n=0.333\). That suggests examining this row's influence, but \(4/n\) is a heuristic rather than a formal fixed-significance test. The formula explicitly shows why a row can be influential: a large scaled residual multiplies a high-leverage factor. You should describe both contributions rather than report Cook's distance as a mysterious single score.

19. The leave-one-out identity and its meaning

Case deletion need not be an opaque software procedure. For a single observation in ordinary least squares, if \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is the full-fit coefficient vector, then the deleted-case estimate obeys \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{(i)}=\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}-(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf x_i e_i/(1-h_{ii})\), provided the remaining design retains full column rank. Thus the change is proportional to the residual, amplified by a small denominator when leverage is close to one, and shaped by the predictors through the inverse cross-product matrix.

Key equation
\[\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{(i)}=\frac{(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf x_i e_i}{1-h_{ii}}\]

This algebra connects the seemingly separate ideas of residual, leverage, and coefficient sensitivity. It also shows why deleting one row can move several coefficients simultaneously when predictors are correlated. The identity is not a license to extrapolate a removed-row model to the point that was deleted: its predictors may lie outside the retained range. Report the models, sample sizes and ranges side by side. If a diagnostic summary changes dramatically on deleting several observations, note that choosing the set of deletions itself is an additional modeling decision requiring scrutiny.

20. DFBETAS, DFFITS and why the target conclusion matters

Cook's distance summarizes a multidimensional change, but an analyst may care specifically about the income coefficient or one predicted household profile. DFBETAS standardizes how a particular coefficient changes upon case deletion; DFFITS standardizes how a case's own fitted value changes. These diagnostics answer distinct questions. A large DFBETAS for the income slope is important when interpreting income conditional on household size; a large DFFITS might matter when predicting spending for a high-income family.

When interpreting any flag, ask which conclusion moved: sign of the slope, confidence interval, magnitude in dollars, substantive policy recommendation, or prediction at a target income. A coefficient can be statistically significant in both fits yet economically very different. Conversely a small coefficient difference can cross a conventional significance threshold in a small sample. Thus a robust sensitivity paragraph should report numeric estimates, uncertainty, a scientifically meaningful comparison scale, and the reason for considering alternative models. No single diagnostic value answers the substantive question independently of the research objective.

21. What multicollinearity actually means

Multicollinearity occurs when one explanatory variable is well approximated by a combination of other included explanatory variables. Exact linear dependence makes the design matrix rank-deficient; near dependence leaves estimation possible but can make individual coefficient estimates imprecise and highly sensitive to modest changes in the data. Think of recording income in dollars and income in hundreds of dollars as two independent predictors in the same regression: one column is exactly 100 times the other. No data can separately identify their two effects while both are included.

A less extreme example arises when income and disposable income are highly correlated, or when age and years of work experience move together. Distinguish three questions: Can the model estimate individual conditional slopes reliably? Can the combination predict expenditures at profiles represented in the data? Is the model's relationship causal? Multicollinearity primarily challenges the first, not automatically the second, and cannot answer the third. Adding more observations with similar predictor ratios may help little; observing new combinations that separate predictors can be more informative.

22. VIF from an auxiliary regression

For a predictor \(X_j\), regress it on all the other predictors (including an intercept when appropriate) and record that auxiliary regression's \(R_j^2\). The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) is \(\operatorname{VIF}_j=1/(1-R_j^2)\). It compares the model-based coefficient variance with what it would be, holding the relevant residual variance and predictor variation fixed, if that predictor were orthogonal to the others. It does not measure how much a response variable's prediction variance increases in every circumstance.

Key equation
\[\operatorname{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}\]

For our two-predictor example, the sample correlation between income and household size is approximately \(0.77768\). With only one other nonconstant predictor, auxiliary \(R^2=r^2\approx0.60478\), so \(\operatorname{VIF}=1/(1-0.60478)\approx2.53024\) for each slope. Thus the fitted conditional slopes have standard errors multiplied by \(\sqrt{2.53024}\approx1.59\) relative to an otherwise comparable orthogonal design. Whether this is concerning depends on the research question, desired precision, design and substantive overlap. Calling every VIF above five 'invalid' is not justified; thresholds are contextual prompts rather than automatic rejection rules.

23. Derive the variance inflation interpretation

Under fixed-design independent homoscedastic errors with variance \(\sigma^2\), the slope variance in a multiple regression can be written \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta_j\mid\mathbf X)=\sigma^2/[S_{jj}(1-R_j^2)]\), where \(S_{jj}=\sum_i(X_{ij}-\bar X_j)^2\). The numerator is the irreducible error scale under the model, while \(S_{jj}\) measures spread in the predictor. The factor \(1-R_j^2\) is the share of that predictor's variation not explained by the other columns.

Key equation
\[\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta_j\mid\mathbf X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{jj}}\operatorname{VIF}_j\]

If an auxiliary model predicts 96% of a predictor's variation, \(R_j^2=0.96\) and VIF is \(25\). The corresponding standard-error multiplier is \(\sqrt{25}=5\), not \(25\). It would be incorrect to say that five times the standard error means five times the causal uncertainty; the expression is a conditional sampling-variance calculation under specific assumptions. Also, changing the response variable does not directly change VIF when the predictor matrix stays fixed; VIF is a design diagnostic. These distinctions help prevent using VIF as a generic score of model quality.

24. A deliberately collinear miniature design

Suppose four synthetic observations have \(X_1=(1,2,3,4)\) and \(X_2=(2,4,6,8)\). Regressing \(X_2\) on \(X_1\) gives an exact fit, so \(R_2^2=1\) and its VIF is infinite. In a model with an intercept and both predictors, the columns are linearly dependent because \(X_2=2X_1\); the normal-equation matrix cannot be inverted. Many software packages respond by dropping a column or using a generalized inverse. The existence of printed coefficients does not mean that both separate effects were identified.

If we modify the last \(X_2\) from eight to \(8.1\), exact dependence disappears, but the four points remain nearly collinear. Coefficients may then be numerically estimable yet unstable. A model can predict well along the observed relation \(X_2\approx2X_1\) while predicting poorly at an unobserved combination such as \(X_1=4\), \(X_2=2\). Ask whether such a combination is plausible before interpreting conditional slopes. The issue is not simply lack of more data; it is lack of independent predictor variation in the relevant population.

25. Multicollinearity versus high leverage

Leverage and multicollinearity both involve the design matrix, but they refer to different geometry. High leverage concerns one observation whose predictor vector is unusual relative to the sample cloud. Multicollinearity concerns the relationship among entire predictor columns. A dataset can have highly correlated predictors without a single standout row, or one extreme income household with otherwise weakly correlated predictors. Diagnosing one does not substitute for examining the other.

They can also interact. If predictors are nearly redundant, changing a few observations can alter the small independent variation that identifies separate coefficients. Case deletion may then strongly change slopes even when the response residual at the deleted observation is modest. Conversely a high-leverage row might provide valuable new predictor variation and reduce multicollinearity, making simple deletion counterproductive. Examine the condition number, auxiliary regressions and domain of supported predictor combinations in addition to VIF. The purpose is to understand identification and uncertainty, not to maximize or minimize a statistic without a scientific rationale.

26. Condition number, scaling and singularity

A matrix condition number describes sensitivity of linear-system solutions to changes in inputs. For regression it is useful to examine a centered and scaled predictor matrix, because unscaled variables such as dollars and persons can generate a large condition number purely through units. A large condition number after appropriate scaling may indicate near-dependent columns, while an exactly singular design has no unique ordinary least-squares coefficient vector. Neither statement means observed spending predictions are automatically worthless along the well-represented design subspace.

Centering income subtracts its mean and changes the intercept's interpretation; multiplying income by 100 changes the numerical slope inversely. Neither operation, by itself, creates genuinely new independent information. Standardizing continuous columns can improve numerical conditioning and comparisons, but it cannot fix exact linear dependence. Removing a redundant variable, combining correlated measures based on subject-matter meaning, collecting varied samples, or using a carefully justified regularization method are different scientific choices. Ridge regression changes the estimation target by adding a penalty; it should not be presented as repairing causal identification.

27. Why high $R^2$ does not clear the diagnostics

The twelve-household full fit has a high \(R^2\) because spending generally increases with income and household size. But a high \(R^2\) does not ensure a correct functional form, good out-of-sample performance, causal validity, trustworthy homoscedastic standard errors or coefficient stability. A high-leverage observation can make \(R^2\) especially flattering by stretching the range of observed spending. Even when \(R^2\) remains high after deleting the twelfth row, the income and household-size coefficient estimates differ materially.

Predictive performance and explanatory inference ask different questions. For prediction in a similar high-income population, discarding upper-income examples might make coverage worse. For a scientifically interpretable marginal income coefficient, a collinear design can be weak even with small in-sample prediction errors. For public policy, unmeasured differences in location or cost of living can confound the income–spending association. A complete report should specify the primary goal before choosing which diagnostics are most consequential. Reporting \(R^2\) alone is inadequate evidence for any of these goals.

28. A defensible decision tree for an unusual row

Begin with the raw record: is the household identifier duplicated, is the income entry in the wrong currency or month, was spending recorded with a misplaced decimal, and does the survey definition match the other rows? If a confirmed error exists, correct it using the source record and preserve an audit log. If there is no identifiable error, ask whether the point belongs to the target population. A valid high-income household may deserve retention even if it changes the fitted coefficient.

Next, compare specified models with and without the row while labeling the latter as sensitivity analysis. Examine predictions within a common income range; avoid interpreting the excluded-row model at extreme income as if it had direct support. Consider nonlinear mean structure, heteroscedasticity, sampling weights, robust methods and larger samples where scientifically appropriate. Document uncertainty and avoid post hoc deletion rules chosen to obtain significance. Finally, communicate any substantive changes in coefficients or recommendations. The outcome of an investigation may be “retain and qualify,” “correct a documented error,” or “report sensitivity”; “delete because Cook's distance exceeds a threshold” is not a valid standalone conclusion.

29. Three diagnostic visuals that answer different questions

A useful report can include a residual-versus-fitted scatterplot, a Q–Q plot and a leverage–residual (or influence) view. The first asks whether the conditional mean and variance structure look plausible. The second asks whether distributional shape is compatible with assumptions relevant to the chosen inference. The third asks which predictor profiles may exert disproportionate influence. Plot each with clear axes, units, and point identities where needed. A Cook's-distance sequence by row can supplement the leverage view, but it is not a substitute for examining the raw data.

A visual should not be decorative. Before plotting, state the diagnostic question; after plotting, articulate what the viewer can and cannot conclude. With only twelve observations, a smoothly drawn trend line can create a false impression of precision. Annotate the influential twelfth household and keep other values visible, rather than zooming until it dominates every panel. If a point is not shown, explain exactly why. A combined panel should tell a coherent analytical story: model adequacy, unusual geometry, observed coefficient sensitivity, and uncertainty about generalization.

30. What a formal test cannot replace

Formal tests exist for aspects of regression assumptions, but a low or high \(p\)-value is not an all-purpose quality certificate. A small sample can have little power to detect departures from normality; a large sample may detect deviations that have negligible inferential consequences. Tests chosen after inspecting many plots introduce multiple-testing and model-selection issues. A better protocol combines subject-matter reasoning, residual inspection, sensitivity analysis and a pre-specified interpretation goal.

For heteroscedasticity, the subsequent guide develops robust standard errors and sensitivity analyses. For collinearity, VIF and condition number describe the design but do not test whether a coefficient is causally interpretable. For influence, deletion comparisons quantify dependence of estimates on particular records, but they do not label those records as false. Each test has assumptions, sample-size limitations and a narrow null hypothesis. Use numerical tools to answer a defined question, not to replace scientific judgment about sampling, measurement and the target population.

31. Reproducibility and transparent reporting

A reproducible analysis provides the complete dataset or permitted source, variable definitions and units, design matrix, fit specification, software version, calculations, diagnostic thresholds used for screening, and reasons for any corrections or exclusions. In this teaching guide all data are displayed, so anyone can recompute the coefficients. In real household research, privacy may prevent publishing records; a data dictionary, anonymized or approved synthetic illustration, code and a controlled-access reproducibility plan may still support scrutiny, provided data provenance is never misrepresented.

When showing two fits, state \(n=12\) and \(n=11\) directly beside their results. Explain that all reported values are descriptive associations in this nonexperimental example and distinguish a full-population question from a lower-income restricted-population question. Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests also require examination of assumptions. Do not describe an unexecuted Q–Q, browser or statistical test as passed. The research report is strengthened when uncertainty and unavailable checks are visible, not hidden behind numerical polish.

32. From a Q–Q idea to a reproducible quantile comparison

A Q–Q plot should not become an incantation that we interpret solely by looking for a straight line. Suppose, for a simplified illustration, that five internally standardized residuals sorted from smallest to largest are \((-1.5,-0.5,0,0.4,1.7)\). A common plotting-position convention uses \(u_i=(i-0.5)/5\), giving cumulative probabilities \((0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9)\). The corresponding standard-normal quantiles are approximately \((-1.282,-0.524,0,0.524,1.282)\). Plot each theoretical quantile on the horizontal axis and the sorted standardized residual on the vertical axis. The endpoints depart more strongly than the central observations, suggesting somewhat heavier tails in this constructed list. The observation count is too small to infer a population distribution reliably.

In an actual regression, residuals are interdependent due to estimating coefficients and their variances depend on leverage. The preceding list is a separate conceptual illustration, not fabricated diagnostic output from the twelve-household dataset. It teaches how theoretical quantiles are chosen; the household diagnostic should use its own computed residuals. A line through the center is a visual reference rather than a proof of normality. If the research relies on exact small-sample confidence statements, discuss whether the assumptions are justified by measurement and design in addition to the plot. Q–Q behavior alone cannot diagnose nonconstant variance, and a nearly straight Q–Q plot does not rescue a curved residual-versus-income pattern.

33. Standard errors and confidence intervals across sensitivity fits

The consequences of a high-influence observation are clearest when we compare estimated slopes and their uncertainty. Under the conventional homoscedastic OLS calculations, the full twelve-household model estimates the income coefficient as \(0.62356\) with standard error approximately \(0.05856\), producing a nominal 95% \(t\) confidence interval \([0.49109,0.75602]\) with nine residual degrees of freedom. After the transparent eleven-household sensitivity refit, the corresponding income estimate is \(0.43045\), standard error \(0.05363\), and nominal interval \([0.30678,0.55411]\) with eight degrees of freedom. Both intervals are expressed in hundreds of spending dollars per additional hundred income dollars.

The intervals overlap slightly, but this does not mean the two coefficients represent the same estimand or that case deletion is innocuous. They come from overlapping samples and should not be interpreted as independent study results. The model's error scale and predictor geometry change, so the intervals answer different conditional sampling questions under the same model form. Notice too that the household-size coefficient is \(1.20161\) with standard error \(1.13844\) in the full fit; its nominal 95% interval \([-1.37371,3.77694]\) crosses zero. In the reduced fit, the estimate is \(2.50794\), standard error \(0.69911\), and interval \([0.89580,4.12008]\). Merely reporting that significance changed would discard economically meaningful differences and uncertainty. These intervals are illustrative under classical assumptions—not validated inferential claims about an actual population.

34. What case deletion does to prediction, not only slopes

Suppose an analyst wants to predict monthly nonhousing expenditure for a household with income \(X=50\) hundreds of dollars and size \(Z=4\). The full-model fitted mean is approximately \(2.55199+0.62356(50)+1.20161(4)=38.536\) hundreds of dollars. The eleven-row model gives \(6.94805+0.43045(50)+2.50794(4)\approx38.502\) hundreds. At this well-represented profile, predictions are remarkably similar even though the slopes differ. The two models can compensate through intercepts and correlated coefficients, a practical illustration of why prediction stability and individual coefficient stability are separate questions.

Now consider \(X=125\), \(Z=6\). The full fit predicts about \(87.706\) hundreds, while the eleven-row model predicts \(6.94805+0.43045(125)+2.50794(6)\approx75.802\) hundreds. The difference is over \(11.9\) hundreds of dollars, but interpreting it demands an important qualification: income \(125\) is outside the retained eleven-row support, so the reduced fit is extrapolating far beyond the data that estimated it. One should not simply favor the closer prediction to the observed \(Y=92\); the full fit used that response to estimate its coefficients, and evaluating the same row is not an independent predictive test. For genuine forecast evaluation, reserve appropriate new data or use a suitable cross-validation design, while preserving the sampling structure.

35. VIF can be low while inference is still invalid

Consider a set of predictors deliberately varied independently, so each auxiliary \(R_j^2\) is close to zero and VIF values are near one. This is favorable for separating their coefficients conditional on the model, but does not rule out omitted variables, measurement error, misspecified nonlinear relationships, time dependence or heteroscedasticity. A coefficient can be precisely estimated and still fail to represent a causal effect. Conversely a VIF of fifteen does not by itself invalidate a prediction about the conditional mean in the observed predictor domain. The distinction is not semantic: uncertainty about a coefficient direction differs from uncertainty about a linear combination of coefficients at a supported predictor profile.

The conditional variance of a fitted mean at a new vector \(\mathbf x_0\) under classical assumptions is \(\sigma^2\mathbf x_0^\mathsf T(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf x_0\). This quantity depends on the entire inverse matrix and on the prediction profile, not on any single VIF alone. In a strongly correlated design, certain combinations such as \(\beta_1+2\beta_2\) can be estimated much more accurately than the individual coefficients. This motivates examining which scientifically meaningful contrast is actually identified. When writing a research conclusion, state whether the concern is coefficient interpretation, predicted means, extrapolation or causal attribution, then report the relevant uncertainty measure instead of treating VIF as a model-wide veto.

36. A disciplined response to an unexpectedly large diagnostic

Imagine that Cook's distance highlights three observations instead of one. A poor workflow might repeatedly remove the largest row, refit, remove the next, and stop when a favored hypothesis becomes significant. That procedure uses the outcomes twice: first to select a convenient sample and then to assess significance as though the sample had been fixed in advance. A more defensible workflow logs the original study population, verifies each flagged record against raw sources, specifies a limited set of justified alternative analyses, and reports the entire sensitivity range. If there are several plausible definitions of an error, use the definitions consistently, not selectively.

A complete analyst's note should identify the numerical flag, the independent evidence about data quality, whether the case is included in the target population, the exact refit specification, coefficient and prediction changes, uncertainty limits, and an explicit final decision. In regulated or policy settings, store both the untouched dataset and documented corrected copy so that others can reproduce the outcome. Sensitivity is not a weakness to hide: it identifies which assumptions carry the conclusion. When a conclusion is fragile, the scientifically useful next step may be to improve sampling rather than invent a universal numerical cutoff.

37. Conceptual synthesis before the independent exercises

At this point one might ask how the three main families of diagnostics can be remembered without mixing them up. Start with the response direction: raw and studentized residuals ask whether outcomes differ unusually from what the model predicts, while residual patterns suggest possible misspecification. Next consider predictor geometry: leverage asks which rows sit at unusual predictor positions, and VIF asks whether entire predictor columns are redundant. Finally consider conclusion sensitivity: Cook's distance and case deletion quantify how much the fitted answer depends on individual rows. High leverage and collinearity both arise from the design matrix but describe different dimensions of the problem.

To test your own understanding, construct three hypothetical households: one with ordinary income and unexpectedly high spending, one with extreme income but spending exactly on the fitted plane, and one with both extreme income and unexpected spending. The first has an outcome deviation; the second has potential influence through leverage; the third can have observed influence. Then imagine a dataset in which income and years of work experience almost duplicate one another for all households; this creates column collinearity without requiring any of those three cases. A student who can explain these distinctions and select an appropriate diagnostic before seeing a software result has moved beyond memorizing thresholds to genuine transfer.

38. Worked exercise 1: compute a residual and interpret units

Question. In the full fitted model \(\widehat Y=2.55199+0.62356X+1.20161Z\), take a household with \(X=40\), \(Z=4\) and observed \(Y=36\). Find its fitted value and raw residual, and translate the result into dollars. Solution. Substitute the two recorded predictor values: \(\widehat Y=2.55199+0.62356(40)+1.20161(4)=2.55199+24.9424+4.80644≈32.3008\). Therefore \(e=36-32.3008=3.6992\) hundreds of dollars, or about \(369.92 more observed spending than the fitted mean. The positive sign indicates above-prediction spending. Do not say that this household “spent \)369.92 because it had four members.” Regression residuals collect both noise and unmodeled features, and all coefficients are approximate due to rounding.

Independent check. Insert the row in the original dataset and fit the coefficients with full precision; the result should agree within the displayed rounding error. Transfer. If income were expressed in dollars instead of hundreds, the reported numerical income slope would divide by 100, while this household's predicted spending in hundreds of dollars would not change. State the units every time a coefficient is interpreted.

39. Worked exercise 2: verify the leverage average

Question. A model has twelve observations and three fitted coefficients. What is the mean leverage, and what does a leverage of \(0.735\) mean? Solution. The hat matrix trace equals its rank \(p=3\) under full column rank, so \(\sum_i h_{ii}=3\). Dividing by \(12\) gives the average \(3/12=0.25\). The twelfth household's \(0.735\) is about \(0.735/0.25=2.94\) times the mean, showing an unusual predictor position. It is not a 73.5% probability that the row is wrong and not a 73.5% chance of an outlier.

Verification. Add the twelve displayed leverages; they should total three apart from rounding. Transfer. If a model adds a genuinely independent predictor, \(p\) becomes four and mean leverage becomes \(4/12=0.333\). This increase follows from parameter count and does not establish that any observation became erroneous.

40. Worked exercise 3: standardize the influential residual

Question. Given \(e_{12}\approx4.294\), \(s\approx3.273\) and \(h_{12,12}\approx0.735\), calculate the internally studentized residual. Solution. First compute \(1-h\approx0.265\) and its square root \(\sqrt{0.265}\approx0.515\). Multiply by the residual scale: \(3.273(0.515)\approx1.685\). Divide \(4.294/1.685\approx2.54\); using unrounded inputs gives about \(2.548\). This is a dimensionless deviation, whereas the raw residual was expressed in hundreds of dollars.

Verification. The studentized residual differs from \(e/s\approx1.31\) because high leverage reduces the raw-residual variance. Interpretation. A value above two deserves inspection but, in twelve observations, is neither a data-error diagnosis nor permission for automatic deletion. If the model is heteroscedastic, the formula's homoscedastic variance interpretation also needs qualification.

41. Worked exercise 4: calculate Cook's distance

Question. Use \(r=2.548\), \(h=0.735\) and \(p=3\) to approximate Cook's distance. Solution. Square the standardized residual: \(2.548^2\approx6.493\). Divide by three to get about \(2.164\). Calculate the leverage ratio \(0.735/(1-0.735)=0.735/0.265\approx2.774\). The product is approximately \(2.164(2.774)\approx6.00\). This large value means that the row has substantial measured influence on the fitted coefficient vector in the standard OLS metric.

Verification. The independently refitted eleven-observation coefficients differ materially from the full fit, consistent with the Cook's-distance diagnostic. Interpretation. The frequently used guideline \(4/n=4/12=0.333\) is a screening reference, not a probabilistic threshold or an automatic removal rule. Report which coefficients and conclusions changed rather than only the flag.

42. Worked exercise 5: measure coefficient sensitivity

Question. Compare the conditional income slopes before and after omitting household 12 for sensitivity. Solution. The full slope is approximately \(0.62356\) and the eleven-row slope is \(0.43045\). Their absolute difference is \(0.19311\) hundreds of dollars spent per \(100 in income, equivalent to about \)19.31 in monthly expenditure for a \(100 income difference. Relative to the eleven-row estimate, this is \)0.19311/0.43045\approx44.9%$. The comparison shows that the fitted income association is sensitive to inclusion of the high-income row.

Verification. Compare the complete coefficient vectors, not only the income slope; the size slope also changes. Interpretation. The restricted fit cannot reliably describe income \(125\) merely because its eleven-row slope seems more typical. The target population and reasons for changing the sample must be stated before treating either specification as preferable.

43. Worked exercise 6: calculate a VIF

Question. An auxiliary regression predicts an explanatory variable with \(R_j^2=0.84\). Compute its VIF and the standard-error multiplier. Solution. The unexplained fraction is \(1-0.84=0.16\). Therefore \(\operatorname{VIF}_j=1/0.16=6.25\). The multiplier for the standard error under the stated fixed-design comparison is \(\sqrt{6.25}=2.5\). The VIF of 6.25 is a variance multiplier, not a statement that 6.25% of outcomes are wrong.

Verification. Multiplying a hypothetical orthogonal-design slope variance of \(4\) by \(6.25\) gives \(25\), and standard errors change from \(2\) to \(5\), a factor of \(2.5\). Interpretation. Whether this threatens the research depends on required precision, predictor meaning and independent variation; no universal VIF cutoff determines model validity.

44. Worked exercise 7: demonstrate exact singularity

Question. A regression includes an intercept, income \(X\) and annual income \(12X\) where both columns use consistent monthly units. Can their separate slopes be uniquely estimated? Solution. No: the annual column is an exact scalar multiple of the monthly column. In the design matrix, one column is a linear combination of another, so there exists a nonzero vector \(\mathbf v\) with \(\mathbf X\mathbf v=0\). If \(\mathbf b\) fits the observations, then \(\mathbf b+c\mathbf v\) produces the same predictions for every scalar \(c\). Hence the coefficient vector is not unique and \(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X\) is singular.

Verification. The fitted sum \(b_1X+b_2(12X)=(b_1+12b_2)X\) depends only on the combined slope. Interpretation. Remove or redefine a redundant column according to the scientific question; collecting additional rows with the exact same conversion cannot separate the two supposed effects.

45. Worked exercise 8: diagnose an omitted curve

Question. Residuals against income are positive at both low and high values but negative in the middle, while their sample correlation with income is zero. Is the mean equation validated? Solution. No. OLS forces sample residuals to be orthogonal to included predictor columns, which makes a zero linear correlation unsurprising. A U-shaped pattern suggests an omitted quadratic or other nonlinear mean relationship. Sketch the curve, identify the range where predictions are systematically too low or high and consider whether a physically or economically plausible nonlinear specification is justified.

Verification. A quadratic pattern can have opposing positive and negative contributions to \(\sum_i X_ie_i\) that cancel. Interpretation. Test or validate the revised specification rather than assuming a curvature term is automatically correct. In the same dataset, after-the-fact model selection changes the evidentiary status of naive \(p\)-values.

46. Worked exercise 9: distinguish normality, spread and dependence

Question. Three graphs show (A) curved residuals versus fitted values, (B) increasing vertical spread with fitted values, and (C) long runs of positive and negative residuals ordered by month. Name each concern. Solution. Graph A suggests the conditional mean is misspecified; B suggests heteroscedasticity, because error variance changes with predictors; and C suggests possible serial dependence. A normal Q–Q plot would investigate distributional shape but would not directly resolve any of these three structural concerns. Different remedies are required: investigate a justified mean specification, reconsider the variance/inference model, and account for time-dependent sampling respectively.

Verification. Ask whether the pattern persists within meaningful groups and whether it could be generated by data-entry errors. Interpretation. No residual plot proves causal identification or independence. Graphical appearances should lead to qualified questions, not unquestioned model changes.

47. Worked exercise 10: examine a high-leverage but well-fitted row

Question. Suppose another high-income household has \(h=0.80\) but exactly zero residual in the fitted model. What does standard Cook's distance imply? Solution. From \(D_i=(r_i^2/p)h/(1-h)\) and \(r_i=0\), Cook's distance is zero despite large leverage. The observation has the potential to pull the model if its response changed, but it exhibits no measured leave-one-out influence on the fitted coefficient vector under the current fit. Leverage and influence are not interchangeable.

Verification. The coefficient-deletion identity includes the factor \(e_i\), so a zero residual produces no coefficient change when the remaining design is identifiable. Interpretation. In high-dimensional or near-singular designs, assess rank and numerical stability as well; a zero Cook's distance does not certify the full model's substantive validity.

48. Worked exercise 11: decide whether to remove a record

Question. A household is flagged by Cook's distance. The source survey confirms that its high income and spending were transcribed correctly and that it belongs to the target population. What should the analyst do? Solution. Do not delete solely because of a diagnostic threshold. Retain it in the primary stated analysis, document the source check and quantify sensitivity using a clearly labeled alternative fit. Consider increasing the representation of similar high-income households, checking functional form and reviewing the population definition. If a narrower low-income population is the legitimate scientific target, define that target explicitly and design a corresponding analysis rather than silently changing the sample.

Verification. Compare predictions and confidence intervals at predictor profiles common to both fits; avoid claiming the eleven-row fit was directly validated at income 125. Interpretation. The goal is transparent inference and generalizability, not a smaller Cook's distance or a preferred coefficient sign.

49. Worked exercise 12: a full diagnostic transfer decision

Question. A new study has high \(R^2\), VIFs of 18 and 21 for two expenditure predictors, one row with high leverage, and a residual plot that fans outward with fitted values. Can the researcher say the income coefficient is precise, the model predicts reliably and the unusual row must be removed? Solution. No to all three inferences without further evidence. High VIF values suggest weak separation of the two conditional slopes, so standard errors may be inflated. The funnel suggests nonconstant variance, potentially invalidating conventional standard errors. High leverage is only potential influence until residuals, Cook's distance and deletion sensitivity are examined. High in-sample \(R^2\) cannot validate prediction on new households; examine appropriate out-of-sample performance and predictor support.

Verification plan. Audit raw records; construct residual/fitted and Q–Q plots; calculate leverage, studentized residuals, Cook's distance, VIF and condition number; refit specified sensitivity models; check independent variation; evaluate held-out predictions if the sample permits; and report uncertainty. Final interpretation. The model may be useful for limited prediction while failing to separate individual coefficients precisely. It may also require a revised variance model. No screening statistic alone determines which households belong in the study.

50. Closing synthesis: explain the reasoning, not just the flags

A good regression diagnosis distinguishes three axes of vulnerability. Residual diagnostics ask whether the conditional mean, error spread, distributional assumptions and dependence structure are plausible. Influence diagnostics ask whether unusual observations in predictor or outcome space disproportionately change fitted conclusions. Multicollinearity diagnostics ask whether the design contains enough independent information to separate conditional slopes. One model can fail on one axis while appearing satisfactory on the others.

Return to the household example. The twelfth row is high leverage because its income is unusual. Its scaled residual and Cook's distance show influential behavior under the specified OLS fit. The income slope changes materially when the row is omitted, but this does not establish that the record is wrong. The income–size VIF indicates some predictor overlap; it does not establish poor predictions or causal bias. The scientifically defensible output is a documented investigation with sensitivity results and boundaries on generalization. Student Understanding Test: Can you reproduce the full-data prediction, the standardized residual, Cook's distance, a deletion comparison and a VIF, and explain in your own words why none alone authorizes deleting a real household? If not, revisit the relevant worked exercise before treating the diagnostic as understood.
