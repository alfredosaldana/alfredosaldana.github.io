Accelerated Failure Time Regression: Estimation and Interpretation

Derive and verify Time Ratios, quantile predictions, censored likelihood, and the special Weibull AFT–PH equivalence.

Prerequisites: 32, 53, 76, 78Complete synthetic datasetMathJax 332 modules
MODULE 01 / 32

1. The question changes: from a distribution to a regression

Guide 78 described a single lifetime distribution: the probability that a device survives beyond a particular operating hour, the instantaneous hazard among functioning devices, and the likelihood contribution of a censored record. Those ideas are now our foundation. In practice, machines rarely operate under identical conditions. Some undergo ordinary loading; others run at elevated temperature or load. A maintenance manager wants to know not only how long equipment lasts overall, but how the entire distribution of failure times changes with conditions. That is the motivating question for Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) regression.

An AFT model relates covariates to the logarithm of an event time. Its most useful interpretation is multiplicative on the original time scale: a covariate can compress or stretch the distribution of lifetime. A factor of 0.70 says that comparable time quantiles are 70% as long, not that the probability of failure is 70%. Similarly, a factor of 1.30 means a 30% increase in comparable times, not a 30-percentage-point increase in survival probability.

We will follow a hypothetical set of industrial modules, recording the first component failure under standard or high-load operation. The complete dataset is synthetic and constructed solely for instruction. It supplies a transparent foundation for every likelihood calculation, fitted parameter and illustrated curve. The difference between the two operating groups is an observational association within the invented example; it is not evidence that load causally changes real equipment life.

The essential prerequisites are Guide 32 on continuous distributions, Guide 53 on regression interpretation and functional form, Guide 76 on Cox regression, and Guide 78 on Exponential, Weibull and Lognormal lifetime models. We reconstruct the key principles here. By the end, you should explain what a Time Ratio means, derive a censored AFT likelihood, verify a median prediction, distinguish AFT from Proportional Hazards (PH), and recognize what would invalidate the conclusions.

Learning promise. Each important mathematical relationship is followed by its interpretation, a numerical check and a limitation. The objective is not merely to read a coefficient; it is to explain why the coefficient corresponds to a change in duration.

MODULE 02 / 32

2. Define the clock, outcome and covariate before the model

A survival analysis starts with a coherent clock. In our example time zero is the first moment of monitored operation. The event is the first functional failure under a declared test definition. An observed lifetime ends when that failure occurs; a censored follow-up ends while the module is still functioning. This distinction is essential because a module that worked for 14 hundred-hour units might fail the next day. Its observed value of 14 is not its known failure time.

Let \(T>0\) be actual time until first failure, \(C>0\) the independent administrative withdrawal time, \(Y=\min(T,C)\) observed follow-up, and \(\delta=I(T\le C)\) the event indicator. Here 1 means a failure was observed, and 0 means the observation is right-censored. Let \(x=0\) indicate standard operation and \(x=1\) indicate high-load operation. Recorded times are hundreds of operating hours: a value 5 means 500 hours, not five calendar days.

These are all twelve records. Standard operation has four failures, two censored records and observed exposure \(5+7+9+12+14+18=65\) hundred-hours. High-load operation also has four failures and two censored observations, with exposure \(3+5+6+8+10+12=44\) hundred-hours. Together there are 8 failures and 4 censored observations across 109 hundred-hours of follow-up. These totals are useful cross-checks, not estimates of average complete lifetime. Never treat \(Y\) as if it were always the actual \(T\).

Our primary analysis assumes independent modules, the same timing and failure definitions across groups, positive event times, a correctly specified error family, and censoring independent of latent failure time conditional on the covariates included. In a real investigation, condition-based withdrawal of suspicious machines would challenge the last assumption.

MODULE 03 / 32

3. AFT as a transformation of time

Start with an intuitive comparison. Suppose a reference component has median lifetime 1,200 hours. If high load multiplies all time quantiles by 0.70, its median becomes 840 hours. Its 90th percentile also becomes 0.70 times the reference 90th percentile. This is stronger than merely saying that two means differ: it describes an entire family of comparable quantiles under a common time transformation.

One convenient formulation sets a baseline random lifetime \(T_0\) and a positive acceleration factor \(a(x)\):

KEY EQUATION
\[T_x=a(x)T_0,\qquad a(x)=\exp(x^{\mathsf T}\beta)>0.\]

Taking natural logarithms turns multiplication into addition:

KEY EQUATION
\[\log T_x=x^{\mathsf T}\beta+\log T_0.\]

The random reference term \(\log T_0\) still needs a distribution. We usually represent that randomness with a location, scale and standardized error; the choice of error distribution will distinguish Lognormal from Weibull AFT. The factor \(a(x)\) is dimensionless because it multiplies a duration. Consequently its logarithm is also dimensionless, while a raw intercept for \(\log T\) implicitly refers to the specified time unit.

When \(a(x)<1\), event times are compressed; when \(a(x)>1\), they are stretched. The phrase “accelerated failure” can confuse because some authors call the reciprocal \(1/a(x)\) an acceleration factor. In these guides, Time Ratio equals \(a(x)\). We explicitly state that choice before interpreting any output. Smaller Time Ratio means shorter predicted duration. Different terminology must not cause an inverse interpretation.

The transformation assumes a single scale factor applies across the modeled quantiles for given covariates. It does not imply every observed machine fails at exactly \(a\) times another machine's recorded time: individuals have different random lifetimes. Nor does this purely statistical transformation assert that operating conditions were randomized or that changing load physically causes the predicted change.

MODULE 04 / 32

4. The log-linear statistical model and notation

A commonly used AFT equation is a linear regression on log-time:

KEY EQUATION
\[\boxed{\log T=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\cdots+\beta_px_p+\sigma\varepsilon},\qquad \sigma>0.\]

Here \(T\) is a strictly positive failure time; \(x_j\) is the value of predictor \(j\); \(\beta_0\) is the log-time intercept; \(\beta_j\) is the conditional effect of one unit of predictor \(j\) on log-time; \(\sigma\) is the positive error-scale parameter; and \(\varepsilon\) is a standardized random error whose specified distribution determines the lifetime family. If a predictor measures temperature in degrees Celsius, then its coefficient concerns a one-degree change unless temperature has been explicitly rescaled.

For a fixed covariate vector, the model implies a conditional location for log-time \(\mu(x)=\beta_0+x^{\mathsf T}\beta\). An observable duration is not predicted exactly from this location because \(\sigma\varepsilon\) remains random. AFT therefore predicts conditional distributions and quantiles, not a certain failure deadline. In a Lognormal AFT model the chosen error is standard Normal, so \(\log T\mid x\) is Normal with mean \(\mu(x)\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). A Weibull AFT model uses a particular extreme-value error, with a different connection between the location and the distribution's scale.

Subtract log-times for two covariate profiles that differ by one unit of \(x_j\), holding all other covariates constant. Their systematic difference is \(\beta_j\). Exponentiating gives the multiplicative time contrast. Because the random error distribution and \(\sigma\) are common, the contrast applies to all corresponding modeled quantiles.

KEY EQUATION
\[\frac{t_p(x_j+1,x_{-j})}{t_p(x_j,x_{-j})}=e^{\beta_j}\equiv\mathrm{TR}_j.\]

This equation makes the interpretation testable. The numerator and denominator are times at the same event-time percentile under two profiles. It is not a comparison of cumulative failure percentages at an arbitrary fixed hour. The word “conditional” matters: with several predictors, \(e^{\beta_j}\) compares profiles with other predictors held fixed, provided that contrast is meaningful within the observed covariate range.

MODULE 05 / 32

5. Interpret a coefficient without confusing percentages

Consider a declared illustrative coefficient of \(\beta_{\mathrm{load}}=-0.40\). Its Time Ratio is \(e^{-0.40}=0.670320\). Thus the modeled lifetime quantiles under high load are about 67.0% of the comparable quantiles under standard load; equivalently they are about 33.0% shorter. An engineer should not say “67% lower” or “a 0.40-hour decrease.” Neither description follows from the log-time parameter.

KEY EQUATION
\[\mathrm{TR}=e^{-0.40}=0.670320,\qquad 100(0.670320-1)=-32.968\%.\]

If the reference median were 1,200 hours, the high-load median under that illustrative coefficient would be \(1200\times0.670320=804.384\) hours. The calculation is a multiplication on the original time scale. There is no subtraction of 0.40 hours, and the result does not describe a reduction in the probability of failure. If the reference 80th percentile were 2,000 hours, the corresponding percentile would be \(2,000\times0.670320=1,340.64\) hours.

An alternative legitimate interpretation reverses the comparison: standard versus high load has Time Ratio \(1/0.670320=1.491825\), meaning 49.2% longer modeled quantiles for standard relative to high load. Note why percentage statements are not symmetric: 33.0% shorter relative to one baseline corresponds to 49.2% longer relative to the smaller baseline. Both use the same ratio, with opposite reference direction.

For a continuous covariate, a change of \(\Delta\) units gives \(\exp(\beta_j\Delta)\), not just \(\exp(\beta_j)\). If \(\beta_j=0.03\) per degree and temperature rises five degrees, Time Ratio is \(e^{0.15}\approx1.1618\), or approximately 16.2% longer predicted quantiles under the model. Such a sign would demand careful physical explanation for a real device; the mathematics does not guarantee it is scientifically plausible.

MODULE 06 / 32

6. Survival, density and hazard under time stretching

An AFT model is useful because the time transformation translates directly into familiar survival quantities. If \(T_x=a(x)T_0\), then \(T_x>t\) exactly when \(T_0>t/a(x)\). Therefore the conditional survival curve shifts horizontally on the time axis:

KEY EQUATION
\[S(t\mid x)=S_0\!\left(\frac{t}{a(x)}\right).\]

This is not merely an approximation near the median: it is the exact survival relationship of an AFT scaling model. It says that the survival probability at 800 hours for a time-compressed profile equals the reference survival probability at a later baseline-equivalent time \(800/a\). For \(a=0.8\), that is \(S_0(1000)\). Hence the compressed profile has generally lower survival at the same fixed operating hour when baseline survival decreases with time.

Differentiate the survival relationship with respect to time. Using the chain rule and \(f=-S'\), we get a scaled density. Dividing by survival gives the corresponding conditional hazard:

KEY EQUATION
\[\begin{aligned} f(t\mid x)&=\frac{1}{a(x)}f_0\!\left(\frac{t}{a(x)}\right),\\ h(t\mid x)&=\frac{1}{a(x)}h_0\!\left(\frac{t}{a(x)}\right). \end{aligned}\]

The factor \(1/a\) gives the correct density and hazard units: if \(t\) is measured in hundred-hours, then both quantities have inverse hundred-hour units. Survival remains a dimensionless probability. Notice that a hazard under AFT is not necessarily a constant multiple of the baseline hazard at the same time: it also evaluates the baseline hazard at \(t/a\). This is the main reason Time Ratios and Hazard Ratios cannot generally be treated as reciprocals.

AFT comparisons can still cross or have time-dependent hazard ratios depending on the baseline family. The AFT assumption concerns time-scaled survival curves, not necessarily Proportional Hazards. Investigate whether a common time factor fits different observed quantiles rather than assuming a model is valid because its coefficient has an attractive interpretation.

MODULE 07 / 32

7. Why ordinary linear regression on observed times fails

A tempting shortcut is to calculate \(\log Y\) for every row and run ordinary least squares (OLS) on load. That would be wrong under right censoring because \(Y\) is not always \(T\). A censored module observed at 1,400 hours contributes evidence that its actual lifetime exceeded 1,400 hours; OLS falsely treats it as a failure precisely at 1,400 hours. It imposes a value on the unobserved response that was never measured.

Even fitting OLS only to failures is generally invalid. The failure-only subset is selected according to whether failure occurred before censoring, so it overrepresents early events. The four standard-group failures occur at 5, 9, 12 and 18, whereas the two censored standard records still might have much longer true durations. Omitting those two observations loses real information and changes the conditional distribution of the cases used for regression.

An AFT likelihood instead builds a distinct contribution from each record. A failed observation at \(y\) contributes the conditional density \(f(y\mid x)\); a censored observation contributes the conditional survival probability \(S(y\mid x)\). That difference is not an optional technical adjustment—it is the mathematical representation of what we actually know.

KEY EQUATION
\[L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n} f(y_i\mid x_i;\theta)^{\delta_i}\,S(y_i\mid x_i;\theta)^{1-\delta_i}.\]

The parameter vector \(\theta\) includes regression coefficients and distribution-specific scale or shape parameters. This formula assumes independent observations and noninformative censoring conditional on the included predictors; it also assumes the chosen likelihood correctly describes the distribution of the event time. A fitted model cannot rescue informative withdrawal or a wrongly defined time origin.

MODULE 08 / 32

8. Lognormal AFT: obtain the formulas rather than memorizing them

Choose the standardized error \(\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)\), a standard Normal random variable. Then the model implies \(\log T\mid x\sim N(\mu(x),\sigma^2)\), where \(\mu(x)=\beta_0+x^{\mathsf T}\beta\) and \(\sigma>0\). To calculate probabilities on the original time scale, standardize the log-time using \(z(t,x)=[\log t-\mu(x)]/\sigma\). The normal CDF \(\Phi(z)\) measures the probability of a standardized Normal draw at or below \(z\).

The conditional survival function is the complement of the failure CDF. The density requires the Jacobian \(d(\log t)/dt=1/t\); omitting this factor makes the expression integrate incorrectly on the time scale.

KEY EQUATION
\[\begin{aligned} z(t,x)&=\frac{\log t-\mu(x)}{\sigma},\\ S(t\mid x)&=1-\Phi(z(t,x)),\\ f(t\mid x)&=\frac{\phi(z(t,x))}{t\sigma},\\ h(t\mid x)&=\frac{\phi(z(t,x))}{t\sigma[1-\Phi(z(t,x))]}. \end{aligned}\]

Here \(\phi\) is the standard Normal density. As a check, when \(t=e^{\mu(x)}\), we have \(z=0\) and \(S=0.5\). Hence the conditional median is exactly \(e^{\mu(x)}\). The conditional mean is instead \(e^{\mu(x)+\sigma^2/2}\), larger than the median when \(\sigma>0\). Mixing these two summaries is a frequent interpretation error.

For example, let \(\mu(0)=\log(12)\) and \(\sigma=0.50\). The standard-operation median is 12 hundred-hours, or 1,200 hours. Under a coefficient \(-0.40\), the high-load median is \(12e^{-0.4}=8.04384\) hundred-hours, or approximately 804.4 hours. The 90th percentile for either group uses the same standard Normal quantile \(z_{0.90}=1.28155\), so the ratio between the groups remains \(e^{-0.4}\).

MODULE 09 / 32

9. Lognormal quantiles and full time-scale interpretation

A conditional event-time percentile \(t_p(x)\) satisfies \(P(T\le t_p\mid x)=p\), for \(0<p<1\). In the Lognormal model, write \(\Phi([\log t_p-\mu(x)]/\sigma)=p\), apply the inverse Normal CDF and exponentiate. Every step matters because taking an inverse on the wrong scale gives a nonsensical negative failure time.

KEY EQUATION
\[\boxed{t_p(x)=\exp\!\left[\mu(x)+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)\right]}.\]

At \(p=0.5\), the standard Normal quantile is zero; hence the median is \(e^{\mu(x)}\). At \(p=0.90\), the quantile is approximately 1.28155. For reference \(\mu(0)=\log12\) and \(\sigma=0.5\), the 90th percentile equals \(12\exp(0.640775)\approx22.775\) hundred-hours. Under high load with \(\beta_1=-0.4\), the same percentile is approximately \(22.775\times0.67032\approx15.267\) hundred-hours. The ratios match because the distributional spread \(\sigma\) is assumed common.

The 90th percentile is the time by which the model expects 90% of events to have occurred, not the time at which 90% remain alive. In survival language the 90th event percentile corresponds to \(S=0.10\). This distinction is important when communicating to a maintenance manager: an 80th-percentile failure time and an 80% reliability horizon are opposite tail statements.

To verify a proposed percentile, substitute it back into the normal standardized expression. The reference 90th percentile has \(z=[\log(12e^{0.640775})-\log12]/0.5=1.28155\), so \(F=\Phi(1.28155)\approx0.90\). This independent check detects mistakes in signs, the logarithm base or time units.

MODULE 10 / 32

10. Build the censored Lognormal likelihood record by record

For a failed module we know its event time and evaluate the density; for a censored module we know only that it survived past the recorded cutoff and evaluate the survival function. Plug the Lognormal formulas into the general likelihood and take logarithms to replace products with sums:

KEY EQUATION
\[\ell(\beta,\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\delta_i\left[-\log y_i-\log\sigma-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12 z_i^2\right]+(1-\delta_i)\log[1-\Phi(z_i)]\right\}.\]

Here \(z_i=[\log y_i-x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta]/\sigma\); the intercept is included in the predictor vector. The \(-\log y_i\) term arises from the log-time transformation and cannot be omitted. For a censored observation the \(\log S\) contribution is generally negative because survival probabilities lie between zero and one. A late censored observation can still strongly influence the fit because it tells the model the subject survived a relatively long time.

Consider record 2, standard operation and censoring at \(y=7\). Its contribution is \(\log\{1-\Phi[(\log7-\beta_0)/\sigma]\}\), not the Lognormal log-density at seven. Consider record 7, high-load failure at \(y=3\). Its contribution is \(-\log3-\log\sigma-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12[(\log3-\beta_0-\beta_1)/\sigma]^2\). Both expressions use the same coefficients and scale, but different information about the outcome.

Numerical optimization chooses coefficients and \(\sigma>0\) to maximize the sum. A common computational parameterization sets \(\rho=\log\sigma\) and then \(\sigma=e^\rho\); this guarantees a positive standard deviation for every real optimization iterate. We disclose parameterization before reporting results. The example contains only twelve rows, so numerical agreement does not establish reliable inference about real-world high-load devices.

12. Weibull AFT: define the extreme-value parameterization

A Weibull lifetime need not be introduced only through a survival curve. Let \(\varepsilon\) have the minimum extreme-value distribution with \(P(\varepsilon\le e)=1-\exp[-\exp(e)]\). Equivalently its survival probability is \(P(\varepsilon>e)=\exp[-\exp(e)]\). Use the AFT equation \(\log T=\mu(x)+\sigma\varepsilon\), where \(\sigma>0\). We define shape \(k=1/\sigma\) and Weibull scale \(\eta(x)=e^{\mu(x)}\).

For any positive \(t\), the event \(T>t\) corresponds to \(\varepsilon>[\log t-\mu(x)]/\sigma\). Substitute into the stated error survival function to obtain the Weibull AFT survival curve:

KEY EQUATION
\[\boxed{S(t\mid x)=\exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\eta(x)}\right)^k\right]},\quad \eta(x)=e^{\beta_0+x^{\mathsf T}\beta},\quad k=1/\sigma.\]

This derivation explains what the intercept means. For Weibull, \(e^{\mu(x)}=\eta(x)\) is the characteristic scale at which \(S=e^{-1}\approx0.3679\); it is not generally the median. The Weibull median is \(\eta(x)(\log2)^{1/k}\). However, the median ratio between two profiles is still \(e^{\beta_1}\) because shape \(k\) is shared and the factor \((\log2)^{1/k}\) cancels.

The Weibull hazard is \(h(t\mid x)=k t^{k-1}/\eta(x)^k\). It increases with \(t\) if \(k>1\), decreases if \(0<k<1\), and is constant when \(k=1\). A clear error convention is indispensable: replacing the minimum extreme-value error with another sign convention without adjusting coefficients can reverse the reported time effect.

MODULE 13 / 32

13. Weibull censored likelihood, and the fitted example

Define \(\mu_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\), \(\sigma>0\), \(k=1/\sigma\), and \(u_i=\exp[(\log y_i-\mu_i)/\sigma]=(y_i/e^{\mu_i})^k\). A Weibull event contributes \(\log h(y_i)+\log S(y_i)\), whereas a censored record contributes only \(\log S(y_i)=-u_i\). Combining both cases gives a compact and computationally stable likelihood:

KEY EQUATION
\[\ell(\beta,\sigma)=\sum_i\left\{\delta_i\left[\log k-\log y_i+\frac{\log y_i-\mu_i}{\sigma}\right]-\exp\!\left(\frac{\log y_i-\mu_i}{\sigma}\right)\right\}.\]

The final exponential term appears for every observation. Censoring only removes the density's event component, not the survival term. This provides a useful audit: if a program contains no contribution from censored rows, it is not implementing the likelihood above. Optimize over \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\rho=\log\sigma\) and transform back to positive \(\sigma\) and shape \(k\).

For the same synthetic twelve records, the approximate fitted Weibull AFT parameters are \(\hat\beta_0=2.653043\), \(\hat\beta_1=-0.386243\), \(\hat\rho=-0.889503\), so \(\hat\sigma\approx0.4109\) and \(\hat k\approx2.4339\). The maximized log-likelihood is approximately \(-25.449226\). The Weibull Time Ratio is \(e^{-0.386243}\approx0.679606\), interpreted as a 32.0% shorter modeled time scale for high-load operation.

The standard-group median is \(e^{2.653043}(\log2)^{1/2.4339}\approx12.2125\) hundred-hours; the high-load median is approximately 8.2997 hundred-hours. Check that their ratio is about 0.6796. Despite the superficially similar load estimates, these are two different distributional assumptions. With twelve illustrative records we cannot use their close fitted log-likelihoods to assert that either family describes the mechanism of actual industrial failures.

MODULE 14 / 32

14. AFT versus Cox PH: different questions and different ratios

Guide 76 introduced the Cox Proportional Hazards model \(h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)\exp(x^{\mathsf T}\gamma)\). Holding the other predictors fixed, \(e^{\gamma_j}\) is a Hazard Ratio (HR): a ratio of conditional instantaneous event rates at the same time among individuals still at risk. An AFT coefficient instead yields a Time Ratio (TR): a ratio of corresponding time quantiles, comparing the horizontal stretching or compression of survival curves.

KEY EQUATION
\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{AFT:}\quad &\mathrm{TR}_j=e^{\beta_j},\\ \mathrm{PH:}\quad &\mathrm{HR}_j=e^{\gamma_j}. \end{aligned}\]

The two ratios have different denominators, units of comparison and assumptions. A HR of 2 does not generally mean median lifetime is halved. A TR of 0.7 does not generally mean the event rate is \(1/0.7\) times as high at every time. Those statements become linked by a specific transformation only when the assumed survival family supports both AFT and PH with compatible fixed-shape parameterization.

Under an AFT model, the hazard ratio may depend on time because it evaluates \(h_0(t/a)\). Under a PH model, the hazard ratio is required to remain constant in time, while the relationship between quantiles can vary with percentile. Neither framework is automatically superior. The scientific question matters: a maintenance team may understand hours gained through AFT more naturally; a study of instantaneous event rates may emphasize PH. Good reporting states which contrast was modeled, how the baseline is defined and whether diagnostics support the assumption.

A further distinction concerns modeling and prediction. Cox regression is often fitted using a partial likelihood for regression effects without specifying a parametric baseline hazard, whereas parametric AFT specifies a full error distribution for event times. This can make AFT survival quantile prediction convenient but adds sensitivity to distributional misspecification and extrapolation. Neither approach turns observational associations into causal effects by itself.

MODULE 15 / 32

15. The special Weibull AFT–PH equivalence, derived carefully

Weibull is the important special case where the same fixed-shape lifetime family can be expressed as both AFT and PH. Start with the Weibull AFT survival function using shape \(k>0\) and scale \(\eta(x)=e^{\beta_0+x^{\mathsf T}\beta}\). Its cumulative hazard is \(H(t\mid x)=(t/\eta(x))^k\). Differentiate to obtain:

KEY EQUATION
\[\begin{aligned} h(t\mid x)&=k t^{k-1}\exp[-k(\beta_0+x^{\mathsf T}\beta)]\\ &=\underbrace{k t^{k-1}e^{-k\beta_0}}_{h_0(t)}\exp[-k x^{\mathsf T}\beta]. \end{aligned}\]

Compare the right-hand side with the PH form \(h_0(t)e^{x^{\mathsf T}\gamma}\). Coefficients must satisfy \(\gamma=-k\beta\), where \(k=1/\sigma\) under our declared extreme-value convention. For a single one-unit contrast, the relation among ratios follows:

KEY EQUATION
\[\boxed{\mathrm{HR}=e^{-k\beta}=\mathrm{TR}^{-k}},\qquad \boxed{\mathrm{TR}=\mathrm{HR}^{-1/k}}.\]

Apply this to the fitted Weibull values. With \(\widehat{\mathrm{TR}}\approx0.679606\) and \(\hat k\approx2.4339\), the corresponding modeled HR is \(0.679606^{-2.4339}\approx2.5602\). It is not \(1/0.679606\approx1.4714\). Shape changes the translation. The HR of about 2.56 means the model-implied high-load hazard is roughly 2.56 times the standard-load hazard at the same time. That statement is conditional on the fitted Weibull model and a common shape.

The shortcut fails for a general Lognormal AFT model, whose hazard ratio is not constant in time. It also fails if the Weibull shape differs by profile or a model violates its own time-scaling assumptions. Never invert a Cox HR to obtain a Time Ratio unless a compatible distributional argument has been established. This is a conceptual boundary, not an algebraic trick.

MODULE 16 / 32

16. Confidence intervals: uncertainty must be transformed, too

A point estimate never exhausts a scientific conclusion. AFT coefficients are estimated from finite data and thus uncertain. Under a sufficiently regular correctly specified model, an approximate covariance matrix can be obtained by inverting the observed information, the negative Hessian of the log-likelihood at its maximum. The square root of the relevant diagonal element is an approximate standard error of a coefficient. In small or highly censored samples, asymptotic Normal intervals may be unreliable; likelihood profiling, simulation or bootstrap checks may be preferable.

Suppose, as a separate illustrative interval exercise, the estimated log-time coefficient is \(\hat\beta=-0.40\) with estimated standard error \(0.15\). A two-sided approximate 95% Wald interval on the log-time scale is \(-0.40\pm1.96(0.15)\). Multiply first: \(1.96\times0.15=0.294\). Hence the interval endpoints are \(-0.694\) and \(-0.106\), which are then exponentiated to return to the Time Ratio scale.

KEY EQUATION
\[\begin{aligned} CI_{95\%}(\beta)&=[-0.694,-0.106],\\ CI_{95\%}(\mathrm{TR})&=[e^{-0.694},e^{-0.106}]\approx[0.500,0.899]. \end{aligned}\]

All values in this interval example are chosen for illustration, not the calculated standard error of our twelve-row fit. The interval is asymmetric around the Time Ratio point estimate \(e^{-0.4}\approx0.6703\), because exponentiation is nonlinear. It lies below 1 in this example, but that alone would not establish a causal load effect. Validity depends on model specification, independence, censoring, sampling, uncertainty estimation and the actual study design.

For a predicted median, uncertainty also enters through the intercept, coefficients, and potentially shape or scale. For Lognormal, \(\log t_{0.5}(x)=x^{\mathsf T}\beta\); its approximate variance is \(x^{\mathsf T}\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta)x\). The covariance between intercept and slope matters. Exponentiate endpoints of a confidence interval on log-median rather than simply adding and subtracting hours from the median.

MODULE 17 / 32

17. Predict a whole profile: percentiles, survival and units

Time Ratios are compelling because a fitted AFT model can provide a distribution of possible lifetimes for a specified profile. For Lognormal AFT, choose the desired percentile \(p\) and compute \(t_p(x)=\exp[\mu(x)+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)]\). For Weibull AFT, compute \(t_p(x)=\eta(x)[-\log(1-p)]^{1/k}\). Different quantiles encode different maintenance questions: the 10th percentile is an early-failure threshold, while the 90th percentile concerns a far longer horizon.

KEY EQUATION
\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Lognormal:}\quad t_p(x)&=e^{\mu(x)+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)},\\ \mathrm{Weibull:}\quad t_p(x)&=\eta(x)[-\log(1-p)]^{1/k}. \end{aligned}\]

For our fitted Lognormal model, the estimated standard and high-load medians are about 1,186.6 and 765.7 operating hours. These figures are obtained by multiplying the fitted medians in hundred-hour units by 100. If a software package reported times in hours rather than hundred-hours, the log-time intercept would shift by \(\log100\) while the load slope and Time Ratio would stay unchanged. Thus coefficients on covariate contrasts are invariant to a common positive rescaling of time, but baseline location parameters are not.

A fixed-time survival prediction also has an engineering meaning. Under Lognormal with \(\hat\mu_0\approx2.473683\), \(\hat\mu_1\approx2.035626\), and \(\hat\sigma\approx0.547939\), the standardized time at \(t=10\) hundred-hours is \((\log10-\hat\mu_x)/\hat\sigma\). Evaluating \(1-\Phi(z)\) provides the modeled chance of operating beyond 1,000 hours in each profile. A visual comparison of the curves should agree with the shorter fitted high-load quantiles.

A survival probability at time \(t\) and a time percentile \(t_p\) are inverse types of statements. State clearly which is being answered before giving a numerical result. Neither is an individual guarantee. Predicting at a covariate value absent from the data, or far beyond observed follow-up, is extrapolation whose uncertainty may be much greater than a standard confidence band conveys.

MODULE 18 / 32

18. Reading two survival curves as a horizontal movement

Imagine plotting fitted survival against operating hours for standard and high load. The high-load curve reaches any chosen survival level earlier when the fitted Time Ratio is below 1. At the 50% survival level, the horizontal gap corresponds to the difference between the two medians. At the 10% survival level the horizontal gap is larger in absolute hours, but the ratio of the horizontal times remains the same under a common-shape AFT model.

A useful visual test picks a survival height first, draws a horizontal line and reads the two intersection times. Dividing the high-load time by the standard time should yield the same fitted Time Ratio for multiple survival heights if the model follows the AFT structure. Reading the vertical difference in survival probabilities at the same hour answers a different question, so it need not equal a Time Ratio or a constant difference.

A second figure looks at conditional hazard. The Weibull AFT parameterization with common shape also implies PH, so its hazards differ by a constant ratio; the Lognormal AFT hazards generally do not. The shapes of the two plots should be interpreted rather than regarded as decorative illustrations.

The plotted curves are derived from illustrative fitted coefficients and should not be mistaken for nonparametric estimates. In a real study, compare fitted curves with a Kaplan–Meier benchmark by group while considering censoring, sampling noise and whether groups are sufficiently large. Smoothness of a parametric curve is a consequence of its formula, not proof that reality behaves smoothly.

MODULE 19 / 32

19. Model assessment and responsible comparison

AFT model validation has several layers. First check basic data integrity: the event definition, time origin, positivity, handling of ties and covariate coding. Second inspect distributional adequacy: do fitted survival curves agree reasonably with observed event-time patterns inside the period supported by data? Third examine the covariate structure: are time ratios plausibly constant across quantiles, or do early and late failures tell different stories? Fourth assess calibration and prediction error with censoring-aware methods, preferably using data not used to select and fit the model.

The Lognormal and Weibull fits in our synthetic example have log-likelihoods of about \(-25.4823\) and \(-25.4492\). Each estimates three parameters. Therefore their illustrative Akaike Information Criteria (AIC), calculated as \(2p-2\ell\), are approximately 56.9647 and 56.8985. The difference is only about 0.066. It would be indefensible to claim one family is scientifically established by this tiny difference from twelve invented records.

Model Parameters \(p\) Maximum log-likelihood AIC \(=2p-2\ell\)
Lognormal AFT 3 -25.482348 56.964697
Weibull AFT 3 -25.449226 56.898453

AIC compares relative penalized likelihood among the models actually fitted; it does not measure causal truth, validate censoring assumptions, guarantee correct tail behavior, or directly estimate out-of-sample error. With very small data, its assumptions and limitations deserve special care. If the failure mechanism plausibly changes over time, both candidate families might be inadequate. A good analysis would inspect residual patterns, distributions and sensitivity to alternatives before recommending equipment replacement intervals.

MODULE 20 / 32

20. Censoring and the risk of informative withdrawal

Right censoring is not the same as a missing value that can be filled with an average. A unit withdrawn at 700 hours while still functioning contributes the statement \(T>700\). Under noninformative censoring conditional on modeled predictors, the survivor contribution correctly represents its information. If withdrawal occurs because technicians notice signs of imminent failure, then the censoring mechanism itself depends on the event process and simple independent-censoring likelihoods can be biased.

Administrative study-end censoring is often easier to justify than health-based or condition-based withdrawal, but even administrative patterns require investigation when calendar entry is staggered or high-load equipment is followed for systematically shorter periods. An analyst should record why observation ends, not merely encode a 0. Censoring distribution, entry rules, and comparability of groups are substantive study-design issues.

AFT regression also requires care with delayed entry and left truncation. If devices join observation only after they have already survived a qualification period, the likelihood must condition on surviving to the entry time. Otherwise estimates may overstate longevity. The current worked example assumes everyone enters at the same conceptual time zero; it does not model left truncation or competing failure modes. These boundaries are deliberate and must remain visible.

Finally, a covariate measured after time zero must not simply be treated as if it were known at baseline. Defining “ever received maintenance” using future information can generate immortal-time bias. Handling time-dependent interventions, recurrent failure events and multi-state processes needs a study design and likelihood beyond this introductory baseline-covariate AFT example.

MODULE 21 / 32

21. The meaning of covariate changes: units, groups and interactions

Suppose the only predictor is high load coded 0 and 1. The fitted Time Ratio \(e^{\beta_1}\) compares two specific groups. If the coding were reversed so 1 denoted standard and 0 high load, the new coefficient would be \(-\beta_1\), and the new ratio would be its reciprocal. Predictions for the same named operating conditions would remain unchanged after correctly reparameterizing the intercept. Checking group labels prevents a surprisingly common sign error.

For a continuous predictor with coefficient \(\beta_T\) per degree, the ratio for five degrees is \(e^{5\beta_T}\). Reexpressing temperature in ten-degree units multiplies its coefficient by ten, but does not alter a correctly matched physical contrast. Centering temperature also changes the intercept's reference temperature; it does not change the same-condition predictions. Report units and reference conditions directly with every ratio.

An interaction term allows the time ratio for one predictor to depend on another. If \(\log T=\beta_0+\beta_1 x+\beta_2 z+\beta_3 xz+\sigma\varepsilon\), then the time ratio for \(x\) changing from 0 to 1 at fixed \(z\) is \(e^{\beta_1+\beta_3 z}\), not simply \(e^{\beta_1}\). Interactions require adequate data support; extrapolating a complex interaction in a tiny censored sample is unreliable.

AFT need not mean that the raw observed duration depends linearly on predictors. It asserts linearity for the location of log-time, conditional on the chosen covariate representation. Nonlinear temperature terms or splines can be added, but they should be justified by physical reasoning, data support and transparent interpretation. A single straight line on the log-time scale is a model assumption, not an automatically discovered law.

MODULE 22 / 32

22. A reproducible estimation algorithm without hidden steps

The computational goal is to maximize the right-censored likelihood from all twelve observations. Start by retaining four vectors: positive observed durations \(y_i\), indicators \(\delta_i\), a column of ones for the intercept, and the binary load indicator. Before optimization, verify that the values of \(\delta_i\) are strictly 0 or 1, that no duration is zero, and that the group labels match the original table. These checks prevent a mathematically valid program from analyzing the wrong data.

For Lognormal AFT, choose parameters \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\rho\) with \(\sigma=e^\rho\). For each row compute \(\mu_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\), standardized \(z_i=(\log y_i-\mu_i)/\sigma\), log-density if \(\delta_i=1\), and log-survival otherwise. Sum the contributions, negate the result, and minimize the negative log-likelihood using a numerical optimizer. Computing \(\log[1-\Phi(z)]\) using a stable Normal log-survival routine avoids catastrophic subtraction when \(\Phi(z)\) is close to one.

For Weibull AFT, use the compact log-likelihood from Module 13 with \(\sigma=e^\rho\) and \(k=e^{-\rho}\). Evaluate it at different starting values and verify that they reach the same objective within numerical precision. Recalculate predictions from returned coefficients, including the Time Ratio and two medians, and independently check their ratio. A successful optimizer message alone does not verify a statistical model.

Reproducibility check. The provided source table and equations are sufficient to reproduce the rounded estimates with a suitable numerical optimizer. A clean execution needs the exact formula, optimization convention and event indicator coding—not just a final coefficient table. Software verification is not a substitute for explaining each term of the likelihood.

MODULE 23 / 32

23. Independent numerical checks of the fitted likelihood

A likelihood formula is easier to trust when several individual terms can be reproduced without the optimizer. Take the fitted Lognormal parameters rounded to \(\beta_0=2.473683\), \(\beta_1=-0.438057\), \(\sigma=0.547939\). For standard operation at \(y=7\) with censoring, compute \(z=(\log7-2.473683)/0.547939\). Using \(\log7\approx1.945910\), the numerator is \(-0.527773\), hence \(z\approx-0.9632\). A standard Normal calculation gives \(\Phi(-0.9632)\approx0.1677\), so Survival is approximately \(0.8323\), with log-survival near \(-0.1835\). This is a probability contribution, not a failure density.

For high load at \(y=3\) with a failure, first calculate \(\mu_1=2.473683-0.438057=2.035626\). Then \(z=(\log3-2.035626)/0.547939\approx-1.710\). The event log-density is \(-\log3-\log0.547939-\frac12\log(2\pi)-\frac12z^2\). Substituting approximate values produces a negative log-density; it can be checked by direct evaluation of the Lognormal PDF. Unlike the censored log-survival, this density includes \(-\log y\) and the Normal quadratic term.

A density can exceed one numerically when the time unit is sufficiently small, because a density is not itself a probability; it has inverse-time units. Therefore an individual log-density need not behave like the logarithm of a probability. In contrast, every censoring contribution \(\log S\) is nonpositive. This distinction helps diagnose an implementation that incorrectly uses PDF values as probabilities of survival. Repeating calculations at two rows with contrasting event indicators is a stronger check than merely reproducing the final maximized log-likelihood.

MODULE 24 / 32

24. What would disprove a shared Time Ratio?

AFT imposes proportionality among corresponding quantile times. Suppose an actual reliability study estimated the standard group's 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles as 500, 900 and 1,500 hours, and the high-load group's as 400, 630 and 750 hours. The empirical ratios would be \(400/500=0.80\), \(630/900=0.70\), and \(750/1500=0.50\). They decrease markedly with percentile. If the estimates were sufficiently precise and not distorted by heavy censoring, a single Time Ratio would describe these contrasts poorly.

This hypothetical comparison is a diagnostic thought experiment, not another empirical dataset and not a substitute for valid censoring-aware quantile estimation. A percentile may be unidentified if follow-up ends before the Kaplan–Meier curve crosses the desired level. For example, if fewer than 50% of events are observed and the survival curve never reaches 0.50, the nonparametric median may be unestimable without assumptions. A parametric model can still output a median by extrapolation, but that number is then heavily model-dependent.

Plots of estimated conditional quantiles against each other, or quantile-residual diagnostics, can reveal lack of common scaling. The fitting distribution also matters: Weibull with constant shape and Lognormal with constant log-scale produce different tail and hazard behavior even while both obey AFT. Apparent differences in empirical quantile ratios should be evaluated with uncertainty, particularly when the at-risk count drops late in follow-up.

A responsible report states the time window over which the model was checked, the numbers at risk and the extent of censored information. It does not label an assumption violated solely because one noisy late percentile differs. Nor does a nonsignificant formal test prove that shared time scaling is exactly true. Diagnostics are evidence about adequacy under the available design, not absolute verdicts.

MODULE 25 / 32

25. Why changing the time unit cannot change the Time Ratio

Suppose the same duration is expressed in hours \(T_h\) and hundreds of hours \(T_c=T_h/100\). Taking logarithms yields \(\log T_h=\log T_c+\log100\). Hence a Lognormal AFT fitted using hours has the same covariate slopes and error scale as a fit using hundred-hours, but its intercept differs by \(\log100\approx4.60517\). This follows because multiplying all times by a fixed positive constant is a translation on the logarithmic scale.

For example, if the standard-group fitted Lognormal median is 11.8661 hundred-hours, it becomes 1,186.61 hours. On the log scale the original intercept \(2.473683\) becomes \(2.473683+4.605170\approx7.078853\). Exponentiating gives approximately 1,186.61. The high-load coefficient remains \(-0.438057\), so its Time Ratio remains \(e^{-0.438057}\approx0.645289\). A different value would signal a coding, unit conversion or model-definition error.

Weibull AFT has the same property: multiplying time by 100 multiplies the scale \(\eta\) by 100 and adds \(\log100\) to \(\beta_0\), but leaves shape \(k\) and covariate Time Ratios unchanged. Hazard rates, on the other hand, have inverse-time units and must be converted: a rate per hundred-hours is one hundred times a rate per hour when referring to the same physical failure process. Survival probabilities at corresponding physical times do not change.

This unit-invariance check is inexpensive and surprisingly valuable. If the fitted slope or its physical interpretation changes after reexpressing the exact same recorded lifetimes in hours, either the software input, formula, or reported comparison is inconsistent. Do not confuse changes in numerical density values under a new unit with changes in probability; densities and hazards rescale because their units rescale.

MODULE 26 / 32

26. Conditional versus marginal prediction, and who is being compared

With more than one covariate, a Time Ratio is conditional on all remaining predictors in the AFT equation. Suppose high load is correlated with older equipment. The unadjusted difference between standard and high-load records then mixes the association with load and the association with age. Fitting an adjusted AFT model can separate those covariates statistically if the model is identified and data support the comparisons. It cannot by itself remove unmeasured confounding or prove a causal load effect.

To report a standardized comparison across a population, one can predict the survival distribution for each individual under two specified covariate profiles and average predicted probabilities over a target covariate distribution. This is a marginal estimand and need not equal a simple Time Ratio interpretation derived from one conditional coefficient after nonlinear averaging. Moreover, averaging predicted medians is not the same as computing the median of the average survival distribution; quantiles and averaging generally do not commute.

An operational recommendation should define whether the objective is a conditional comparison for a representative machine, a portfolio average, or a replacement threshold for a particular subgroup. Also state the decision loss: a 10th-percentile replacement time may be conservative but expensive, while a median-based schedule risks many failures before replacement. AFT parameters help quantify the trade-off; they do not decide what level of risk is acceptable.

Prediction intervals for a future individual's event time are wider and conceptually different from confidence intervals for a population median or a coefficient. The former incorporate individual outcome variability as well as parameter uncertainty; the latter address uncertainty about the estimated conditional distribution. Conflating them leads to misleading guarantees of device lifetime.

MODULE 27 / 32

27. Final diagnostic checklist before applying an AFT model

A substantive review asks first whether the time origin and event are defined in terms an engineer, physician or business analyst could reproduce. Second, are there duration values that are impossible, duplicated units with incompatible origins, or indications of delayed entry? Third, were censored records properly retained, and can one defend censoring independence conditional on the included predictors? Fourth, is the distributional family scientifically plausible within the observed time range?

Next examine whether every intended predictor was measured before or at the stated baseline. If a variable is determined later by events during follow-up, using it as a baseline predictor risks leakage and immortal-time bias. Check the covariate range and positivity: estimating a load effect outside the range of observed loading is extrapolation. Check that coding reflects the stated reference; a reversal of a binary label reverses the coefficient and inverts the Time Ratio. Then verify likelihood optimization, numerical stability and uncertainty estimation.

Finally interpret rather than merely repeat software coefficients. State a Time Ratio alongside its comparison direction, model family, event-time unit and confidence interval when estimated. Show at least one survival curve and an interpretable time quantile, and explain their differences. Contrast with a PH analysis only after clarifying that HR and TR answer different questions; use the Weibull identity only when its shared-shape assumption and parameter convention are justified.

This checklist does not replace data collection, model diagnostics or scientific review. It is intended to prevent the familiar failure mode in which a technically executed regression is interpreted as an exact physical prediction. A small didactic dataset can demonstrate the arithmetic; validation of actual equipment maintenance decisions requires sufficient representative observations, recorded censoring reasons, model checks and independent performance assessment.

29. Worked solutions I: Time Ratios and Lognormal predictions

Solution 1. Exponentiate the coefficient: \(e^{-0.25}\approx0.778801\). Therefore comparable modeled event-time quantiles are about 77.88% of reference quantiles, or \(100(0.778801-1)\approx-22.12\%\) shorter. Multiply the reference median: \(900(0.778801)\approx700.92\) hours. As a check, \(700.92/900\approx0.7788\). This uses a ratio of times at the same percentile; it does not state the chance of failure by 900 hours, which would require a fitted survival distribution.

Solution 2. If A/B = 0.80, then B/A = \(1/0.80=1.25\). Relative to B, A is \((0.80-1)\times100=-20\%\) shorter. Relative to A, B is \((1.25-1)\times100=+25\%\) longer. The two verbal statements do not use identical percentage values because the denominators differ. Always state which condition is the reference.

Solution 3, medians. Lognormal median is \(e^\mu\), so reference median \(e^{\log10}=10\) hundred-hours = 1,000 hours. Exposed median \(e^{\log10-0.3}=10e^{-0.3}\approx7.40818\) hundred-hours = 740.818 hours. Verify \(7.40818/10=e^{-0.3}\approx0.740818\). The interpretation is a 25.9% decrease in modeled median duration for exposure, conditional on the specified AFT family.

Solution 3, upper percentile. The 90th percentile is \(10\exp(0.4\times1.28155)=10e^{0.51262}\approx16.697\) hundred-hours, or approximately 1,669.7 hours. Check: \([\log(16.697)-\log10]/0.4\approx1.28155\), so \(\Phi(z)\approx0.90\). Thus about 90% of modeled failures happen by that time; only about 10% remain functioning past it. The result is conditional on the assumed Lognormal spread.

These solutions illustrate two independent safeguards: calculate on the log-time scale only where the model defines the linear relation, then exponentiate to return to positive time; and verify a Time Ratio by independently dividing two predicted quantiles. Doing only one of these steps makes sign and unit errors easier to miss.

MODULE 30 / 32

30. Worked solutions II: censoring, Weibull and uncertainty

Solution 4. Under the group-specific Exponential model, a failure at \(y\) contributes \(\lambda_x e^{-\lambda_x y}\), and a censored record contributes \(e^{-\lambda_x y}\). Thus the four contributions are \(\lambda_0 e^{-2\lambda_0}\), \(e^{-5\lambda_0}\), \(\lambda_1 e^{-3\lambda_1}\), and \(e^{-6\lambda_1}\). Multiply and collect exponents: \(L(\lambda_0,\lambda_1)=\lambda_0\lambda_1 e^{-7\lambda_0-9\lambda_1}\). The two censored rows contribute five and six units of exposure; the failed rows add two and three. Thus standard exposure totals 7 and high-load exposure totals 9. There is exactly one event in each group. The leading rate factors count failures, not follow-up records.

Solution 5. For Weibull shape \(k=2\) and Time Ratio 0.75, \(\mathrm{HR}=\mathrm{TR}^{-k}=0.75^{-2}=1/0.5625=1.777778\). Simply using \(1/0.75=1.3333\) would silently impose \(k=1\), the Exponential case. The correct ratio is about 1.78 at every time only under the declared common-shape Weibull AFT/PH equivalence. Do not generalize this result to Lognormal errors.

Solution 6. The 95% Wald margin on the log-time scale is \(1.96(0.10)=0.196\). Hence the coefficient interval is \([-0.30-0.196,-0.30+0.196]=[-0.496,-0.104]\). Exponentiation gives \([e^{-0.496},e^{-0.104}]\approx[0.609,0.901]\). It excludes 1 because both endpoints are below one. These are approximate sampling statements conditional on a correctly fitted model; they are not a probability that an individual unit fails earlier.

Solution 6, check and interpretation. The estimated Time Ratio itself is \(e^{-0.30}\approx0.7408\), inside the interval. The interval is not symmetric around 0.7408; exponentiation transforms additive uncertainty into multiplicative uncertainty. When samples are small or censoring is extensive, use more careful uncertainty checks rather than assuming the normal approximation is reliable.

MODULE 31 / 32

31. Worked solution III: an applied critique and a transferable decision

Solution 7, error one: censored time is not failure time. OLS on \(\log Y\) treats censored observations as fully observed lifetimes even though the actual \(T\) is unknown. This changes the modeled response and can bias the regression. Correction: construct a likelihood with density contributions for observed failures and survival contributions for censored follow-up, subject to defensible independent-censoring assumptions.

Error two: selecting only failures is not a valid repair. Removing censored modules loses legitimate survival information and selects the subset that failed early enough to be observed. Correction: retain all eligible observations and document the censoring mechanism. Error three: a regression association is not automatically causal. High load may be associated with differences in equipment age, operator behavior, thermal stress or maintenance policy. Correction: distinguish an adjusted conditional prediction from a causal effect and use a design appropriate to a causal claim.

Error four: the 35% statement might be misinterpreted. A Time Ratio near 0.65 describes approximately 35% shorter modeled comparable time quantiles, not a 35-point reduction in survival or an instantaneous hazard contrast. Correction: state Time Ratio, reference group, percentile meaning, model family and units. Error five: distributional validity is untested. A Lognormal or Weibull fit must be assessed against the observed event/censoring pattern. Correction: compare survival curves, examine sensitivity to plausible families and quantify coefficient/prediction uncertainty.

Error six: long-range prediction is not a guarantee. Our toy records end by 1,800 hours, so a 20,000-hour statement extrapolates far beyond observed events. Parametric tails can disagree drastically while fitting within the observed range. Correction: label such estimates as extrapolations, show alternatives, obtain longer follow-up, and avoid a guarantee. Error seven: time origin and selection can compromise comparisons. Verify that standard and high-load groups have the same origin, event definition and enrollment criteria, and investigate delayed entry or withdrawal related to health.

A defensible workflow begins with the full dataset and an explicit estimand: the time-ratio association between predefined operating conditions. It documents observation rules, fits at least one justified parametric AFT family using censoring-aware likelihood, verifies derived medians, compares alternatives without overclaiming, and reports uncertainty and limitations. This procedure transfers to customer retention, time to relapse, maintenance intervals and other duration outcomes only after carefully redefining the event, clock and censoring process.

MODULE 32 / 32

32. A final synthesis: what you should be able to explain

An AFT regression is a model of event-time scaling. It uses a log-time equation and a specified error distribution to turn linear predictor differences into dimensionless Time Ratios. Every predicted time percentile changes by the same multiplicative factor for a fixed covariate contrast under the shared-scale specification. This is a property of the model, not a guarantee that data support it for every population or operating regime.

The Lognormal AFT model uses a Normal error for log-time and provides \(S(t\mid x)=1-\Phi((\log t-\mu(x))/\sigma)\). The Weibull AFT model uses the declared minimum extreme-value error and yields \(S(t\mid x)=\exp[-(t/e^{\mu(x)})^k]\), with \(k=1/\sigma\). Both allow likelihood estimation from failures and right-censored records. Neither can be correctly fitted using ordinary regression that ignores which records are censored.

Remember the key conceptual boundary: Time Ratio and Hazard Ratio are different quantities. Under our specific Weibull parameterization, \(\mathrm{HR}=\mathrm{TR}^{-k}\). For Lognormal AFT there is generally no constant HR, so neither reciprocal nor power shortcuts can be used without a valid derivation. When communicating findings, use exact units, reference profiles, uncertainty and model limitations.

Before declaring that you understand AFT, check whether you can derive the survival scaling identity from \(T_x=aT_0\), reproduce a censored contribution without treating a survivor as a failure, compute a median ratio from \(\beta\), and explain why 20,000-hour predictions are not verified by an 1,800-hour dataset. If any step is unclear, return to its worked example and recompute it without looking at the result. This self-assessment is a study aid, not an actual test with students or a certification of industrial reliability.
