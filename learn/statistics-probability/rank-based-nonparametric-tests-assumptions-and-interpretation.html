1. Why rank-based inference matters

A hospital can measure waiting times, a transit authority can measure delayed minutes, and a digital service can record how many seconds a user needs to finish a task. In all three settings, measurements may be strongly right-skewed: a few very long delays coexist with many short ones. Calculating an average remains legitimate, but a small sample with extreme observations may make a mean-based normal model difficult to defend. Rank-based tests offer another way to compare groups, provided we understand the design and the hypotheses. They are not a universal replacement for a t test or analysis of variance (ANOVA), and being called nonparametric does not mean that they require no assumptions.

Our guiding question is specific: when can the order of observed values provide convincing evidence about populations? A rank is a position in the sorted combined observations, not the original number of minutes. By using positions, some tests become insensitive to the magnitude of a single extreme measurement. That robustness is valuable, but information about distances between observations is lost. Two samples separated by one minute and two samples separated by one hundred minutes can generate the same ranks. Statistical significance therefore does not tell us whether a difference is economically, clinically, or operationally meaningful.

Three designs require three distinct tests. For two independent groups we develop Mann–Whitney U (also called the Wilcoxon rank-sum test). For before-and-after measurements on the same subjects we use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, ranking absolute paired differences. For three or more independent groups we use Kruskal–Wallis H. We will reproduce every reference calculation with complete small synthetic datasets, keep the measurement units visible, and distinguish what the data show from what a model allows us to infer. The guide closes with a selection decision, transfer questions, complete solutions, and limitations that matter in real studies.

2. Prerequisites, notation and measurable objectives

The official learning sequence expects familiarity with sampling distributions, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and one-way ANOVA from Guides 38, 42, 43 and 47. We explain the additional material needed here. A population is the full process of interest, such as all comparable future service requests; a sample is what was observed. A null hypothesis, H₀, is the reference claim whose consequences generate a sampling distribution. An alternative hypothesis, H₁, expresses an allowed departure. A p-value is the probability, under a specified null model, of a statistic at least as extreme as observed. It is not the probability H₀ is true or that results arose “only by chance.”

Write n_A and n_B for the two sample sizes; N for the total number of observations; k for the number of independent groups; R for a sum of ranks; U for the Mann–Whitney statistic; W⁺ and W⁻ for the positive and negative signed-rank sums; and H for the Kruskal–Wallis statistic. α is the preselected significance level, illustrated as 0.05. Minutes and seconds remain units of the raw responses and their differences. Ranks, U, W and H are dimensionless because they count positions or functions of positions. A test statistic is not itself a time difference.

By the end you should be able to rank raw values including ties, calculate U in two independent ways, enumerate an exact reference distribution for a small dataset, compute a signed-rank statistic using paired differences, compute and correct Kruskal–Wallis H, and interpret each result without substituting “median” for “distribution.” A separate objective is diagnostic: you should reject a proposed test when the observations are not independent or when the pairs have been lost. Finally, you will explain why a non-significant p-value is not proof that groups are equivalent, and why an effect size with understandable units should accompany inferential evidence.

3. Start with the study design, not with the histogram

Suppose a service manager measures handling time in minutes. Scenario I randomly selects five requests processed under protocol A and five different requests under protocol B. The units are requests, and no request belongs to both samples. This is a two-independent-samples design. Scenario II measures six agents before and after training, identifying the same agent twice; there are six independent pairs, not twelve independent observations. Scenario III samples five separate requests from each of three staffing plans. These are three independent groups and call for an omnibus analysis before asserting specific pairwise conclusions.

It may be tempting to look at a skewed histogram and decide on a nonparametric test immediately. But a histogram cannot tell us whether observations are independent, whether one customer contributed several requests, or whether the assignment mechanism created confounding. If ten responses come from two agents, the effective independent information is not necessarily ten units. Repeated measures need a dependence-aware design, such as paired analyses, clustered methods or mixed models, depending on the question. No transformation of raw times into ranks repairs a flawed unit of analysis.

Worked design decision: if you compare an employee's waiting time before and after a new routing tool, use the employee identifier to construct each difference. If you instead draw unrelated workers in each period, you cannot invent pairs by sorting both lists. And if you compare hospitals with very different case mixes, a rank test does not balance severity or remove confounding. Design-based causal claims would additionally require defensible assignment or adjustment; a small p-value from an observational comparison is not a causal estimate.

4. What ranks preserve, what they discard, and why ties matter

Sort the six measurements 4, 8, 6, 6, 12, 2 minutes into 2, 4, 6, 6, 8, 12. Their ranks would be 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 if no values were repeated, but the two measurements equal to 6 must receive the average of positions 3 and 4, namely (3+4)/2=3.5 each. The resulting ranked sequence is 1, 2, 3.5, 3.5, 5, 6. In original order the ranks are 2, 5, 3.5, 3.5, 6, 1. Check: both raw and ranked records contain six values and the ranks sum to 21=6×7/2. This invariant catches many transcription mistakes.

A tie is equality in the recorded variable, not an excuse to break equality arbitrarily. Rounding response times to whole minutes may create ties even when underlying continuous times would differ. Midranks are a principled way to keep equal measurements equal. Yet tied observations alter the exact null distribution; blindly using a no-ties probability table can produce an incorrect p-value. We will calculate tie corrections explicitly where relevant. Missing values are different from ties: excluding them can alter the question if missingness depends on performance.

Ranking is invariant to a strictly increasing change of scale. Converting minutes to seconds by multiplying by 60, or applying a strictly increasing logarithm to positive times, leaves the order unchanged. It is not invariant to arbitrary transformations: squaring negative numbers can reorder them, and coarse rounding can manufacture ties. Consequently, rank methods answer order-related questions. They do not magically estimate a clinically meaningful difference in minutes, which must be reported separately using appropriate summaries and intervals.

Every number in this guide is synthetic, constructed for instruction, not an observation from a real hospital, company, patient or employee. Dataset I compares independent protocol A response times A=[3,4,6,7,9] minutes with independent protocol B times B=[10,11,12,14,15] minutes. There are n_A=n_B=5 requests, and lower is faster. The selection mechanism is assumed to make independent, representative observations within each group for the illustration; random assignment of protocols was not performed. The scientific target is whether the entire response-time distributions are the same, or whether one tends to be faster.

Dataset II follows six independent service agents. Their before times in minutes are [14,17,20,23,26,29]; after training they are [13,15,17,19,21,23], respectively. The six differences defined as before minus after are [1,2,3,4,5,6]. Each positive difference represents a reduction in handling time. The pairs are independent of one another for the exercise, and repeated observations within each pair are deliberately dependent. No causal conclusion about training is justified without ruling out practice, changing workload or other time effects.

Dataset III compares three independent scheduling configurations using five observations each: C₁=[2,3,4,5,6], C₂=[7,8,9,10,11], C₃=[12,13,14,15,16] minutes. N=15 and k=3. These complete datasets are small enough to rank on paper. They are intentionally separated to make calculations transparent, not to demonstrate typical effect sizes or the power available in applied research. The Kruskal–Wallis chi-square reference is an asymptotic approximation and is particularly questionable at small group sizes; we will explicitly distinguish it from a permutation reference.

6. Mann–Whitney U: rank all independent observations together

For Dataset I pool A=[3,4,6,7,9] and B=[10,11,12,14,15]. The ordered ten values are 3(A),4(A),6(A),7(A),9(A),10(B),11(B),12(B),14(B),15(B). No values tie. The A ranks are 1,2,3,4,5, so the rank sum R_A=1+2+3+4+5=15. The B ranks are 6,7,8,9,10, so R_B=40. As a structural check, R_A+R_B=55=N(N+1)/2=10×11/2. If you obtain another total, the sorting, ranking, or labeling must be revisited before computing a test.

Why rank the two groups together? Ranking each group separately would give 1 through 5 in both groups and erase the between-group ordering that we want to test. Pooled ranks retain information about which sample tends to contain larger values. Yet they discard the numerical distances: these same ranks would appear if every time were multiplied by ten. We therefore describe the procedure as an ordering-based distribution comparison, rather than a direct estimate of minutes saved.

The null hypothesis in its basic randomization interpretation is that the observations in A and B are exchangeable between labels under equal distributions: F_A(t)=F_B(t) for every time t, where F denotes a cumulative distribution function. An alternative may be two-sided (distributions differ) or directional (A tends to have smaller values than B), but the direction should be chosen before examining the sample. With purely observational groups, this hypothesis is an inferential model requiring comparable sampling processes; it does not imply that protocol assignment is causal.

7. Derive U from a rank sum and independently from pairs

For the first group, define U_A=R_A−n_A(n_A+1)/2. Substitute R_A=15 and n_A=5: U_A=15−5×6/2=15−15=0. The complementary count is U_B=n_A n_B−U_A=5×5−0=25. The identity U_A+U_B=25 is another independent audit. Some traditional two-sided tables report min(U_A,U_B)=0, while scientific software often returns U for the first input. Reporting simply “U=0” without the order of groups can hide which comparison was made.

What is U counting? Compare every A value with every B value. There are 5×5=25 ordered cross-group pairs. Define U_A as the number of pairs in which an A value exceeds a B value, counting half a unit for each tie. Here no A value is greater than a B value, and no values tie, so U_A=0. The pair-counting definition confirms the rank formula without using it. In the reverse direction every B exceeds every A, giving U_B=25. Neither calculation uses the absolute number of minutes between a pair.

The null expectation is E[U_A]=n_A n_B/2=12.5 when the group labels are exchangeable and ties receive appropriate half-counting. This follows by symmetry: for each cross-group pair, each label is equally likely to carry the larger value under equal continuous distributions. An observation of 0 is far below 12.5, suggesting A tends to have lower service times. However, comparing 0 with its expected value alone does not yield a p-value; we still need the full null reference distribution or a defensible approximation.

8. Exact reference for U: enumerate what could have happened

Under the null of label exchangeability, keep the ten distinct times fixed and assign exactly five of the ten ranks to A. There are C(10,5)=10!/(5!5!)=252 equally likely assignments, where C denotes a binomial combination and ! means factorial. Of those assignments, only one places ranks 1,2,3,4,5 entirely in A, producing U_A=0. Only one places ranks 6,7,8,9,10 entirely in A, producing the equally extreme opposite U_A=25. Thus the two-sided exact p-value for the observed extreme is (1+1)/252=2/252≈0.0079365. This is not an approximate normal tail or an assumed probability of the null; it is a combinatorial tail under exchangeability.

At α=0.05 we reject the equal-distribution null, conditional on the design and reference assumptions. The evidence indicates an ordering difference for these populations; this conclusion should not be rewritten as proof that the population medians must differ without further shape conditions. In this specially separated sample, the sample medians are 6 and 12 minutes, but observing different medians is distinct from proving the only population difference is a location shift. We can report those sample medians descriptively while keeping the inferential target clear.

An asymptotic normal approximation may give a different p-value in such a small sample. Using the conventional continuity-corrected approximation can give approximately 0.01219 rather than 0.00794. Both are below 0.05 here, but that agreement is not guaranteed near a decision threshold. Document which reference generated the reported p-value. Exact procedures with ties require special care because the simple no-ties counting distribution changes; later we show a tied example explicitly.

9. Probability of superiority and interpretable effect size

The Mann–Whitney statistic has a useful pairwise interpretation. Let θ=P(X_A>X_B)+0.5P(X_A=X_B), where X_A and X_B are independent future observations drawn from the two populations. Under representative independent sampling, U_A/(n_A n_B) estimates θ. In Dataset I the estimate is 0/25=0. To express the desirable direction, P(X_A<X_B)+0.5P(X_A=X_B) is estimated by 25/25=1. In our sample, every cross-group comparison favored the lower time for A. That is a statement about the observed pairs, not certainty that every future A request will beat every future B request.

The rank-biserial form for the chosen orientation can be written r_rb=2θ−1. For U_A as the probability A exceeds B, r_rb=2×0−1=−1; negative values indicate A tends to be smaller. Authors differ about signs and the order of subtraction, so the direction should always accompany the number. A value at the extreme can be common in a tiny perfectly separated synthetic sample; it does not guarantee a stable extreme population effect. Sampling uncertainty, selection, and measurement resolution remain important.

The effect measure θ is unitless. To communicate operational value, also provide actual summaries: sample medians A=6 and B=12 minutes, an observed 6-minute median gap, and perhaps a robust interval for a time shift if its assumptions can be supported. A difference in sample medians is not necessarily the population parameter tested by U. A Hodges–Lehmann-type estimator based on cross-group differences can target a shift under an appropriate common-shape shift model; without that model its interpretation must be more cautious. Report assumptions and avoid treating any single effect size as a causal estimate.

10. Why a rank test does not automatically test medians

Consider population A that is constant at 0, and population B that equals −1 with probability 0.49 and +1 with probability 0.51. Both populations have median 0? No: B has median +1 because its cumulative probability below +1 is only 0.49. To avoid a misleading example, instead consider A taking −2, 0, +2 with probabilities 0.25, 0.5, 0.25 and B taking −100, 0, +1 with the same respective probabilities. Both have a median of 0 (each assigns at least half its probability to values on each side of zero), yet their distributions differ greatly in spread and asymmetry. A full-distribution rank comparison can respond to such differences without warranting the sentence “the median is different.”

What extra assumption permits a location interpretation? A common-shape shift model: F_B(t)=F_A(t−δ) for a fixed displacement δ, with the same shape and spread. In that restricted model, comparing distribution locations also compares their medians, assuming identifiable medians. Without a justified shift model, a statistically significant U indicates incompatibility with the equal-distribution null, not a theorem about which characteristic changed. It may reflect location, shape, scale, or combinations. This is why a plot of each group's raw distribution and summaries of tails belong beside the p-value.

The opposite mistake is interpreting a failure to reject as proof of equal medians or equal distributions. For example, a small sample with overlapping ranks may lack power to identify a real shift. An equivalence claim requires a prespecified tolerance, an appropriate confidence interval and an equivalence design—not merely p>0.05. Reading the question carefully prevents a false syllogism: “rank tests are nonparametric; therefore they measure medians.” The conclusion does not follow from the adjective.

11. Tied independent observations: midranks and variance correction

A more realistic rounded-time sample is A=[1,2,2,4] and B=[2,3,3,5] minutes. Pooling gives 1(A),2(A),2(A),2(B),3(B),3(B),4(A),5(B). The three 2s occupy positions 2,3,4, so each gets rank 3; the two 3s occupy positions 5,6, so each gets 5.5. The remaining ranks are 1,7,8. A receives ranks [1,3,3,7], giving R_A=14 and U_A=14−4×5/2=4; B receives ranks [3,5.5,5.5,8], giving R_B=22 and U_B=16−4=12. Check: R_A+R_B=36=8×9/2 and U_A+U_B=16.

For the asymptotic variance under label exchangeability, tied blocks of sizes t_j reduce Var(U) to (n_A n_B/12)[N+1−Σ(t_j³−t_j)/(N(N−1))]. Here N=8, n_A=n_B=4 and blocks have sizes t₁=3, t₂=2, so Σ(t³−t)=(27−3)+(8−2)=30. Substitute Var(U)=(16/12)[9−30/(8×7)]=(4/3)(9−0.535714)=11.285714, giving a standard deviation √11.285714≈3.3594. E(U)=8. A continuity-corrected standardized score from the lower U is z=(4−8+0.5)/3.3594≈−1.0418; a two-sided normal tail is about 0.2975.

This approximate p-value is not evidence at α=0.05. Crucially, an “exact” U formula designed for no ties does not automatically retain its claimed exact error rate on these midranks. A label-permutation test preserving the observed tie pattern may be used when its exchangeability assumption is defensible. Software can differ in its convention, continuity correction and treatment of ties, so a reproducible analysis should name the method and its parameters rather than only the test.

12. Wilcoxon signed-rank: the paired question is different

Dataset II records each of six agents before and after training. Define d_i=before_i−after_i in minutes, so a positive difference means the later response was faster. We chose this direction explicitly to avoid interpreting a negative test statistic backwards. The six pairs are (14,13), (17,15), (20,17), (23,19), (26,21), (29,23). Subtraction gives d=[1,2,3,4,5,6] minutes. The observational unit for inference is an agent's difference, not each separate clock reading.

The signed-rank test discards the level of each agent's baseline response time; it combines the magnitude ranking of each nonzero difference with its sign. This is more informative than merely counting positives and negatives, because a change of six minutes receives more weight than a change of one minute through its rank, not linearly through its size. The sign test, by contrast, ignores magnitude even at the ranking level. The paired t test would work with the arithmetic mean of the differences and require a different sampling model.

The usual signed-rank null is symmetry of the distribution of paired differences about zero, under independent sampled pairs and appropriate continuous/no-tie conditions for the standard exact reference. If the difference distribution is symmetric around an unknown center, the test can be used to assess whether that center is zero; under symmetry this center is also the median. Without symmetry, presenting it as a generic test of zero median or as proof of training benefit is too strong. We must judge the differences, not the separate before and after marginal histograms, for this assumption.

13. Construct the signed-rank table without skipping a step

Begin with the differences d=[+1,+2,+3,+4,+5,+6]. Compute absolute magnitudes |d|=[1,2,3,4,5,6]. Order the absolute values (already sorted here), assigning ranks 1,2,3,4,5,6. Reattach the signs of the original differences: all six signed ranks are positive. Therefore W⁺=1+2+3+4+5+6=21 and W⁻=0. The total is W⁺+W⁻=n(n+1)/2=6×7/2=21. Some programs report the smaller of W⁺ and W⁻ for the two-sided alternative, namely W=min(21,0)=0. Others return the positive sum for one-sided alternatives. A report should state the exact convention.

Why absolute differences before attaching signs? If we ranked signed differences themselves, large negative values could receive small ranks simply because they are negative. That would confuse direction with magnitude. The signed-rank design instead asks whether large and small differences are distributed symmetrically across positive and negative signs. Under the symmetric null, the signs behave like independent fair coin flips conditional on the observed nonzero magnitudes, when the standard exchangeability assumptions hold.

Notice that raw before times [14,17,20,23,26,29] have a large spread and each worker has a different baseline. These differences do not contaminate the calculation because the within-pair subtraction removes an agent's stable individual level. That is why preserving IDs is valuable. But the subtraction does not remove influences that change between periods, such as workload, a software update, seasonal demand or measurement changes. A paired calculation handles stable between-agent variation; it is not a causal design by itself.

14. Exact sign enumeration and the two-sided decision

There are n=6 distinct nonzero absolute differences. Under symmetric independent differences about zero, conditional on magnitudes, each of the 2⁶=64 assignments of plus and minus signs is equally likely. Exactly one assignment yields W⁺=21 (all positive), and exactly one yields W⁺=0 (all negative). These are the two extreme patterns in opposite directions. Consequently, the two-sided exact p-value for W=0 is 2/64=0.03125. At α=0.05 we reject the symmetric-zero null. This calculation is transparent because there are no ties, zeros, or uncertain sign conventions.

The observed mean reduction is (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6=21/6=3.5 minutes, and the sample median reduction is (3+4)/2=3.5 minutes. These descriptive numbers are in minutes, unlike W. The test itself does not test that the mean is 3.5, nor does it produce a confidence interval for that mean. A location effect can be estimated using a matched-pair Hodges–Lehmann approach based on Walsh averages, with corresponding intervals under the appropriate model. For six differences, there are 6×7/2=21 Walsh averages (d_i+d_j)/2 with i≤j; the center of these values estimates a pseudomedian, which agrees with the median for symmetric difference distributions.

At n=6, one-sided and two-sided hypotheses give different tails. If the direction “after is faster” was prespecified, the one-sided exact p-value for all positive differences is 1/64≈0.015625. Choosing that direction only after seeing six positive differences is not a valid prospective one-sided analysis. A small p-value does not tell us whether the operational change was worth its implementation cost, nor can a before-and-after observational design separate training from changes over time.

15. Zero differences, ties and the limit of an exact table

Real paired data frequently include an unchanged response: suppose an additional agent has d=0. A standard Wilcox zero convention removes zero differences before ranking, leaving our six nonzero differences and n=6 for the statistic. That is a modeling and computational choice, not proof that the unchanged agent is irrelevant. The Pratt convention includes zeros when assigning ranks but excludes their signed contributions. A zsplit convention splits ranks associated with zero differences between positive and negative sums. Because these methods produce different rank sums and reference distributions, the report must identify which was used. An all-zero sample does not support the usual signed-rank test.

Ties of absolute magnitudes also matter. Imagine d=[−2,+2,+4,−4]. The absolute values [2,2,4,4] get midranks [1.5,1.5,3.5,3.5]. Then W⁺=1.5+3.5=5 and W⁻=1.5+3.5=5, with total 10. The observed signs are balanced in ranked magnitude. An off-the-shelf exact distribution based on four distinct absolute ranks 1,2,3,4 is not the conditional reference for midranks [1.5,1.5,3.5,3.5]. With few paired observations, explicit sign permutation over the observed nonzero magnitudes is often clearer, provided symmetry/sign-exchangeability is credible.

Measurement rounding can generate a zero from a small real change or make two absolute changes appear equal. Record resolution and unit conversions therefore affect the analysis. Never describe the software's automatic selection of “exact” as guaranteed exact when zeros or ties are present. Specify the version, method, zero convention and whether p is based on enumeration, normal approximation or permutation. For a credible report, a plot of the paired differences—with zero marked—makes assumptions and exceptions easier to inspect than separate before/after averages.

16. When the sign test may be the more defensible question

A sign test counts how many paired differences are above and below zero; it discards their magnitudes altogether. For Dataset II, six of six changes are positive. Under an independent fair-sign null, the probability of all six positive is 1/64 and the two-sided exact p-value is 2/64=0.03125. This happens to equal the signed-rank p-value in this perfectly aligned extreme example. That coincidence is not general. With mixed signs, signed-rank distinguishes whether the larger absolute differences tend to share a direction, whereas the sign test treats a one-second increase and a one-hour increase equally as positive.

Why might the simpler sign test be preferable? The sign test can target a median-zero statement under appropriate independent observations and continuity conditions without assuming a symmetric difference distribution. Wilcoxon signed-rank draws additional power from the ranked magnitudes but needs stronger structure for its standard location interpretation. If differences are visibly strongly asymmetric, choosing signed-rank just because it is well known can misstate the hypothesis. Conversely, discarding magnitude information may cost power when symmetry is plausible. This is an example of a real methodological tradeoff rather than a universal winner.

Imagine five modest improvements of one minute and one deterioration of twenty minutes. The sign test would count five favorable signs versus one unfavorable sign; signed-rank would give the deterioration the largest absolute rank, influencing its test statistic. Their judgments need not match, and their target properties differ. For practical decisions, the large adverse change could be more important than either p-value. Display the original signed differences, include units, and ask whether the study is about a typical individual's direction of change or the distributional balance of magnitudes.

17. Kruskal–Wallis: an omnibus comparison of three independent groups

Dataset III contains three separate configurations: C₁=[2,3,4,5,6], C₂=[7,8,9,10,11], and C₃=[12,13,14,15,16] minutes. Each configuration has n_j=5, so N=15 and k=3. All fifteen responses are different, and the groups are assumed independently sampled. The Kruskal–Wallis null compares their population distributions: F₁(t)=F₂(t)=F₃(t) for every t, under the common-distribution model. Under an additional common-shape shift assumption, its evidence can be discussed in terms of locations or medians; without that assumption, it is not a universal three-median test.

The purpose is omnibus: detect evidence that at least one distribution differs from the others. A significant H cannot specify whether C₁ differs from C₂, C₁ from C₃, or C₂ from C₃, nor does it show the precise source of the difference. That is analogous to the global F test from one-way ANOVA, but the construction and reference assumptions differ. While ANOVA decomposes squared deviations from means, Kruskal–Wallis compares group rank sums. A highly skewed response may make the latter attractive, but data dependence, confounding and selective testing remain concerns.

The sample medians are 4, 9 and 14 minutes. They describe the center of the observed groups but are not sufficient by themselves to calculate H. To reproduce the statistic, we must pool and rank all fifteen observations, compute each group rank sum, substitute the sizes, and apply a reference distribution. We will follow that chain explicitly, rather than interpreting a displayed p-value as if the calculation were a black box.

18. Compute pooled group ranks and prove the total is consistent

The ascending responses are all observations from C₁, then all from C₂, then all from C₃. Thus C₁ receives ranks 1,2,3,4,5, whose sum is R₁=15. C₂ receives 6,7,8,9,10, sum R₂=40. C₃ receives 11,12,13,14,15, sum R₃=65. Verify R₁+R₂+R₃=15+40+65=120, equal to N(N+1)/2=15×16/2=120. Each group has five observations, so its average ranks are 15/5=3, 40/5=8 and 65/5=13. The average rank across all observations is (N+1)/2=8.

Under equal distributions and independent sampling, labels would tend to be spread across low and high ranks; each group's mean rank would fluctuate around 8. Here group 1 concentrates entirely below that midpoint, group 3 entirely above it, and group 2 is centered on it. The rank sums show which groups are descriptively high or low, but the global hypothesis still requires the appropriate sampling distribution before declaring the departures statistically significant.

This rank-table audit prevents an important calculation error: someone might rank within each group and obtain R₁=R₂=R₃=15. That would falsely erase the comparison. Kruskal–Wallis requires one pooled ranking across all groups. Another common error is to multiply a rank sum by the group size a second time. Each R_j already sums exactly n_j ranks; the formula divides its square by n_j once. If the data include ties, assign midranks across all groups before summing them, and apply the appropriate tie factor later.

19. Calculate H from first principles and check every intermediate value

For k independent groups without ties, use H=[12/(N(N+1))]Σ(R_j²/n_j)−3(N+1). Each piece has a purpose: R_j²/n_j measures how far each group's rank total contributes on the common scale; the multiplier normalizes the between-group dispersion; and 3(N+1) subtracts the center implied by the pooled ranking. Substitute N=15, k=3, n₁=n₂=n₃=5, and rank sums 15,40,65. The three terms are 15²/5=225/5=45, 40²/5=1600/5=320, and 65²/5=4225/5=845. Their sum is 45+320+845=1210.

The multiplier is 12/(15×16)=12/240=0.05. Therefore H=0.05×1210−3×16=60.5−48=12.5. Check the result by observing that H is nonnegative (apart from negligible floating-point noise) and that the group mean ranks are widely separated. H has no minutes unit. If all group average ranks coincided exactly at the pooled center, the statistic would instead be zero. This makes the formula an interpretable measure of rank separation rather than an unexplained computational ritual.

A common large-sample reference uses χ² with k−1=2 degrees of freedom under equal distributions, independent observations and regularity conditions. For df=2, the survival probability at H is exp(−H/2); hence p≈exp(−12.5/2)=exp(−6.25)≈0.00193045. At α=0.05, this asymptotic reference rejects the global equal-distribution null. Yet with only five observations per group we should explain that χ² is an approximation; an exact label-permutation reference is conceptually available and may differ in finite samples. We do not silently call 0.00193 “exact.”

20. Kruskal–Wallis with ties: derive the correction instead of hiding it

Suppose response times are recorded as integer minutes and the pooled dataset contains tied values. In the Kruskal–Wallis formula, midranks reduce the variability possible under the null. Let each tie block contain t_l repeated observations. Define C=1−[Σ(t_l³−t_l)]/(N³−N). Divide the uncorrected H by C to obtain H_corrected=H_raw/C, provided C>0. This adjusts the statistic for the reduction in rank variance caused by equal observations. The correction is not a license to manufacture random rank orders within tied blocks.

To see the arithmetic, imagine N=9 values with one block of three equal measurements and one block of two equal measurements, all other measurements distinct. The tie sum is (3³−3)+(2³−2)=24+6=30. The denominator is 9³−9=729−9=720, so C=1−30/720=1−0.0416667≈0.9583333. If the rank-sum portion produced H_raw=5.75, the tie-corrected result would be 5.75/0.9583333=6. This is a formula illustration: the chosen H_raw is not claimed to arise from a separately supplied nine-value dataset. Distinguishing illustrative arithmetic from a reproducible dataset prevents false provenance.

What if all observations have exactly the same recorded time? Then the single tie block has t=N, giving C=1−(N³−N)/(N³−N)=0; all ranks are identical and there is no rank separation to test. Dividing by zero would not produce a meaningful H. For heavily rounded data, ties may substantially affect power and approximation quality. An exact or permutation procedure should condition on the data structure and respect the study's exchangeability restrictions, not randomly shuffle patients or repeated time measurements as if all observations were independent.

21. What a significant omnibus result cannot identify

Our three-group H=12.5 and asymptotic p≈0.00193 supply evidence against the joint claim that all three population distributions are identical. They do not identify particular pairs with familywise control. It is descriptively true that C₁ has the smallest values and C₃ the largest in this constructed dataset, but inferential claims about C₁ versus C₂, C₁ versus C₃ and C₂ versus C₃ require separately specified comparisons and consideration of multiplicity. Inspecting the smallest raw p-value after a global rejection and reporting it as if it were the only planned test is not valid error accounting.

For example, with three groups there are C(3,2)=3 possible pairwise comparisons. If you choose to evaluate all three, define that family and use an appropriate procedure, such as Dunn-type comparisons with suitable adjustment, or permutation-based contrasts where justified. The exact choice depends on the inferential target, ties, dependence and error guarantee. Guide 46 explains familywise error rate (FWER) and false discovery rate (FDR); Guide 48 explains ANOVA-based post-hoc procedures. Neither Tukey's equal-variance mean-comparison guarantees nor an uncorrected series of independent Mann–Whitney tests should be automatically relabeled a Kruskal–Wallis post-hoc analysis.

A strong report should separate the global conclusion (“there is evidence of a difference somewhere in the distribution family”) from the descriptive ordering (“in this dataset C₁ times are below C₂ times”) and any adjusted pairwise inference actually performed. If a client needs to choose a service plan, statistical significance is only one component: reliability, cost, staffing, worst-case response, and measurement uncertainty must also be considered. No p-value chooses an operational policy on its own.

22. An effect size for multiple groups, and its limits

An often-reported Kruskal–Wallis rank-based effect size is ε²_H=(H−k+1)/(N−k), with interpretations and small-sample properties depending on its particular definition and use. For Dataset III, plug in H=12.5, k=3, N=15: ε²_H=(12.5−3+1)/(15−3)=10.5/12=0.875. This is a large numerical effect in the constructed sample, consistent with extreme separation of ranks. It is not a statement that 87.5% of the variance in minutes has a causal explanation; the calculation is on ranks and the expression is a sample estimator with limitations.

Why report an effect size when p is already small? The test statistic and p-value mix the apparent separation with sample size and reference assumptions. A well-described effect size helps compare magnitudes on a consistent inferential scale, but even a large rank-based value cannot replace raw time differences or uncertainty intervals. Here the three sample medians are 4, 9 and 14 minutes, and the adjacent observed median gaps are five minutes. That practical difference may or may not justify changing schedules: it depends on the associated costs and the operational threshold set before looking at attractive outcomes.

Some formulas for epsilon-squared or eta-squared based on H can give slightly different answers or negative estimates in weak samples. A negative estimate should not be interpreted as a negative amount of explained variation; depending on the chosen estimator, authors may truncate or explain it. Always name the exact formula. None of these quantities, by itself, identifies a causal mechanism, guarantees future performance, or determines which pairwise hypothesis survives adjustment. The effect size and its uncertainty should be judged against the actual decision problem.

23. Nonparametric does not mean assumption-free

A useful assumption checklist separates sampling, design, measurement, reference distribution, and interpretation. Sampling asks whether observations represent the populations named in the conclusion. Design asks whether the units are independent across groups, or are appropriately paired. Measurement asks whether values are ordered meaningfully and whether a tie is an actual equality or a rounding artifact. Reference distribution asks whether a small-sample exact enumeration, asymptotic normal or chi-square model, or restricted permutation is justified. Interpretation asks whether a conclusion concerns entire distributions, an ordering probability, a shift parameter, or some other feature.

For Mann–Whitney, independent observations within and between the two samples and exchangeability under the equal-distribution null support the conventional reference. Different shapes make a generic median-only conclusion unsafe. For signed-rank, paired differences must be independent across pairs, while the usual signed-rank location interpretation requires symmetry of the difference distribution. For Kruskal–Wallis, groups must be independent and the large-sample chi-square reference needs appropriate conditions and group sizes. The common rule of thumb to have at least five observations per group is not a universal mathematical guarantee; small or heavily tied samples can still need another calibration.

A convenient habit is to write one sentence identifying the null and one sentence identifying the conditions under which the chosen reference is valid before opening a statistical package. After computing, report the test, alternative direction, statistic, p-value, reference method, effect summary and limitations. If any assumption is not supported, that belongs in the conclusions rather than in an invisible footnote. Good practice is not to run a preliminary normality test and mechanically switch procedures at a p-value threshold: that data-dependent rule changes the behavior of the entire analysis.

24. Distributions, not just boxplot center lines

A boxplot emphasizes quartiles, median and possible outliers, but two populations can have equal medians while their tails differ. Imagine wait-time population A with most jobs clustered near ten minutes but rare very long delays, and B with a wider central spread but no extreme delays. Whether customers prefer A or B may depend on tail risk rather than the median. A single rank test may detect differences in relative ordering yet not explain their source; a non-significant test likewise does not certify equal tails. Complementary empirical cumulative distribution plots help visualize how frequently times fall below operational targets.

For Dataset I, every observed A time is below every observed B time. This complete separation makes a rank-based result intuitive: each of 25 cross-group comparisons points in the same direction. In Dataset III, the median ordering also matches the full rank ordering. Do not generalize those unusually clean synthetic patterns to actual work settings, where distributions overlap, are censored by closing time, contain missing records, and may be measured on rounded clocks. A graphic should be introduced as an explanatory object, checked against the raw data, and interpreted alongside—not instead of—the numerical results.

A logarithmic axis can make right tails easier to see, but it does not change the ranks when applied to positive times, because log is strictly increasing. This provides a useful cross-check: the rank statistic should agree before and after an exact monotone transformation of the responses. Conversely, changing units to rounded whole minutes can introduce ties and legitimately alter the statistic's tie structure. A reader who understands this distinction can explain why apparently different graphs sometimes yield identical U values and why other preprocessing choices do not.

25. Exact, asymptotic and permutation p-values are different promises

An exact reference is constructed from a finite set of assignments under the stated null and design. In our two-group no-tie sample, C(10,5)=252 labelings permit direct enumeration. In the six-pair signed-rank example, 2⁶=64 sign assignments permit direct enumeration. An asymptotic reference uses a distribution—often normal for U or signed-rank, chi-square for H—that approximates the statistic when sample sizes and regularity conditions support it. A permutation reference computes or estimates the distribution induced by allowable rearrangements of labels or signs. “Exact” describes calibration conditional on assumptions, not that the model or scientific conclusion is unquestionably true.

For Mann–Whitney, the untied variance under H₀ is n_A n_B(N+1)/12. With n_A=n_B=5 and N=10, this equals 25×11/12≈22.9167 and the standard deviation is √22.9167≈4.7871. The mean is 12.5. A continuity-corrected lower-tail z for U_A=0 is approximately (0−12.5+0.5)/4.7871≈−2.506, yielding the approximate two-sided p≈0.01219. The exact result was 0.00794. Neither value is an arithmetic mistake; they arise from different reference calibrations.

A permutation test is justified only by the exchangeability permitted by the design and null. Randomly swapping treatment labels across independent randomized subjects may be legitimate. Shuffling “before” values among unrelated people destroys pairing; shuffling individual observations inside clusters breaks the dependence structure. A restricted sign flip for paired differences embodies a different symmetry null from arbitrary label exchange. The reference distribution must follow from the scientific process rather than from what the software makes easy to shuffle. When sample size grows, randomly sampled permutations can estimate a tail probability but introduce Monte Carlo uncertainty that should be reported.

26. Power, sample size, and what a non-rejection means

The power of a test is the probability of rejecting a false null under a specified alternative and sampling plan. Power depends on sample size, degree of distributional separation, shape, ties, variability, significance level and whether the alternative is one-sided or two-sided. Rank tests can perform well under heavy-tailed responses, but may lose power relative to well-specified mean-based methods under favorable parametric conditions. The choice should follow the scientific estimand, measurement process and defensibility of assumptions—not a slogan that one family of tests is always safer.

Our synthetic samples were selected to produce easily inspected rank patterns. That selection makes them teaching devices, not evidence of expected power in real service centers. In Dataset I, perfect separation is observable even with five values per group. A different sample from overlapping populations could produce many interleaved ranks and a far less extreme U. Failure to reject might then reflect an effect too small to detect, noisy data, insufficient observations, or a genuinely absent difference. It cannot distinguish those possibilities without design information and uncertainty analysis.

A sample-size plan should state the target property—such as a probability-of-superiority difference regarded as operationally important—a plausible distribution or simulation scenario, the number of groups, expected tie rate, and the chosen error level. If estimating a reduction in minutes is the main priority, a design built only around the significance of U may not answer the management question. Provide uncertainty intervals for time-scale effects or other estimands when possible. If multiple endpoints, departments or subgroups are tested, account for the family of tests rather than reporting only the smallest p-value.

27. A decision workflow that survives a different dataset

Start by naming the variable and unit, target population, independent experimental or sampling unit, and estimand. Next, ask whether there are exactly two independent groups, matched pairs, or three or more independent groups. For two independent groups with an equal-distribution or relative-ordering null, consider Mann–Whitney while checking exchangeability, dependence and ties. For independent pairs with a symmetric-difference null, consider Wilcoxon signed-rank, carefully computing differences and zero conventions. For k≥3 independent groups with a global equal-distribution null, consider Kruskal–Wallis, documenting tie correction and small-sample reference limitations.

Then inspect raw data: show a dot plot or empirical distribution, identify missingness, record rounding, outliers and possible clustering. Establish alternative direction and multiplicity handling in advance. Choose exact, asymptotic or permutation calibration based on the null and design. Perform an independent calculation check: total rank sums N(N+1)/2; U_A+U_B=n_A n_B; W⁺+W⁻=n(n+1)/2 without zeros; and the separate terms of H. Finally, present a transparent conclusion specifying what differs (or is not established), for whom, and under which assumptions.

This is not an instruction to choose tests solely from the number of groups. If outcomes are censored, a survival analysis method may be needed. If observations are repeated beyond a simple pair, Friedman-type or longitudinal models may be better suited. If the question is confounding-adjusted prediction, regression or robust modeling is more suitable. If two independent groups have clearly different dispersions and the scientific target is the mean, Welch's t test may address the right question even when ranks appear attractive. The correct procedure is the one aligned with the actual target, not necessarily the one with the smallest p-value.

28. Reproducing without treating software as an oracle

Scientific computing is useful for checking arithmetic, not for replacing explanation. In a local Python environment with SciPy available, mannwhitneyu(A,B,alternative="two-sided",method="exact") can verify the untied independent example: statistic 0 and p≈0.0079365. wilcoxon(d,alternative="two-sided",method="exact") can verify the six distinct nonzero paired differences: the two-sided statistic is 0 and p=0.03125. kruskal(C1,C2,C3) can verify H=12.5 and the chi-square-based p≈0.00193045. Those commands require importing the relevant SciPy functions and defining all three datasets exactly as supplied above. This guide is an HTML study guide rather than an executable notebook, so it does not claim to execute a user's local Python installation.

The default options of a package are not a scientific design statement. An auto setting may choose a different reference from the one expected, depending on sample size, zeros, ties and library version. When tied data are present, a method advertised as “exact” under no ties may no longer be an exact calibration for the observations at hand. A reproducible method section reports test name, directional alternative, reference method, handling of ties and zero differences, the library version if used, and any permutation count or random seed. A screenshot of code output without that information is incomplete evidence.

A powerful independent check for the main dataset is to enumerate all 252 selections of five pooled ranks for A and compute U from each. Count the two extreme assignments to recover 2/252. For pairs, enumerate 64 possible sign assignments on fixed absolute ranks and count the extremes to recover 2/64. For Kruskal–Wallis, calculate three rank sums by hand and confirm the three R²/n contributions before comparing with a package. These separate paths reduce the chance that an incorrect input order or misunderstood default produces a convincing-looking but wrong result.

29. Eight recurrent interpretation errors and their repairs

Error 1: “Nonparametric means no assumptions.” Repair: state independent or paired sampling, null exchangeability and reference calibration. Error 2: “All three methods test medians.” Repair: distinguish equal distributions, relative ordering and signed-difference symmetry; a common-shape shift model can enable location statements. Error 3: “A p-value is the probability H₀ is true.” Repair: describe a tail probability conditional on H₀. Error 4: “p>0.05 proves equality.” Repair: report lack of rejection, uncertainty and potential power limitations.

Error 5: “Pairs are two independent samples.” Repair: compute person-level differences and rank their absolute values. Error 6: “Every exact method handles ties exactly.” Repair: verify the actual conditional reference for tied ranks or use a justified permutation. Error 7: “A significant global Kruskal–Wallis test identifies the best pair.” Repair: separate descriptive ranks from properly adjusted pairwise inference. Error 8: “An extreme rank effect means a large monetary improvement.” Repair: communicate original units, an uncertainty interval and an operational threshold.

A useful study exercise is to rewrite each error as a question. For example: which observations are allowed to exchange labels under the null? What distributional property is actually being tested? What would the same rank result mean if times were measured in milliseconds instead of minutes? Which differences could be produced by rounding? How many independent units were sampled? These questions often detect errors that a calculator cannot. A strong statistical analysis is not simply one with the correct arithmetic; it preserves the connection between sampling process, model, decision and limitations through the final paragraph.

30. Model results paragraph with explicit assumptions and uncertainty

For the independent comparison, a defensible results paragraph reads: “In a synthetic illustration with five independent observations per protocol, all 25 cross-group comparisons favored lower response times for A. The Mann–Whitney statistic for A relative to B was U_A=0. The untied two-sided exact label-exchangeability reference gave p=0.00794. Sample medians were 6 and 12 minutes. These findings are evidence against equal population distributions under the sampling assumptions; they do not establish causal improvement or a median-only difference.” This paragraph names the data status, design, statistic, reference, observed magnitude and limits.

For the paired example, a similarly careful report states that six agent-specific differences, defined as before minus after, were positive; W⁺=21, W⁻=0, and the exact two-sided sign-symmetry p was 0.03125. The observed median reduction was 3.5 minutes. The analysis rests on independent pairs and symmetry of the difference distribution for location interpretation; unmeasured changes over time prevent a causal training claim. For the three-group example, report H=12.5, df=2 and asymptotic p≈0.00193, while acknowledging that n_j=5 is small for a chi-square approximation and that no adjusted pairwise differences were established.

Do not report these three p-values together as an empirical study: they come from three separate purposely constructed teaching scenarios. In real research, a prespecified plan should also describe sampling exclusions, missing values, ties, the direction of each hypothesis, practical thresholds, multiple comparisons and reproducible software settings. Interpretation should remain proportional to the evidence. A persuasive academic explanation allows a reader to follow the calculations, reconstruct the assumptions and clearly state what is not known.

31. Worked exercise 1 — the hidden midrank

Problem. Seven wait times in minutes are [3,5,5,5,8,9,12]. Assign ranks to all seven and explain why their sum is unchanged by ties. Solution. They are already sorted. The 3 receives rank 1; the three 5s occupy positions 2,3,4 and each receives (2+3+4)/3=3; the 8, 9 and 12 receive 5,6,7. Therefore the assigned ranks are [1,3,3,3,5,6,7]. Their sum is 1+9+5+6+7=28, the same as 7×8/2=28. Averaging the tied positions redistributes ranks but cannot alter their total.

Reasoning check. Giving each 5 the same rank 2 would make the sum 25, violating the pooled-rank invariant. Giving ranks 2,3,4 arbitrarily to equal times would make the result depend on input row order rather than measurement. Both are errors. If the measurements came from two groups, their labels would be retained when summing ranks after pooling. A tie affects the reference distribution as well as the visible rank table. Transfer: if all times were multiplied by 60 to obtain seconds, the values become [180,300,300,300,480,540,720] but the ranked list remains exactly [1,3,3,3,5,6,7]. Units change; order does not.

32. Worked exercise 2 — reconstruct U from a new independent sample

Problem. Independent response times are A=[2,5,8] and B=[3,6,9] minutes. Find R_A, U_A and U_B without running software. Solution. Pool and order: 2(A) rank1; 3(B) rank2; 5(A) rank3; 6(B) rank4; 8(A) rank5; 9(B) rank6. So R_A=1+3+5=9, R_B=2+4+6=12, and their sum 21 is 6×7/2. With n_A=n_B=3, U_A=R_A−3×4/2=9−6=3. U_B=3×3−3=6. Check: the A values exceed B in exactly three pairs: 5>3 and 8>3,6, totaling 1+2=3.

Interpretation. The observed probability that an A time exceeds a B time, counting ties at one-half, is 3/9=1/3. A is descriptively somewhat faster, but the ranks overlap and a statistic of 3 alone cannot establish statistical significance. We would need an explicitly chosen alternative and exact or approximate null reference. Transfer: if someone accidentally analyzes B as the first sample, the reported U will be 6 rather than 3, although the two-sided p-value should remain the same under a symmetric two-sided reference. This is why input order and direction belong in a report.

33. Worked exercise 3 — exact two-sided probability at complete separation

Problem. Two independent samples contain three distinct responses each, and all A responses are lower than all B responses. Find U_A and the exact two-sided p-value under exchangeable labels. Solution. With n_A=n_B=3, all three A values receive pooled ranks 1,2,3; R_A=6. Then U_A=6−3×4/2=0. There are C(6,3)=6!/(3!3!)=20 equally likely assignments. Only one assigns all three lowest ranks to A, and only one assigns all three highest ranks to A. Their combined tail probability is 2/20=0.10.

Decision. At α=0.05, this maximally separated three-versus-three sample is not statistically significant in the conventional exact two-sided test. This is not a contradiction: with very small samples there are only twenty assignments, so the attainable p-values are coarse. It would be improper to change the alternative to one-sided after seeing this result merely to obtain 1/20=0.05. Transfer: compare with five observations per group, where complete separation produces 2/C(10,5)=2/252≈0.00794. Sample size changes the amount of tail evidence even if the rank ordering is equally perfect.

34. Worked exercise 4 — one negative signed difference

Problem. Five independent pairs produce differences before−after d=[1,−2,3,4,5] minutes. Compute W⁺ and W⁻, and show the check. Solution. The magnitudes are |d|=[1,2,3,4,5], ranked [1,2,3,4,5]. Signs are +,−,+,+,+. Therefore W⁺=1+3+4+5=13, W⁻=2, and their sum 15=5×6/2. The conventional two-sided statistic is W=min(13,2)=2. The single deterioration counts through rank 2, not as negative two minutes in the rank sum. The raw signed mean is (1−2+3+4+5)/5=11/5=2.2 minutes, a different summary with original units.

Interpretation and transfer. The positive ranks dominate, but we must not declare significance merely because 13 exceeds 2. The null distribution over 2⁵=32 sign allocations is needed for an exact p-value under symmetry. If the negative difference were −20 rather than −2, ranking would change: its magnitude 20 would receive rank 5, not 2, and the positive ranks would collectively sum 10. This is precisely why Wilcoxon signed-rank weights ordered magnitudes, unlike a sign test. Also verify whether the paired differences plausibly come from a symmetric distribution before translating the result into a location claim.

35. Worked exercise 5 — zero values change the denominator

Problem. Paired differences are [0,1,−2,3,0,4] minutes. Under the Wilcox zero convention compute W⁺, W⁻ and the effective number of ranked differences. Solution. Discard the two zeros before ranking; the retained nonzero differences are [1,−2,3,4], with absolute ranks 1,2,3,4. Positive sum W⁺=1+3+4=8; negative sum W⁻=2. Check 8+2=10=4×5/2, not 6×7/2. The original sample contains six pairs, but the Wilcox signed-rank reference works here with four nonzero pairs. If the report merely states n=6 without describing how zeros were treated, a reader cannot reproduce the statistic or p-value.

Interpretation and transfer. The zeros mean two recorded outcomes did not change at the measurement resolution. They should not disappear from the descriptive report; the zero proportion is 2/6≈33.3%. If we instead use Pratt's convention, the zeros participate in assigning ranks, changing the ranks attached to the nonzero differences and thus the sums. The two conventions need different null calibration. If the instrument rounds to the nearest minute, an observed zero may conceal a small underlying change. Do not treat this ambiguity as random measurement noise that statistics can automatically repair.

36. Worked exercise 6 — an independent-group omnibus calculation

Problem. Three independent groups each contain two responses: G₁=[1,2], G₂=[3,4], G₃=[5,6]. Rank them and compute the uncorrected Kruskal–Wallis H. Solution. Pooled ranks for G₁ are 1,2 with R₁=3; for G₂ 3,4 with R₂=7; for G₃ 5,6 with R₃=11. Total rank sum 3+7+11=21=6×7/2. With N=6, k=3 and n_j=2, calculate Σ(R_j²/n_j)=3²/2+7²/2+11²/2=(9+49+121)/2=179/2=89.5. The multiplier is 12/(6×7)=2/7. Thus H=(2/7)×89.5−3×7=179/7−21=(179−147)/7=32/7≈4.57143.

Reference caution. This example teaches arithmetic; with only two observations in each group, the usual chi-square reference is particularly fragile. Do not mechanically use a large-sample p-value as if it were exact. The maximum separation is visible, but valid significance calibration must respect the finite label assignments under exchangeability. Transfer: if a student computes ranks 1,2 separately within all three groups, they will obtain R₁=R₂=R₃=3 and H=0, contradicting the full data ordering. Always pool groups before ranking.

37. Worked exercise 7 — a tie correction with clear arithmetic

Problem. A pooled sample has N=10 observations and two tied blocks of sizes 2 and 3; all other values are distinct. Find the Kruskal–Wallis tie factor C. If an independently calculated uncorrected statistic is H_raw=4.4, compute the corrected value. Solution. For t=2, t³−t=8−2=6. For t=3, it is 27−3=24. The total tie contribution is 30. The denominator N³−N=1000−10=990. Hence C=1−30/990=1−1/33=32/33≈0.969697. Dividing H_raw by this factor gives H_corrected=4.4/(32/33)=4.4×33/32=4.5375.

Interpretation. The correction increases H because midranks reduce the null variability available to the rank statistic. We did not pretend the arbitrarily stated H_raw arose from a published ten-observation dataset: this problem isolates the mechanical correction as a conditional exercise. If the original raw measurements were available, we would first pool and assign midranks, compute group rank sums, obtain H_raw and only then apply C. Transfer: if there were no ties, every block would have size one and t³−t=0, so C=1 and the corrected and uncorrected statistics would agree.

38. Worked exercise 8 — interpreting equal sample medians

Problem. Population A takes −2,0,+2 with probabilities 1/4,1/2,1/4; population B takes −100,0,+1 with the same probabilities. Determine their medians and decide whether having the same median proves that rank-based tests are irrelevant. Solution. In both populations, at least half of the probability is at or below zero and at least half is at or above zero, so zero is a median. Yet A has symmetric tails at −2 and +2 while B has a much heavier negative extreme at −100 and a positive endpoint of +1. Their cumulative distribution functions are not identical. Equality of medians therefore does not entail equality of distributions.

Interpretation. A rank test can have sensitivity to some distributional differences even when medians coincide, but power depends on the exact alternatives, sample sizes and probabilities. Do not claim that a particular rank test must reject with small samples on this example: that would require a power calculation or an actual sampled dataset. Transfer: if the scientific question exclusively concerns a median difference, consider whether a targeted median inference method, a quantile regression model, or a justified common-shape assumption fits better than treating the Mann–Whitney p-value as a universal median certificate.

39. Worked exercise 9 — choose the correct design under a complication

Problem. A clinic reports waiting times from forty appointments belonging to eight patients, five appointments per patient. It proposes Mann–Whitney to compare times between two clinics, treating all forty appointments as independent. Identify the defect and outline an analysis plan. Solution. The independent unit is not automatically an appointment because repeated times from the same patient can be correlated. Even if the software accepts the forty numbers, its conventional variance and p-value may be too optimistic. Determine which patients attended which clinic, whether there are repeated observations in both clinics, the assignment mechanism, timing, censoring and case mix. A patient-level summary might be defensible for a specific estimand; otherwise a hierarchical, cluster-robust or repeated-measures approach may be required.

Interpretation and transfer. Merely converting all forty observations to ranks cannot remove clustering. If the eight patients each had a before-and-after observation, a paired analysis might be relevant—but only if the scientific pairing is genuine and other conditions are met. If they attended different clinics multiple times, the structure is more complicated than six independent pairs. Report the actual effective sampling structure and do not claim that a significant U proves one clinic caused shorter waits. This exercise tests a deeper skill than choosing a test by counting rows: correct inference begins with the unit of independence.

40. Worked exercise 10 — transfer to a new operational decision

Problem. A manager wishes to compare four independent service centers on response times and then identify a preferred center. Each center has 20 independent requests, but recorded minutes are rounded and many values tie. Write the analysis plan, including a valid interpretation if the global result rejects H₀. Solution. First identify requests as units and verify that repeat customers do not introduce dependence; record how the centers were selected and whether demand differs. Show all eighty raw recorded times or an accessible dataset, the group sizes, empirical distributions, tie counts and service-level threshold. Define the global equal-distribution hypothesis across four centers, α and alternative before outcomes. Kruskal–Wallis with pooled midranks is a reasonable candidate if exchangeability and independence are defensible. Compute H and the tie factor C, and document whether the χ² reference with k−1=3 degrees of freedom is suitable or whether a restricted permutation reference is preferred.

If the global null is rejected, the permissible statement is that there is evidence of a distributional difference among centers; it does not identify the winner. Prespecify which pairwise contrasts are useful and adjust for multiplicity, preferably with methods suited to ranks and ties. Report estimated waiting-time differences, tail performance and uncertainty alongside adjusted p-values. If the goal is causal benchmarking, account for case mix and assignment; ordinary rank comparisons alone do not justify causal claims. Student-understanding check: can you explain why using unadjusted Mann–Whitney tests on all six pairs after looking at the result would silently multiply opportunities for a false discovery? The answer is that each additional hypothesis provides another chance to reject a true null unless the family is controlled.

41. Final synthesis: explain the inference in your own words

The three worked cases share a surface feature—the original responses are replaced by ranks—but their logic is not interchangeable. Mann–Whitney pools independent observations in two groups, calculates a rank sum and converts it to U. The paired signed-rank test first subtracts within matched units, ranks absolute nonzero differences, and combines their signs. Kruskal–Wallis pools independent observations from three or more groups, uses every group's rank sum to form H, and evaluates a global equal-distribution null. Each procedure has a recognizable arithmetic fingerprint and a distinct hypothesis. A learner who cannot identify the design before calculating ranks is not ready to interpret the resulting p-value.

The key verified results from our deliberately synthetic service scenarios are U_A=0, exact bilateral p=2/252≈0.0079365; signed-rank W=min(W⁺,W⁻)=0, exact bilateral p=2/64=0.03125; and Kruskal–Wallis H=12.5, asymptotic χ²₂ p≈0.00193045. The first two p-values come from finite enumeration under their respective nulls, while the third relies on an asymptotic reference. That distinction is conceptually as important as the arithmetic. Ties, zero differences, small samples, missingness, unequal distribution shapes and clustering can change calibration and interpretation.

Before considering the guide complete, try this Student Understanding Test aloud: “If I were learning this subject for the first time, could I follow every reasoning step, reproduce the calculations and explain in my own words why it works?” You should be able to defend why each observation got its rank, reconstruct U from pair counting, explain the 64 sign assignments, verify H from three R²/n terms and say precisely which conclusion is limited by shape or reference assumptions. Most importantly, you should be able to explain why significant is not causal, not significant is not equivalent, and rank-based does not automatically mean median-based. If any of these statements seems mysterious, revisit the matching derivation and reproduce it without software.
