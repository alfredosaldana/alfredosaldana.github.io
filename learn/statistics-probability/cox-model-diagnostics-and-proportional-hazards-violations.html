Cox Model Diagnostics and Proportional Hazards Violations

From risk sets and Schoenfeld residuals to stratification, time interactions and start–stop data: complete calculations, interpretation and solutions.

Prerequisites: Guides 53, 54 and 76Recommended review: Guide 55Clearly labeled synthetic examples

1. Why a fitted Cox model still needs diagnosis

A Cox proportional hazards model is not automatically trustworthy because the software returned a coefficient, a confidence interval, and a small p-value. Those quantities answer questions conditional on a model. Diagnostics ask a different question: does that model adequately describe the observed sequence of failures and people still at risk? This distinction is important in customer retention, clinical follow-up, equipment reliability, and employee turnover. A visually impressive fitted survival curve can conceal a covariate effect that is strong early and weak late. A single reported hazard ratio would then hide the timing that matters for a decision.

Imagine a subscription company comparing customers who received onboarding assistance with customers who did not. During the first month, assistance may prevent confusing early cancellations; after a year, product fit and pricing may dominate. Reporting one constant hazard ratio assumes that the relative instantaneous cancellation rates remain the same throughout follow-up, after adjusting for other modeled characteristics. We will learn how to test the plausibility of that representation, not how to prove that an intervention caused retention. Assignment to assistance in an observational dataset may reflect customer motivation, service tier, and staff selection.

Our running example is explicitly synthetic, constructed for transparent arithmetic rather than an estimate of any real company. The event is first cancellation, measured in months since signup. A customer who remains subscribed at the last known observation is right-censored: we know their cancellation time exceeds their observed duration, but not what happens afterward. By the end, you will be able to compute a risk-set residual, recognize a proportional-hazards (PH) warning, distinguish functional-form and influential-case problems, choose among defensible remedies, and reorganize changing exposures into start–stop intervals without assigning future knowledge to the past.

Prerequisites and bridge. Guide 76 supplies Cox regression, partial likelihood, censoring, and hazard ratios; Guide 53 supplies interactions and nonlinear terms; Guide 54 supplies residuals and influence. Guide 55 is helpful for sensitivity and robust uncertainty. The sections below reconstruct the survival concepts before using them, so a reader comfortable with introductory statistics and basic calculus can follow the worked arithmetic. More advanced score-test theory is identified as cited statistical machinery rather than derived from scratch.

2. Rebuilding the survival clock and the risk set

Let \(T\) denote the nonnegative time from signup until cancellation, and let \(C\) denote the time at which observation ends without an observed cancellation. We record \(Y=\min(T,C)\) and \(\delta=I(T\leq C)\), where \(I(\cdot)\) is an indicator equal to one when the statement is true and zero otherwise. If \(\delta=1\), the event was observed. If \(\delta=0\), the record is right-censored. The time origin, measured units, and event definition must be set before fitting: mixing signup time with first-login time creates an ill-defined risk set.

The survival function \(S(t)=P(T>t)\) is a probability. The hazard \(h(t)\), by contrast, is the limiting event rate among individuals who have survived to time \(t\): MODEL\[h(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow0}P(t\leq T<t+\Delta t\mid T\geq t)/\Delta t\]. With months as the unit, its unit is events per customer-month of instantaneous risk, not a probability. For a small interval, the conditional event probability is approximately \(h(t)\Delta t\), provided the hazard does not change too sharply. This is an approximation, not an identity.

For an observed event at time \(t_j\), the risk set \(R(t_j)\) consists of people under observation and event-free immediately before that event. Someone censored before \(t_j\) is absent; someone experiencing the event at \(t_j\) is present immediately before failing. With distinct event times, Cox partial likelihood compares the covariate profile of the person who failed with profiles of everyone eligible to fail then. Thus the calculation uses ordering and eligibility, not an assumed shape for the baseline hazard.

Censoring is not equivalent to survival forever. Standard Cox inference commonly relies on independent or conditionally noninformative censoring given modeled information, in addition to correct specification. If customers disappear from the data precisely because they are quietly cancelling, this assumption can fail. Administrative end-of-study censoring is often more defensible than informative dropout, but the data collection process still needs investigation. Competing events, recurrent cancellations, and delayed entry may demand additional modeling; they are not silently repaired by a residual plot.

3. What proportional hazards actually asserts

For a fixed covariate vector \(x\), the Cox model is MODEL\[h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)\exp(\beta^{\mathsf T}x)\]. Here \(h_0(t)\) is the arbitrary nonnegative baseline hazard, \(\beta\) is a coefficient vector, and \(\beta^{\mathsf T}x\) is a dimensionless linear predictor. With one binary indicator \(x=1\) for onboarding assistance and \(x=0\) otherwise, the hazard ratio at any common follow-up time is MODEL\[h(t\mid1)/h(t\mid0)=\exp(\beta)\]. The baseline hazard cancels. PH requires the ratio to be constant, not the individual hazards and not either group's monthly number of cancellations.

Suppose \(\beta=-0.693147\). Then \(\exp(\beta)\approx0.50\): the model associates assistance with half the instantaneous cancellation hazard at each follow-up time, conditional on modeled variables. If the unassisted baseline hazards in months 1 and 8 are, for illustration, 0.08 and 0.02 per month, assisted hazards would be 0.04 and 0.01. Both hazards changed by a factor of four, yet the ratio stayed 0.5. A hazard ratio of 0.5 does not assert that the one-year cancellation probability is cut in half. The probability depends on accumulated hazard throughout the year.

An instructive contrast is a genuinely time-dependent association: an early hazard ratio of 0.4 and a late hazard ratio of 1.1. No single \(\exp(\beta)\) represents both periods. A constant-ratio model may average heterogeneous risk-set comparisons, with weights governed by events, censoring, and covariate distributions. Such a coefficient cannot automatically be interpreted as a simple arithmetic mean of the period-specific ratios. The appropriate response depends on the study question: an early retention program may warrant an explicitly early-effect estimate, while prediction may require sufficiently flexible temporal structure.

A crossing pair of Kaplan–Meier curves can raise suspicion of non-PH, but visual survival curves are not a definitive PH test. A noncrossing pair can also violate PH. Why? Survival accumulates earlier hazards: MODEL\[S(t\mid x)=\exp\{-H_0(t)\exp(\beta^{\mathsf T}x)\}\] under the fixed-covariate model, where \(H_0(t)=\int_0^t h_0(u)\,du\) is cumulative baseline hazard. Instantaneous relative rates and accumulated survival probabilities are different mathematical objects.

5. Partial likelihood before residual diagnostics

With one coefficient \(\beta\), each risk-set member \(i\) receives relative-risk weight \(w_i=\exp(\beta x_i)\). For event at \(t_j\) involving individual \(i(j)\), the partial-likelihood contribution is WORKED CALCULATION\[L_j(\beta)=\exp(\beta x_{i(j)})/\sum_{k\in R(t_j)}\exp(\beta x_k)\]. The full partial likelihood is the product of these contributions across the five distinct event times. It is partial because the unspecified baseline hazard cancels in each conditional comparison; it is not the unconditional probability of the entire observed dataset.

To isolate arithmetic from coefficient estimation, first evaluate the likelihood at the illustrative trial coefficient \(\beta=\log2\), so an assisted person's weight is 2 and an unassisted person's weight is 1. This is deliberately not presented as the fitted coefficient. At month 2 there are four assisted and four unassisted risk-set members. Denominator \(=4(2)+4(1)=12\); A has weight 2; contribution \(=2/12=1/6\approx0.166667\). At month 3 there are three assisted and four unassisted: denominator \(=3(2)+4(1)=10\); B has weight 1; contribution \(=1/10=0.1\).

Continue the same method: month 6 denominator \(=2(2)+3(1)=7\), failed D weight 1, contribution \(1/7\). Month 7 denominator \(=2(2)+2(1)=6\), failed E weight 2, contribution \(2/6=1/3\). Month 9 denominator \(=1(2)+1(1)=3\), failed G weight 2, contribution \(2/3\). Hence WORKED CALCULATION\[L(\log2)=(1/6)(1/10)(1/7)(1/3)(2/3)=1/1890\approx0.000529101\]. It is a likelihood value evaluated at a trial coefficient, not a probability that this coefficient is true.

For genuine diagnostics, the risk weights would normally use \(\widehat\beta\), the fitted maximum partial-likelihood estimate. We keep \(\log2\) for an explicitly illustrative residual calculation so that every term can be reproduced by hand. The distinction prevents a subtle error: a numerical residual calculated at an arbitrary coefficient is a learning example, not evidence that a particular fitted model violated PH.

6. Schoenfeld residuals: observed minus expected at each failure

At every observed event, the Schoenfeld residual for covariate \(x\) is WORKED CALCULATION\[r_j=x_{i(j)}-\overline x_w(t_j)\], where WORKED CALCULATION\[\overline x_w(t_j)=\sum_{k\in R(t_j)}x_k\exp(\beta x_k)/\sum_{k\in R(t_j)}\exp(\beta x_k)\]. The first part is the covariate value of the person who failed. The second is the weighted mean of the same covariate among everyone who could have failed then, given the trial or fitted coefficient. Thus a positive residual means that the observed failed subject had a higher value than the risk-set weighted expectation. In a binary example its possible values lie between -1 and 1.

Work through month 2. Customer A failed and had \(x_A=1\). The assisted group contributes \(4\times1\times2=8\) to the numerator; the unassisted group contributes zero because \(x=0\). The denominator is 12. Thus \(\overline x_w(2)=8/12=2/3\) and WORKED CALCULATION\[r_2=1-2/3=1/3\approx0.333333\]. At month 3 the numerator is \(3\times2=6\) and the denominator is 10, giving \(\overline x_w(3)=0.6\). Since B had \(x_B=0\), \(r_3=0-0.6=-0.6\). The arithmetic reflects risk-set comparison, not whether assisted service was effective.

They do not form a convincing PH diagnosis: the sample is extremely small, the coefficient is not fitted, and individual residuals fluctuate even when a PH model is correct. Their purpose is to expose exactly what a diagnostic program computes before adding scaling, smoother fitting, and inferential uncertainty.

7. Why residuals plotted against time can detect a changing effect

A Cox model with fixed \(\beta\) expects no systematic change in the relationship between event-time Schoenfeld residuals and time after proper estimation. If a binary exposure becomes less protective over follow-up, late failures can show patterns that differ from early failures, relative to model-predicted risk-set distributions. Graphing the residuals against event time and overlaying a smooth curve is a way to look for that structure. A residual at a censored time is not a Schoenfeld residual: no event selection occurred there.

In practice, software often plots scaled Schoenfeld residuals, using a transformation involving estimated coefficient information or variance. In common implementations the vertical axis can be interpreted as an estimate of the coefficient's change over time, sometimes after adding \(\widehat\beta\); therefore inspect the exact axis definition rather than assuming every package displays the raw \(r_j\). A nearly horizontal smooth curve is visually compatible with PH; an increasing or decreasing curve raises concern. The curve is a diagnostic estimate with uncertainty, not a direct measurement of a hidden true \(\beta(t)\).

Time itself must be specified. Tests and graphs may use raw follow-up time, its logarithm, event rank, or a Kaplan–Meier-based time transformation. These transformations emphasize different parts of follow-up and can change the apparent signal, particularly when late risk sets become small. Prefer a prespecified sensible transformation, show a raw-time figure for interpretation, and use alternative transformations as sensitivity checks. Do not quietly search many transformations until one gives a desired p-value.

A sample Cox diagnostic can be explained as follows without inventing empirical results: suppose a properly fitted, larger retention dataset yields a scaled-residual smooth that moves from -0.9 early to +0.2 late for assistance. Under the usual coefficient-scale interpretation, the association may be strongly protective early and attenuate later. You would need pointwise uncertainty, numbers at risk, and a formal check; the plot alone cannot confirm a time interaction. The hypothetical coordinates are a teaching visualization, not output from the eight-customer example.

8. Formal PH tests: what a p-value can and cannot establish

A familiar formal diagnostic asks whether scaled Schoenfeld residuals are related to a chosen transformation of event time. In a simplified one-covariate description, the null hypothesis is that the coefficient does not change systematically with transformed time: \(H_0:\beta(t)=\beta\) over the modeled follow-up. A test statistic constructed from the residual-time association has an approximate reference distribution under regularity conditions; some implementations report a chi-square statistic with one degree of freedom for one tested term. The exact construction and behavior depend on fitting method, ties, sample size, and software.

Suppose the diagnostic output from a hypothetical separate study is chi-square 7.20 with one degree of freedom, p approximately 0.0073. At a prespecified significance level 0.05, this is evidence against a fixed effect for that term under the test's assumptions. It does not identify why the effect changes, prove an interaction is causal, or guarantee that a proposed replacement model fits. Conversely, if p=0.42, that is failure to reject PH, not proof that PH is true. Small numbers of failures, heavy censoring, sparse late risk sets, and an unfortunate time transformation can leave the test insensitive to a scientifically meaningful departure.

A global PH test combines information across terms. Inspect both the global check and the term-specific checks: a global warning can be driven by one factor, but testing many coefficients inflates the chance of at least one false alarm if every p-value is treated as decisive. Examine effect size, plotted shape, confidence bands, event counts, and relevance to the practical question. A modest violation at the extreme tail may be less consequential for a six-month prediction than a substantial reversal during the first six months. Testing is an aid to reasoning, not a button that certifies truth.

A significant result can also reflect broader misspecification, such as an omitted interaction, incorrect covariate function, unmodeled calendar changes, or poor handling of time-dependent information. Therefore the next step is diagnosis of the diagnosis: verify the event coding and clock; inspect functional forms; investigate influential people; and only then select the most defensible response. Do not refit dozens of alternative models and report only the one that happens to pass a test without acknowledging exploratory selection.

9. A visual comparison: constant versus changing hazard ratios

Consider two deliberately specified mathematical curves, not fitted data. Curve A has \(HR(t)=0.60\) for every month: its log hazard ratio is the horizontal line \(\log(0.60)\approx-0.5108\). Curve B has \(HR(t)=0.35\) through month 4 and \(HR(t)=1.05\) afterward. B represents early protection followed by a slightly adverse late association. The discontinuity is pedagogical; smooth functions or piecewise-constant effects can be estimated if supported by the data and a prespecified question.

When reading the adjacent figure, note that the vertical axis is a hazard ratio, not a survival probability. The reference line at 1 represents equal hazards at the same follow-up time conditional on the other specified predictors. Values below 1 correspond to lower hazards for the exposed group; values above 1 correspond to higher hazards. At month 2, B compares hazards as 0.35 to 1, whereas at month 8 it compares them as 1.05 to 1. Neither ratio gives the probability of cancellation at that month. A late estimate based on very few people needs substantially more caution than a similarly sized early estimate.

A second figure displays the five raw illustrative Schoenfeld residuals from our eight-customer dataset. These dots are not the hypothetically smoother diagnostics of the preceding section. Their purpose is to visualize the signs and event times of the arithmetic we performed. With so few events, drawing a persuasive regression line would be misleading. A responsible visualization labels the source as synthetic, distinguishes observed points from illustrative curves, and states what inference is and is not supported.

10. Functional-form problems: linear predictor does not mean linear hazard

PH and correct functional form are different requirements. Suppose monthly subscription spending \(z\) enters a Cox model as \(\beta_z z\), with spending measured in dollars per month. The model then assumes one extra dollar multiplies the hazard by the same factor \(\exp(\beta_z)\) regardless of whether spending rises from $10 to $11 or $200 to $201. It does not assume the hazard itself is a linear function of dollars. The relationship is linear on the log hazard ratio scale, and may be inappropriate if risk decreases sharply at low spending but flattens among high-spending customers.

For example, let the proposed coefficient be \(\beta_z=-0.02\) per dollar. Comparing $60 with $40 gives KEY EQUATION\[HR=\exp[-0.02(60-40)]=\exp(-0.4)\approx0.6703\], conditional on other covariates. Comparing $160 with $140 gives exactly the same predicted ratio because both comparisons differ by $20. If observed evidence suggests the latter contrast has little association, the linear predictor may be wrong even if a Schoenfeld PH test is not significant. A flexible spline in spending, a justified transformation, or a scientifically meaningful category design could address curvature; arbitrary categorization sacrifices information and can create artificial thresholds.

Interaction terms add another issue. If the spending effect differs by assistance status, a model without \(z\times x\) may show patterns mistakenly blamed on time. Evaluate substantive interactions and nonlinear terms in a planned order, with uncertainty and out-of-sample performance when predicting. Nonlinearity, PH violation, and influential observations can coexist. A diagnostic workflow should separate the questions rather than use a single p-value as an explanation for all three.

11. Martingale residuals: an event count minus a predicted amount

For subject \(i\), a Cox-model martingale residual is WORKED CALCULATION\[M_i=\delta_i-\widehat H_i(Y_i)\]. In a fixed-covariate Cox model, WORKED CALCULATION\[\widehat H_i(Y_i)=\widehat H_0(Y_i)\exp(\widehat\beta^{\mathsf T}x_i)\], where \(\widehat H_0\) is the estimated cumulative baseline hazard. The predicted cumulative hazard is dimensionless and nonnegative. Therefore an observed cancellation has residual at most 1, while a censored customer has residual at most 0. Residuals are asymmetric, potentially with a long negative tail; they are not ordinarily bell-shaped regression errors.

Use a completely labeled illustrative prediction, not an estimate from the eight-customer table. Suppose a customer cancelled (\(\delta=1\)) and the fitted model predicts cumulative hazard \(\widehat H_i(Y_i)=0.30\) up to that time. Then \(M_i=1-0.30=0.70\). Another customer is censored (\(\delta=0\)) after accumulating predicted hazard 1.20. Their martingale residual is \(M_i=0-1.20=-1.20\). These numbers compare the observed counting-process outcome with model-based accumulated expectation. They do not imply that the first person's cancellation probability was 30%: the corresponding modeled event-by-time probability under the model is \(1-e^{-0.30}\approx0.2592\).

Plotting martingale residuals against a continuous covariate can reveal a smooth curved trend when the assumed predictor function misses important curvature. For such a diagnostic, a useful implementation might first fit a model excluding the candidate variable, then plot residuals against it to explore functional shape, or use suitable partial-residual methods; interpretation depends on the diagnostic design. The wild residual scatter and limited range can obscure patterns, so examine a smoother with uncertainty and do not confuse a convenient curve with proof. Censoring patterns, omitted predictors, and extreme risk profiles can also create structure.

12. Deviance residuals and influential observations

The deviance residual rescales the martingale residual to temper some of its strong asymmetry. One common definition for a right-censored Cox model is WORKED CALCULATION\[D_i=\operatorname{sign}(M_i)\sqrt{-2[M_i+\delta_i\log(\delta_i-M_i)]}\], using the continuous-limit convention \(0\log(\cdot)=0\) when \(\delta_i=0\). For the first illustrative customer, \(M=0.70\), \(\delta=1\), and \(\delta-M=0.30\). Thus the term inside brackets is WORKED CALCULATION\[0.70+\log(0.30)\approx0.70-1.203973=-0.503973\], yielding WORKED CALCULATION\[D=+\sqrt{1.007946}\approx+1.00397\]. For the censored customer with \(M=-1.20\), WORKED CALCULATION\[D=-\sqrt{2.40}\approx-1.54919\]. These are residual diagnostics, not standard normal z-scores.

An extreme residual may signal unexpected timing, a poor covariate function, bad data, or a legitimately unusual customer. It does not automatically mean that the observation should be deleted. Influence asks a related but distinct question: how much would a parameter estimate change if a particular observation were removed or perturbed? A dfbeta-like quantity approximates a change in a coefficient induced by an observation. For a coefficient on log-hazard-ratio scale, compare deletion estimates to the full-data estimate and translate material differences back into hazard ratios.

Suppose a full model estimates \(\widehat\beta=-0.50\), or \(HR=e^{-0.50}\approx0.6065\). Deleting one flagged customer yields \(\widehat\beta_{(-i)}=-0.20\), or \(HR=e^{-0.20}\approx0.8187\). The coefficient difference is \(-0.30\), and the hazard-ratio interpretation changes substantially. These are constructed comparison estimates, not actual refits of the eight-person dataset. Investigate that customer's coding, timing, and eligibility; show both fits; and decide whether the original record belongs to the target population. Retaining valid observations while communicating sensitivity is usually more defensible than deleting them to obtain a convenient answer.

13. Diagnostic reasoning as a sequence, not a checklist ritual

Start with the data-generation process: define time zero, observation windows, cancellation criteria, right censoring, and any exposure that may change. Construct an event-count table over time so the sparse late tail is visible. Check impossible durations, duplicate customers, dates after cancellation, and whether each customer is eligible for the recorded period. A perfectly drawn residual plot is useless if the event indicator has been inverted or the clock starts after a customer becomes eligible.

Next fit the scientifically motivated baseline Cox model and preserve its specification. Examine Schoenfeld plots and term/global tests for PH; inspect martingale residual patterns for continuous predictor shape; consider deviance and influence diagnostics for unusual records; examine plausible omitted interactions. Change one interpretable part of the model at a time and track the consequences for the original decision. If the assistance effect appears time dependent, a spending spline will not necessarily resolve it. If spending is nonlinear, stratification by assistance will not directly repair that nonlinear form.

Finally distinguish an inferential question (“how does assistance associate with cancellation at month 2 versus month 10?”) from a prediction question (“which customers are likely to remain at month 6?”). Stratification can improve baseline-hazard flexibility but eliminates a common coefficient for the stratifying factor. Time interaction can retain a time-specific effect but needs enough events across time. Prediction should be evaluated at meaningful horizons, not merely declared superior because one diagnostic p-value increased. Report the original specification, observed diagnostic concern, chosen remedy, alternatives, uncertainty, and unresolved limitations.

14. Remedy A: stratifying the baseline hazard

Suppose PH fails for a service tier variable, but the purpose of the study is to estimate the association of monthly spending and assistance while controlling for tier. A stratified Cox model allows a separate baseline hazard \(h_{0s}(t)\) for each tier \(s\): MODEL\[h(t\mid x,s)=h_{0s}(t)\exp(\beta^{\mathsf T}x)\]. Within each stratum, comparable people are evaluated against that stratum's risk set. Baseline hazards may have different shapes across tiers; the model does not require a constant ratio comparing the strata themselves.

The cost is crucial: because tier is represented in the stratum-specific baseline, the model does not estimate a single common tier hazard ratio. If your core research question is “what is the tier HR?”, stratifying tier discards precisely that coefficient. If the question is “what is the spending HR adjusted for tier?”, stratification may be useful, provided the spending effect reasonably obeys PH and there are enough events within strata. In very small strata the risk-set comparisons become unstable, and coefficients can become imprecise or unidentified.

Imagine the same spending increase of $20 for two customers in the basic tier at a common month; a common spending coefficient \(-0.02\) predicts \(\exp(-0.4)=0.6703\) for that within-tier contrast. A comparable within-premium-tier contrast has the same coefficient-based hazard ratio, although the two tiers have distinct baseline hazards and distinct absolute survival curves. You should not compare their baseline hazards and relabel the ratio as an estimated fixed tier effect. The stratified model changes both the mathematical assumption and the answer it can provide.

15. Remedy B: a coefficient that changes with follow-up time

A changing association can be modeled explicitly by WORKED CALCULATION\[h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)\exp\{\beta(t)x\}\] for a binary fixed exposure. One interpretable design uses a prespecified boundary at month 4: \(\beta(t)=\beta_E\) for \(0<t\leq4\), and \(\beta(t)=\beta_L\) for \(t>4\). The two hazard ratios are \(e^{\beta_E}\) and \(e^{\beta_L}\). The boundary should be driven by a reasonable mechanism or training-data analysis with transparent model-selection procedures, not selected after repeated searching for the most striking result.

For teaching, take WORKED CALCULATION\[\beta_E=\log(0.40)\approx-0.91629\] and WORKED CALCULATION\[\beta_L=\log(1.10)\approx0.09531\]. At month 2, assisted customers have modeled hazard \(0.40\) times that of otherwise comparable unassisted customers. At month 8, they have \(1.10\) times the hazard. The late value does not imply a 10% increase in the probability of cancellation by month 8; it describes the instantaneous relative rate at that time. If the late risk set contains few failures, its confidence interval may be wide and could include 1 even when the point estimate exceeds 1.

An equivalent interaction representation is WORKED CALCULATION\[\beta(t)x=\beta_E x+\gamma x I(t>4)\], with WORKED CALCULATION\[\gamma=\beta_L-\beta_E=\log(1.10/0.40)=\log(2.75)\approx1.01160\]. Before month 4, the added term is zero. After month 4, the log ratio becomes \(\beta_E+\gamma=\beta_L\). This formulation retains a time-specific association for assistance; unlike stratification, it does not conceal that association inside separate baseline hazards. The model assumes within-period constancy, so you still examine residuals and scientific plausibility afterward.

16. Remedy C: nonlinear time interactions and alternatives

A more flexible coefficient can take the form WORKED CALCULATION\[\beta(t)=\beta_0+\gamma g(t)\]. If \(g(t)=\log(t)\), then the hazard ratio for a one-unit exposure contrast is WORKED CALCULATION\[HR(t)=\exp(\beta_0)t^{\gamma}\] for strictly positive \(t\). When \(\gamma=0\), the original PH model is recovered. When \(\gamma>0\), the relative hazard grows with follow-up time. A time function must match the clock, exposure interpretation, data support, and numerical stability. In particular, \(\log(0)\) is undefined, so time zero requires handling rather than an invisible mathematical shortcut.

For a concrete numerical bridge, take \(\beta_0=\log(0.5)\) and \(\gamma=0.5\), with time expressed as months and the logarithm applied to \(t/(1\text{ month})\) to keep the argument dimensionless. At month 1, \(HR(1)=0.50\). At month 4, \(HR(4)=0.50\sqrt4=1.00\). At month 9, \(HR(9)=0.50\sqrt9=1.50\). The association changes direction relative to unity, yet every comparison is still conditional on the other predictors. Note that this is a constructed model, not an estimate from the tiny dataset.

Splines of follow-up time can make \(\beta(t)\) smooth, but the flexibility consumes information and can behave erratically where events are rare. Other options include choosing a more meaningful time origin or time scale, modeling separate clinically or commercially relevant periods, employing a different survival estimand such as restricted mean survival time, or using an alternative model family if the research question warrants it. An accelerated failure-time model, for example, answers a time-ratio question rather than automatically providing a substitute Cox HR. Explain the change of estimand rather than claiming that every model produces interchangeable coefficients.

17. The crucial distinction: changing coefficient versus changing covariate

Two different ideas are both called “time-dependent” in casual conversation. A time-varying coefficient means the impact of a fixed exposure differs across follow-up time: MODEL\[h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)\exp\{\beta(t)x\}\]. For example, signup assistance was fixed on day one, but its cancellation-hazard association may weaken over months. The person's \(x\) never changes; the multiplier assigned to that \(x\) does. The PH assumption for that exposure is not retained in its original constant-coefficient form.

A time-varying covariate means an individual's recorded characteristic changes as time passes: MODEL\[h_i(t)=h_0(t)\exp\{\beta X_i(t)\}\]. For example, customer J upgrades their plan at month 4, so \(X_J(t)=0\) before the change and \(X_J(t)=1\) afterward. Here \(\beta\) can remain constant: it represents a common instantaneous hazard ratio comparing people with different current plan status at the same time, conditional on other modeled features and appropriate assumptions. Changing a covariate does not by itself mean its coefficient varies with time.

These structures can coexist: MODEL\[h_i(t)=h_0(t)\exp\{\beta(t)X_i(t)+\theta z_i\}\]. But then both mechanisms must be described and justified. A further caution: because plan upgrades may be triggered by prior satisfaction or problems, a time-updated plan-status coefficient is an association conditional on evolving histories, not automatically the causal effect of forcing an upgrade. Standard adjustment for current covariates may fail to address time-dependent confounding affected by previous exposure; that requires more advanced causal methods and explicit assumptions.

18. Start–stop data: only information already known can enter a risk set

To fit a Cox model with an externally or internally observed changing covariate, follow-up is divided into intervals with the convention (start, stop]. Each record states the covariate value available during that interval, and its event indicator is 1 only if the event occurs at its end. For a simple upgrade at month 4 followed by cancellation at month 9, customer J contributes a pre-upgrade record \((0,4]\) with \(X=0,\delta=0\), and a post-upgrade record \((4,9]\) with \(X=1,\delta=1\). There is one customer and one cancellation, not two independent customers.

Consider a complete small illustration with three customers and no delayed entry. J upgrades at month 4 and cancels at month 9; K never upgrades and cancels at month 6; L upgrades at month 3 and is administratively censored at month 8. All times are months after signup; changes are treated as effective after their recorded transition instant for the next interval. The equivalent data representation is:

Customer Start Stop Current upgraded status X(t) Event at stop
J 0 4 0 0
J 4 9 1 1
K 0 6 0 1
L 0 3 0 0
L 3 8 1 0

Check continuity: J's intervals join at 4, L's at 3, and no person has overlapping intervals. Check exposure: J is not upgraded while contributing risk time before month 4. Check events: J contributes exactly one event at 9, K one at 6, L none. At month 6, the at-risk customers are J, K, L, and their current statuses are 1, 0, 1 respectively. At month 9, L has left at 8 and K failed at 6, so only J is at risk. An interval boundary itself is not a duplicate event; the (start, stop] convention determines eligibility.

This transformation is bookkeeping for dynamic histories, not a guarantee that the resulting Cox model is valid. You still need independent censoring assumptions, correct update times, appropriate exposure definitions, adequate event support, and diagnostic investigation of PH for relevant effects. A variable cannot be backfilled to earlier time just because its final value is known at data extraction. If data are recorded only at clinic visits or invoices, the observation schedule may introduce measurement and selection issues.

19. Immortal-time bias: the error that can reverse an answer

Suppose an analyst classifies all customers who ever upgrade as “upgraded” from signup. To receive that label, a customer must remain event-free until the upgrade. The period between signup and actual upgrade is then immortal with respect to the observed upgrade classification: a cancellation before upgrading would prevent the customer from entering the ever-upgraded category. Crediting those guaranteed event-free months to upgraded status can create artificial protection, even if upgrading has no real effect.

In the three-person table, J cannot be regarded as upgraded for months 0–4; L cannot be regarded as upgraded for months 0–3. A naive ever-upgraded comparison assigns J nine months of upgraded-labelled follow-up and L eight, totaling 17 upgraded-labelled months; K contributes six never-upgraded months. Correct time allocation is 10 upgraded months (J: 9−4=5; L: 8−3=5) plus 13 not-yet-upgraded months (J: 4; L: 3; K: 6). Total observed person-time is 23 months in both bookkeeping schemes; the classification differs, not the amount observed.

Neither a crude 17-to-6 nor a crude 10-to-13 person-time split is a hazard-ratio estimate. Events and risk-set composition must be incorporated correctly. The key verification is that the upgrade classification at month 2 is 0 for all three customers, irrespective of later history. At month 6 the current-upgrade values are J=1, K=0, L=1. No future upgrade event has been allowed to reach backward in time. Proper start–stop coding removes this particular structural error but does not remove confounding: the circumstances that cause an upgrade may also influence cancellation.

20. Full worked case: interpreting evidence and selecting a response

Consider a second hypothetical analysis scenario, distinct from the eight-customer arithmetic dataset. A retention study has meaningful numbers at risk during months 1–12. A model includes baseline spending, onboarding assistance, and service tier. The software reports a decreasing Schoenfeld-residual trend for tier, an almost horizontal assistance trend, and curvature in a martingale-residual plot against baseline spending. The tier PH test is small while the assistance test is not. One customer with an unusually large spending value materially changes the spending estimate when removed. These are described findings, not computed output supplied by a real dataset.

The first decision is not “add time to every variable.” Ask which variable is scientifically central. If the company chiefly wants the effect of assistance and spending while accounting for tier, consider stratification on tier, preserving separate baseline hazards. If it instead wants to compare tier hazards explicitly across time, fit a justified tier-by-time interaction. For spending, investigate a smooth function and assess whether its association stabilizes; do not expect stratification by tier to repair spending curvature. For the influential customer, verify data quality, compare retained and exclusion sensitivity estimates, and communicate fragility rather than silently removing the record.

Now suppose the company also wants to include plan upgrades observed during follow-up. This requires current-upgrade status represented by start–stop intervals. The historical upgrade cannot be assigned at signup. If baseline assistance is randomized but upgrades are not, the assistance comparison and the upgrade association have different causal interpretations. The resulting model might be useful for conditional prediction but not automatically a policy evaluation of upgrades. Report event counts over time, the functional form of each term, the retained strata or interactions, confidence intervals for time-specific HRs, and the intended prediction or inference horizon.

Finally revisit the original model: have its concerns actually improved? Plot and test relevant residuals again, inspect whether complexity overwhelms the event information, assess calibration or other prediction measures at the desired horizons if prediction is the objective, and record which decisions were exploratory. A residual test changing from p=0.01 to p=0.18 does not prove the second model is true. It simply removes one detected inconsistency under a test with finite power. The decision must remain understandable even if a reader never sees the software output.

A practical complication: simultaneous failures and limited information

Our eight-customer example deliberately avoids ties: all five observed cancellations occur at distinct months. Real administrative data may record only dates or rounded months, so several cancellations can appear at exactly the same recorded time. They may be genuine simultaneous events or merely events whose precise ordering was lost in aggregation. The simple single-failure denominator shown earlier is then insufficient without a rule for tied failures. Common partial-likelihood approaches include Breslow's approximation, Efron's approximation, and an exact conditional method for suitable settings. These methods treat shared event times differently and can produce different fitted coefficients or diagnostics when ties are frequent. Document the method selected by your software and maintain consistency between model fitting and residual computation.

Why does this matter for teaching Schoenfeld Residuals? For a distinct failure, the observed minus expected comparison identifies one failed covariate profile. With a tied group, the observed contribution is related to the combined covariate values of failures and a denominator that accounts for the simultaneous-event approximation. A hand calculation copied from the distinct-failure case is not a valid substitute. If timestamps are recorded only as whole months, consider whether more precise dates exist; never fabricate an ordering to avoid the problem. The PH assumption remains about relative hazards, not about the computer's method for resolving ties.

Another practical limitation is that censoring does not create the same information as an observed event for estimating time trends. A dataset with ten thousand registered customers may contain only twenty cancellations, many near the start. Its large row count does not supply strong evidence about what happens to relative hazard after month 12. Likewise, a model with numerous interaction coefficients and only a few failures can appear flexible but be statistically fragile. Report the distribution of events across time windows, not only the total sample size. A density of follow-up can be high while the density of usable event comparisons is low.

Choosing a remedy by the question, not by one statistical threshold

Imagine four distinct goals. If tier is a nuisance adjustment and its baseline patterns differ, stratification may preserve a defensible assistance estimate. If the company's central question is whether assistance helps mainly during onboarding, a predefined early/late assistance coefficient is more directly interpretable. If spending creates curved log-hazard associations, a suitable continuous functional form is the natural repair. If plan status changes after signup, a correctly lagged or current status must enter the appropriate start–stop interval; neither stratification nor an arbitrary baseline indicator recreates the missing history. These decisions answer different questions and must not be treated as interchangeable recipes for obtaining p greater than 0.05.

A scientifically careful report might include an original constant-HR estimate for comparison, followed by a time-specific effect figure, its uncertainty, the number of events available in each period, and a paragraph explaining the interpretation of the replacement model. If there was substantial searching across knots, cutoffs, transformations, or covariate sets, that selection should be disclosed; any p-values and confidence intervals calculated as if the final model had been selected in advance may be too optimistic. For predictive applications, reserve independent evaluation data or use a carefully specified validation design to distinguish improvement in apparent fit from generalizable improvement.

One can also describe results with absolute measures alongside hazard ratios. Suppose two model predictions at month 6 are survival probabilities 0.80 and 0.90. Their absolute survival difference is 0.10, or ten percentage points; this cannot be inferred directly from one period-specific HR without the baseline cumulative hazard and the exposure history. The values are illustrative. Absolute risk, restricted mean survival through a horizon, and time-specific association answer different practical questions. Choosing among them requires specifying who is being compared, at what time, under what assumptions, and for what decision.

Finally, document what cannot be learned. Without observed exposure histories, start–stop reconstruction may be impossible. Without late events, smooth late \(\beta(t)\) estimates may be largely extrapolation. Without information about dropout, independent censoring remains uncertain. Without a design addressing treatment selection, time-updated coefficients remain associational. A diagnostically improved model can still be misleading if data provenance or causal interpretation is ignored. The objective is not to make every diagnostic warning disappear but to obtain a model whose claims are proportional to the evidence actually observed.

21. Independent practice: attempt before reading the solutions

Exercise 1 — exact risk-set arithmetic. Use the eight-customer dataset and the explicitly illustrative trial coefficient \(\beta=\log2\). At the month-6 cancellation, identify every risk-set member, their covariates, all risk weights, the weighted mean of assistance, and the Schoenfeld residual. Then state why customer C is absent and why this calculation alone cannot establish non-PH. Keep all intermediate fractions.

Exercise 2 — interpretation and limits. A properly fitted larger study reports a PH test for assistance with \(p=0.38\), but a graph suggests the estimated assistance HR moves from about 0.45 to 0.95. What may be concluded, and what checks should follow? Explain why a single HR and a survival probability are not interchangeable. Say what additional information is needed to treat the visual pattern seriously.

Exercise 3 — stratification tradeoff. In a study of cancellation, service tier has non-PH but is primarily a control variable. Monthly spending is the research exposure and appears to obey PH. Write the stratified hazard model, identify which coefficient remains estimable, and explain what becomes unavailable when tier is used as strata. Would the same remedy answer a study asking for the time-varying hazard ratio of tier?

Exercise 4 — a time-interaction calculation. With a prespecified month-4 break, set \(\beta_E=\log0.40\) and \(\gamma=\log2.75\). Calculate \(HR(2)\) and \(HR(8)\), showing the linear predictor in each period. Interpret each without treating it as an event probability, and state one reason late estimates could be unstable.

Exercise 5 — start–stop transfer. In a different industry, an industrial pump begins operating at month 0. A preventive maintenance intervention occurs at month 5; the pump fails at month 11. A second pump receives no maintenance and is censored at month 9. Create the complete interval table with a current-maintenance variable, correct events, and a time origin. Is the intervention's coefficient automatically causal? Explain.

Exercise 6 — immortal time and residuals. Use J, K, L from the start–stop dataset. Calculate correctly allocated upgraded and unupgraded person-months and compare them with an incorrect ever-upgraded-from-baseline classification. Independently compute the deviance residual of a cancelled customer with predicted cumulative hazard 0.30; write the intermediate logarithm and explain whether the result is a standard-normal statistic.

Exercise 7 — integrated transfer. A hospital records treatment initiation on varying days after admission. A researcher compares survival of “ever treated” patients with “never treated” patients using a fixed indicator determined at discharge, finds a very protective HR, and notes that the PH test is nonsignificant. Identify at least two different problems with their analysis, propose a time-updated representation, and specify an additional limitation that this representation cannot solve by itself.

22. Fully worked solutions and verification

Solution 1 — risk set and residual

At month 6, A and B have already cancelled and C was censored at month 5. The risk set is D, E, F, G, H. Their assistance values are respectively \(0,1,0,1,0\), giving weights \(1,2,1,2,1\) under \(\beta=\log2\). Denominator \(=1+2+1+2+1=7\). Covariate-weighted numerator \(=0(1)+1(2)+0(1)+1(2)+0(1)=4\). Therefore WORKED CALCULATION\[\overline x_w(6)=4/7\approx0.571429\]. D cancelled and has \(x_D=0\), hence WORKED CALCULATION\[r_6=0-4/7=-4/7\approx-0.571429\]. Independent check: two assisted people each weigh two and three unassisted people each weigh one, so \(4/(4+3)=4/7\). C is absent because censoring ended observation, not because C necessarily avoided the event forever. Five events and an arbitrary trial coefficient cannot supply a meaningful fitted-model PH verdict.

Solution 2 — interpret the nonrejection

A p-value of 0.38 means the chosen test did not reject its fixed-effect null at 0.05. It does not establish that the effect is constant. HRs of about 0.45 and 0.95, if supported by precise time-specific estimates, would mean strong early lower instantaneous cancellation rates and little late relative difference. They would not imply 55% and 5% reductions in the corresponding cumulative cancellation probabilities. Check how many events and customers remain late, confidence bands, smoothing choices, time transformation, whether the estimates come from an actually fitted model, and whether other covariates are misspecified. A nearly empty late risk set can produce a dramatic but unreliable curve.

Solution 3 — consequences of stratification

Write WORKED CALCULATION\[h(t\mid z,s)=h_{0s}(t)\exp(\beta_z z)\], where \(z\) is spending and \(s\) is tier. A common spending coefficient \(\beta_z\) remains estimable from within-tier risk-set comparisons if information is adequate. The separate functions \(h_{0s}(t)\) absorb tier differences; there is no single fitted common tier coefficient and thus no \(\exp(\beta_s)\) to quote. If the research target is the tier hazard ratio as a function of time, an explicitly estimated tier-by-time effect addresses that question more directly. Stratification should not be sold as a device that estimates the effect it removes.

Solution 4 — two hazard ratios

For month 2, the indicator \(I(2>4)=0\). Log hazard ratio WORKED CALCULATION\[=\log0.40+0\times\log2.75=\log0.40\], so \(HR(2)=0.40\). For month 8, \(I(8>4)=1\). Log hazard ratio WORKED CALCULATION\[=\log0.40+\log2.75=\log(0.40\times2.75)=\log1.10\], so \(HR(8)=1.10\). These are ratios of instantaneous cancellation hazards at the specified times for otherwise comparable people. They do not directly give the probability that a customer cancels during month 2 or by month 8. Few late events and differential censoring are reasons for wide uncertainty or instability.

Solution 5 — industrial pump timeline

Set month 0 to installation/first eligibility and the event to first pump failure. Pump P has \((0,5],X=0,\delta=0\) and \((5,11],X=1,\delta=1\). Pump Q has \((0,9],X=0,\delta=0\). The first P interval contributes five unmaintained pump-months; its second contributes six maintained pump-months. Q contributes nine unmaintained pump-months. Thus unmaintained person-equivalent equipment time is \(5+9=14\) pump-months, maintained time is 6, and observed total is 20. A technician may schedule maintenance for pumps judged healthier or at higher failure risk, and equipment must survive until maintenance. Correct timing prevents immortal-time misclassification but does not establish exchangeability or a causal maintenance effect.

Solution 6 — person-time and deviance arithmetic

Correct current-status allocation is J: four not-yet-upgraded and five upgraded months; K: six not-upgraded months; L: three not-yet-upgraded and five upgraded months. Adding gives \(4+6+3=13\) not-upgraded and \(5+5=10\) upgraded months. An erroneous ever-upgraded label assigns all nine months of J and all eight months of L to upgraded, yielding 17 upgraded and only K's six nonupgraded months. In both cases \(13+10=17+6=23\), demonstrating that total time does not diagnose status misclassification. For a cancelled subject with \(\widehat H=0.30\), \(M=1-0.30=0.70\). The deviance formula gives WORKED CALCULATION\[D=+\sqrt{-2[0.70+\log(0.30)]}\]; \(\log(0.30)=-1.203973\), so WORKED CALCULATION\[D=\sqrt{1.007946}\approx1.00397\]. It is not a z-statistic and cannot automatically be assigned a standard-normal p-value.

Solution 7 — hospital transfer and causal caution

The fixed “ever treated” indicator uses future information: a patient must remain alive long enough to begin treatment, so prior event-free time is wrongly credited to the treated group. This is immortal-time bias. A second problem is that treatment assignment after admission may depend on evolving severity; a superficially protective HR can reflect clinical selection, confounding, and data collection, even after fixing time allocation. The PH p-value does not diagnose either issue and nonrejection does not certify a valid causal model. Define admission as time zero, record treatment status as zero until initiation and one afterward using nonoverlapping start–stop intervals, and place an observed death only at the last interval endpoint. Then evaluate time-dependent confounding and selection mechanisms with methods suited to the causal question; a conventional time-updated Cox coefficient alone is not automatically the treatment effect.

23. Final synthesis: what you should now be able to explain

The central insight is that a Cox coefficient is a conditional relative-rate parameter tied to assumptions about risk sets, covariate function, time, and censoring. A PH diagnostic asks whether that effect can reasonably be treated as constant over follow-up; a functional-form diagnostic asks whether a predictor enters the log hazard in a defensible way; an influence diagnostic asks whether individual observations materially drive conclusions. Those questions interact, but none can replace the others. A significant Schoenfeld-based test is a warning requiring substantive interpretation; a nonsignificant test is not a certificate of correctness.

A defensible analyst can reproduce at least one Schoenfeld residual by calculating the observed failed subject's covariate and the risk-set weighted mean. The analyst can explain what scaled-residual curves add, why sparse tails matter, and why censored people contribute to risk sets only until censoring. They can say what stratification trades away, calculate a hazard ratio from a time interaction, and separate \(\beta(t)\) from \(X(t)\). Finally, they can build intervals without allowing future exposures to classify earlier time, then acknowledge that removal of immortal-time bias does not by itself prove causality.

Before deploying a model, define the scientific question and relevant follow-up horizon, audit the observed data, examine residuals and uncertainty, compare justifiable alternatives, and report what remains unresolved. Revisit survival calibration or horizon-specific predictive performance if forecasting is the actual goal. Do not use a tiny synthetic example to claim empirical effectiveness; do not imply that mathematically complete diagnostics compensate for informative censoring or unmeasured confounding. The strongest outcome of this guide is not a perfect-looking p-value but an explanation another analyst can reproduce, question, and improve.
