01. The forecasting question: what should a smoothing method remember?

A distribution center wants forecasts of the number of orders it must process next week. A forecast is issued after the latest observed week, not after observing the outcome it predicts. Exponential smoothing is a family of update rules that compresses earlier observations into a small set of states: a current level, optionally a trend, and optionally a repeating seasonal pattern. These states represent quantities with practical meanings. The level is approximately an order volume in orders/week; the trend is a change in orders per week; an additive seasonal effect is measured in orders/week, whereas a multiplicative seasonal factor is dimensionless. The word smoothing does not mean deleting irregularity from the data. It means assigning greater weight to newer information while retaining older evidence. This makes the method interpretable and computationally economical, but a convenient update equation is not automatically a full probability model.

We distinguish three questions throughout. What forecast is produced? This is a point prediction from the estimated state. How was the state learned? This depends on initial states, smoothing coefficients, and observed forecast errors. How uncertain is the forecast? This requires explicit assumptions about future innovations and possibly parameter uncertainty. Students should not answer the third question by simply drawing a band around the first. The worked examples use exact synthetic teaching data, not observations from a real company. No causal attribution is claimed when a model detects an apparent trend or a seasonal pattern. We treat the period of a week as a sampling interval, and use a deliberately constructed four-week operations cycle, not the four seasons of a calendar year.

02. Prerequisite bridge: time index, available information, and residual

Let \(y_t\) be observed orders during week \(t\), and let \(\hat y_{t+h\mid t}\) be the forecast for week \(t+h\) issued with knowledge through week \(t\). Here \(h\) is the horizon, a positive integer, and \(T\) denotes the last training week. The conditioning bar is an information boundary: to compute \(\hat y_{21\mid20}\), we may use \(y_1,\ldots,y_{20}\) but not \(y_{21}\). The one-step-ahead error, also called an innovation in an explicitly defined innovations model, is \(e_t=y_t-\hat y_{t\mid t-1}\). A fitted value after seeing \(y_t\) is not that same forecast. Confusing the two would make a method appear more accurate than it was in real time.

For reference, suppose the forecast for a future week is 130 orders and the observed count later proves to be 136. The signed error is \(136-130=+6\) orders; its absolute error is six and squared error 36 orders squared. The error is not available until the target week has ended. The distinction matters for evaluating forecasts, choosing parameters and estimating dispersion. We use sample means and sums of squared errors as familiar tools from earlier Guides 32 and 66; when a new formula is introduced, its symbols, units and conditions are supplied here instead of assumed.

Formula and verifiable operations
\[e_t=y_t-\widehat y_{t\mid t-1},\qquad \operatorname{SSE}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}e_t^2\]

04. Simple Exponential Smoothing: update one state

Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES) is intended for a series whose future can reasonably be represented by a changing level without a sustained trend or seasonal component. It uses a smoothing coefficient \(\alpha\) and a state \(\ell_t\). Under the conventional weighted-average parametrization, \(0\le\alpha\le1\). The one-step forecast issued before week \(t\) equals \(\ell_{t-1}\); after observing \(y_t\), the revised level is a weighted combination of that observation and the previous level. Equivalently, the revision is the old level plus \(\alpha\) times the forecast error. In the formula below, every term has units of orders/week except \(\alpha\).

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\widehat y_{t\mid t-1}=\ell_{t-1},\qquad \ell_t=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}=\ell_{t-1}+\alpha e_t\]

This second form reveals the mechanism. If the new observation exceeds the prediction, the state moves upward; if it falls short, it moves downward. A value \(\alpha=0\) ignores all new information after initialization, whereas \(\alpha=1\) replaces the level with the last observation. Neither extreme is intrinsically correct. The intended use is a baseline for an appropriate series, not a default shortcut for series with strong trend and seasonality. For SES, all multi-step point forecasts made at a fixed origin are equal to the latest level: no further movement is projected without new observations.

05. SES worked calculation: reconstruct five updates by hand

Use the first five observed orders \(108,119,129,138,116\), a deliberately chosen initial level \(\ell_0=100\) orders/week and \(\alpha=0.30\). Before week 1 the prediction is 100. Its error is \(108-100=8\), so the updated state is \(100+0.3(8)=102.4\). At week 2 the forecast is 102.4, not 108: we must use the previously updated state. The new error is \(119-102.4=16.6\), giving \(\ell_2=102.4+0.30(16.6)=107.38\). At week 3 the error is \(129-107.38=21.62\), so \(\ell_3=113.866\). At week 4 the error is \(138-113.866=24.134\), so \(\ell_4=121.1062\). At week 5 the error is \(116-121.1062=-5.1062\), and \(\ell_5=119.57434\).

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\begin{aligned}\ell_1&=0.30(108)+0.70(100)=102.4\\\ell_2&=0.30(119)+0.70(102.4)=107.38\\\ell_3&=0.30(129)+0.70(107.38)=113.866\\\ell_4&=0.30(138)+0.70(113.866)=121.1062\\\ell_5&=0.30(116)+0.70(121.1062)=119.57434\end{aligned}\]

The negative fifth-week error pulls the forecast down, but not all the way to 116. Forecasts for weeks 6, 7 and 8 issued at origin 5 all equal 119.57434 orders. This is reproducible but inadequate for our constructed series because its level is rising and its seasonal cycle is pronounced. That inadequacy is a useful diagnostic, not a numerical failure. The initialization \(100\) was assumed for teaching; a fitted implementation might estimate an initial level and \(\alpha\) from training data. Reporting these assumptions prevents the arithmetic from appearing to emerge magically from the data.

06. Why older observations still matter: exponential weights

Repeated substitution shows why the method is called exponential. For \(t\ge1\) and fixed \(\ell_0\), \(\ell_t\) is a weighted sum of recent observations plus a diminished contribution from its initialization. The newest observation carries weight \(\alpha\); the previous one carries \(\alpha(1-\alpha)\); the one before that carries \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^2\). When \(0<\alpha<1\), these weights decline geometrically with age, and the initialization receives weight \((1-\alpha)^t\). Their total equals one. This is an identity derived by expanding the recursion, not a claim that older data become irrelevant under every possible series.

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\ell_t=\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}(1-\alpha)^j y_{t-j}+(1-\alpha)^t\ell_0\]

With \(\alpha=0.30\), the weights on the three most recent observations are \(0.300\), \(0.210\) and \(0.147\). Their sum is 0.657; the other weights, including the initial state, must sum to 0.343. A student can check normalization by applying the finite geometric sum formula. A large \(\alpha\) reacts quickly to sudden genuine changes but also to noise; a small \(\alpha\) produces a steadier path but can be slow to follow shifts. No value should be chosen exclusively because it makes an attractive curve. Use training data and time-ordered validation, preserving the future information boundary.

07. Holt's linear method: introduce a slope state

Holt's method extends SES when the series has a persistent local trend rather than only a level. It maintains a level \(\ell_t\) in orders/week and a local trend \(b_t\) in additional orders per week, per week of time. A one-step prediction equals the old level plus the old trend. After observing the new volume, the level blends the seasonally unadjusted observation with its previous projection; the trend blends the latest change in level with the prior trend. We use the common \(\beta^*\) notation, distinguishing it from alternate innovations parametrizations where \(\beta=\alpha\beta^*\).

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\begin{aligned}\widehat y_{t\mid t-1}&=\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}\\\ell_t&=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\\b_t&=\beta^*(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta^*)b_{t-1}\\\widehat y_{T+h\mid T}&=\ell_T+h b_T\end{aligned}\]

The horizon formula adds the latest trend once for each future week. This makes sense for short horizons if current momentum remains useful. It can become unreasonable over long horizons because it extrapolates a constant slope indefinitely. Holt is not a causal model: \(b_t\) captures observed movement, not why the movement occurs. A sudden change in demand can be absorbed into the state even when the cause is unknown. Therefore trend smoothing is a forecasting assumption, not a proof of sustained structural growth.

08. Holt worked calculation and the importance of initialization

Use \(\ell_0=100\), \(b_0=2\), \(\alpha=0.40\), \(\beta^*=0.30\), and the same first observations. Before week 1, forecast \(100+2=102\) orders. After observing 108, update \(\ell_1=0.4(108)+0.6(102)=104.4\). The observed level increment is \(104.4-100=4.4\), so \(b_1=0.3(4.4)+0.7(2)=2.72\). The week-2 forecast is \(104.4+2.72=107.12\). With week 2's observation 119, \(\ell_2=0.4(119)+0.6(107.12)=111.872\) and \(b_2=0.3(111.872-104.4)+0.7(2.72)=4.1456\). Finally the forecast for week 3 is \(111.872+4.1456=116.0176\).

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\begin{aligned}\ell_1&=104.4, & b_1&=2.72\\\ell_2&=111.872, &b_2&=4.1456\\\widehat y_{3\mid2}&=116.0176\end{aligned}\]

The positive errors cause the estimated trend to rise rapidly; however, high weeks partly reflect a recurring seasonal position. Without a seasonal state, Holt may misinterpret part of the cycle as trend. We deliberately selected initial states for transparent arithmetic. In a real analysis, initial level and trend may be estimated jointly with smoothing parameters, and their definitions must be recorded. Comparing Holt and SES on these first two in-sample errors would not establish superiority: training samples are short and both methods were initialized arbitrarily. A fair comparison belongs on later, untouched forecast origins.

09. Damped trend: projecting growth without indefinite straight-line extrapolation

A damped-trend version shrinks projected trend contributions by a factor \(\phi\), usually with \(0<\phi<1\). The next projection uses \(\phi b_T\); the second accumulates \(\phi b_T+\phi^2b_T\); over horizon \(h\) the increments form a geometric sum. This is not a claim that real growth necessarily fades geometrically. It is an explicit modelling choice intended to avoid implausibly large remote forecasts from a locally estimated slope. At \(\phi=1\) the method reduces to undamped Holt, provided the same state updates are used.

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\begin{aligned}\ell_t&=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+\phi b_{t-1})\\b_t&=\beta^*(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta^*)\phi b_{t-1}\\\widehat y_{T+h\mid T}&=\ell_T+b_T\sum_{j=1}^{h}\phi^j\end{aligned}\]

For an illustrative fixed state \(\ell_T=140\), \(b_T=8\) and \(\phi=0.8\), horizons 1–4 give \(146.40\), \(151.52\), \(155.616\) and \(158.8928\) orders. Their increments shrink: \(6.4\), \(5.12\), \(4.096\), \(3.2768\). In contrast undamped Holt would yield \(148,156,164,172\). For large horizons the damped contribution approaches \(8(0.8)/(1-0.8)=32\), so the forecast approaches 172 rather than growing indefinitely. The example states a fixed parameter and state; it does not claim these numbers were estimated from our data. Seasonal structure still requires further modelling.

10. What changes when a season repeats?

Holt–Winters adds a seasonal state \(s_t\) that carries information about the repeating position within a cycle. For our invented four-week cycle \(m=4\), the seasonal state used for the week-\(t\) prediction is \(s_{t-4}\), the corresponding position from the previous cycle. This lag is available when forecasting week \(t\) from week \(t-1\). We must distinguish additive seasonality, where the seasonal effect is an amount in orders/week, from multiplicative seasonality, where the effect is a ratio around one. A season described as +10 orders behaves differently from a season described as 10% above a level that is growing over time.

The additive version is plausible when a seasonal gap stays approximately constant in absolute units as overall demand rises. The multiplicative version is plausible when seasonal oscillations grow roughly proportionally with the series level and observations remain strictly positive. Both descriptions are hypotheses to examine, not automatic choices from an algorithm menu. For data containing zeros or negatives, standard multiplicative seasonal state and relative-error formulations can be invalid or numerically unstable. Also do not confuse two independent choices: additive-versus-multiplicative seasonality describes how components combine, whereas additive-versus-multiplicative errors describes the innovation distribution and uncertainty model.

11. Additive Holt–Winters: component equations with consistent indices

Write \(\alpha,\beta^*,\gamma\in[0,1]\) for smoothing parameters. The one-step predictor uses the old level, old trend and seasonal state from the matching position of the last cycle. After observing the outcome, we estimate the new level by subtracting the old seasonal component, update the trend from the level increment, and update the seasonal effect as a blend of the current de-trended deviation and the old seasonal state. The exact order and indexing matter. In particular, in this component-form convention the new season uses the new level \(\ell_t\); another software package may use a state-space innovations parametrization that looks different but can be equivalent after reparameterization.

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\begin{aligned}\widehat y_{t\mid t-1}&=\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}+s_{t-m}\\\ell_t&=\alpha(y_t-s_{t-m})+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\\b_t&=\beta^*(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta^*)b_{t-1}\\s_t&=\gamma(y_t-\ell_t)+(1-\gamma)s_{t-m}\end{aligned}\]

The level and seasonal state have units of orders/week, whereas the trend is an increment per week and \(\gamma\) is dimensionless. For future horizon \(h\), \(\widehat y_{T+h\mid T}=\ell_T+h b_T+s_{T+h-m(k+1)}\) where \(k=\lfloor(h-1)/m\rfloor\). This indexing selects the last stored seasonal state with the desired within-cycle position: for \(h=1,\ldots,m\) it is \(s_{T+h-m}\). For \(h>m\) the same seasonal positions repeat rather than requiring unseen future seasonal states. Students should explain that the seasonal profile is held fixed while making forecasts, until new data permit another update.

12. Reproduce the first additive seasonal updates

Use known illustrative initial states \(\ell_0=118\), \(b_0=2\), and \(s_{-3}=-12\), \(s_{-2}=-4\), \(s_{-1}=6\), \(s_0=10\) orders/week. They sum to zero, a convenient additive normalization rather than a mathematical necessity for every arbitrary initialization. Set \(\alpha=0.35\), \(\beta^*=0.20\), \(\gamma=0.25\). Week 1's prediction is \(118+2-12=108\), exactly its observation. Thus the new level is \(0.35(108+12)+0.65(120)=120\); its trend is \(0.2(120-118)+0.8(2)=2\); its seasonal effect is \(0.25(108-120)+0.75(-12)=-12\).

Week 2's prediction is \(120+2-4=118\). The observed 119 creates an error of +1 order. The new level is \(0.35(119+4)+0.65(122)=122.35\). The new trend is \(0.20(122.35-120)+0.80(2)=2.07\). The new seasonal effect is \(0.25(119-122.35)+0.75(-4)=-3.8375\). The next week's one-step forecast, using its own seasonal position +6, is \(122.35+2.07+6=130.42\) orders. Actual week 3 is 129, so its new error will be −1.42. These calculations show exactly where the new information enters each component.

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\begin{aligned}\widehat y_{2\mid1}&=118\\\ell_2&=122.35,\quad b_2=2.07,\quad s_2=-3.8375\\\widehat y_{3\mid2}&=130.42\end{aligned}\]

14. Multiplicative Holt–Winters: scaling the seasonal pattern

When volume fluctuations scale with level, a multiplicative seasonal factor may be more appropriate. Here the old seasonal \(s_{t-m}\) is a positive ratio, and the forecast multiplies the projected level by it. To remove seasonal influence before updating level, divide the observation by the factor; to update the seasonal state, divide the observation by the new level. The resulting components should be dimensionally coherent: \(s_t\) is dimensionless, and all level-like quantities retain units of orders/week.

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\begin{aligned}\widehat y_{t\mid t-1}&=(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})s_{t-m}\\\ell_t&=\alpha\frac{y_t}{s_{t-m}}+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\\b_t&=\beta^*(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta^*)b_{t-1}\\s_t&=\gamma\frac{y_t}{\ell_t}+(1-\gamma)s_{t-m}\end{aligned}\]

For a standalone first-week illustration let \(\ell_0=120\), \(b_0=2\), seasonal factor \(s_{-3}=0.90\), observed \(y_1=110\), and the same \(0.35,0.20,0.25\) coefficients. The issued forecast is \(122(0.9)=109.8\) orders. The level update is \(0.35(110/0.90)+0.65(122)=122.07778\); the trend is \(2.01556\); the new factor is \(0.25(110/122.07778)+0.75(0.9)\approx0.90027\). This example is not a hidden re-fit to the additive example. It intentionally uses a different initial seasonal interpretation to demonstrate the ratio operation. A valid standard multiplicative fit requires strictly positive observations and appropriate positive factors; an additive offset cannot simply be relabelled a multiplicative ratio.

15. Initialization: information used before the first forecast

Every recurrence requires initial states. SES needs \(\ell_0\); Holt adds \(b_0\); seasonal Holt–Winters needs an initial profile for all \(m\) positions. A common teaching construction estimates initial level using the average of an initial cycle, initial trend from the difference between two cycle means divided by \(m\), and additive seasonal deviations by subtracting a local level. A multiplicative profile instead divides observations by an estimated local level. These heuristics can be useful, but they use observations from an initial calibration window. If one reports a forecast for week 1 while its initial state was calculated from weeks 1–8, that would be an impossible real-time forecast.

In this guide the canonical initial states are declared, so the first-week demonstration is an algorithm verification. A production fitting exercise might estimate \(\ell_0\), \(b_0\), every initial seasonal state and all smoothing coefficients jointly by a training-only objective. Identifiability conventions often center additive seasonal effects to sum to zero or scale multiplicative factors to average one. Without such conventions, shifting the level and compensating with seasonal effects can produce the same fitted forecasts; the components then lack unique interpretations. Students should record both how states were initialized and which observations were allowed to influence those values.

16. Choosing smoothing parameters: what is actually optimized?

The coefficients \(\alpha,\beta^*,\gamma\) control how rapidly level, trend and seasonal states respond. A common fitting criterion is the training sum of squared one-step-ahead errors, \(\operatorname{SSE}=\sum_{t=t_0}^{T}(y_t-\hat y_{t\mid t-1})^2\), with \(t_0\) chosen to respect initialization. One could also use a likelihood under an explicit innovation distribution. Training SSE answers a narrow question about in-sample fit; it does not directly show whether a method makes the best two- or four-step forecast. More parameters can improve in-sample fit simply by following noise. In addition, the objective as a function of smoothing coefficients and initial states can have local optima, especially for seasonal models.

For the teaching updates we set fixed parameters rather than conceal an optimizer. This distinction is crucial: the fact that \(\alpha=0.35\) gives a convenient state is not evidence that 0.35 is a statistically optimal estimate for future orders. With a real training analysis one should define objective, parameter bounds, initial-state treatment and optimizer, then evaluate competing families on genuinely later forecast origins. Parameters should not be tuned on the same future four weeks later used for unbiased evaluation. When model comparisons use different start-up periods, a common evaluation sample is important because otherwise one model may enjoy a shorter or easier test. If an estimated coefficient is near a boundary, do not interpret it mechanically as proof that the true process has no trend or no seasonal change.

18. Fair rolling-origin evaluation: the algorithm is not the evidence

An honest model comparison first fixes the forecast origin, horizon and information available. At origin 12, for example, fit or update using observations only through week 12, issue a forecast for weeks 13–16 and record it. Advance to origin 16 and do the same for weeks 17–20; then origin 20 forecasts weeks 21–24. Later, each forecast is paired with its realized value. Depending on the evaluation plan, model states may be updated after each new observation, but the historical forecast must not be retroactively overwritten by a state obtained after the target date. Otherwise the exercise becomes reconstruction rather than forecasting.

Comparisons should use the same origin/horizon pairs for SES, Holt, damped Holt, Holt–Winters and simple benchmarks. A metric such as MAE summarizes \(|e|\) in orders; RMSE places greater emphasis on large misses and still has units of orders. Report errors by horizon because a one-step method may outperform a longer-horizon method even when pooled scores reverse that ranking. If orders carry different economic costs depending on shortage versus excess capacity, one-sided or weighted operational loss may matter more than symmetric absolute error. The method with the smallest point-forecast error need not produce the best decision after uncertainty and workload constraints are included. Treat all comparisons on the 24-week synthetic example as illustrations of procedure, not reliable population rankings.

19. A table of methods: different state spaces solve different problems

SES has one level state and a flat multi-step projection: useful when trend and seasonality are absent or negligible. Holt adds a trend and projects it linearly, but cannot represent repeating seasonal departures by itself. Damped Holt preserves a trend while shrinking its distant contributions. Additive Holt–Winters adds a repeating amount to a projected level-plus-trend. Multiplicative Holt–Winters scales projected level-plus-trend by a dimensionless repeating factor. The family is chosen by what the data and intended horizons plausibly support, not by the amount of formula complexity a report can display.

A model with more components can have a lower training SSE yet worse prospective performance if its seasonal period is wrongly specified or initial factors absorb noise. Conversely, a simple method can perform well when trends fluctuate unpredictably. Checking residual patterns matters: serial correlation after fitting suggests the model may not have captured all usable temporal structure. A forecast with apparently stable absolute residual variation may support an additive error assumption, whereas residual variance increasing with the forecast level suggests a multiplicative or transformed error model might deserve investigation. These are diagnostics, not automatic proof of a particular model. Crucially, forecasting the conditional mean and expressing uncertainty about future outcomes are distinct tasks; our next sections make that difference mathematical.

20. Exponential smoothing rules are not yet probability models

A smoothing recursion maps data and parameters to fitted states and forecasts. It does not, on its own, specify a random mechanism for the next observed order count. The ETS framework embeds related recursions in an innovations state-space model. The three letters describe Error, Trend and Seasonal components, with codes A for additive, M for multiplicative and N for none. For example, ETS(A,N,N) is an additive-error level-only model related to SES. ETS(A,A,A) is a level-trend-seasonal additive-error specification. ETS(M,A,A) can give the same point forecasts as its additive-error counterpart under matching states and coefficients while implying different forecast distributions. These are scientifically different statements, not merely different chart labels.

To understand the distinction, write the additive level-only process as \(y_t=\ell_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) with \(\ell_t=\ell_{t-1}+\alpha\varepsilon_t\), where the innovation has conditional mean zero. This yields exactly the SES update because \(\varepsilon_t=y_t-\ell_{t-1}\). A fully specified probability model must go further, declaring an innovation distribution, its scale, conditional independence or dependence assumptions, and how unknown parameters are treated. A normal approximation can produce negative future values despite orders being nonnegative counts, a limitation to assess when predicted counts are small. Multiplicative errors instead scale the shock with the forecast and require positivity conditions. Neither error form is universally safe.

21. SES additive-error forecast uncertainty derived from innovations

For a clear derivation use ETS(A,N,N), with \(\varepsilon_t\) independent across future weeks, mean zero and common variance \(\sigma^2\) in squared orders. At origin \(T\), \(y_{T+1}=\ell_T+\varepsilon_{T+1}\), so the horizon-one forecast error is exactly \(\varepsilon_{T+1}\). At horizon two the state would update after the first future innovation: \(\ell_{T+1}=\ell_T+\alpha\varepsilon_{T+1}\), and therefore \(y_{T+2}=\ell_T+\alpha\varepsilon_{T+1}+\varepsilon_{T+2}\). By induction, the horizon-\(h\) forecast error is \(\varepsilon_{T+h}+\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{h-1}\varepsilon_{T+j}\). Independence makes covariances vanish, yielding the variance below. This derivation is for additive SES with known/fixed parameters and conditional states, not for every ETS model.

Formula and verifiable operations
\[\operatorname{Var}(y_{T+h}-\hat y_{T+h\mid T}\mid\mathcal F_T)=\sigma^2\left[1+\alpha^2(h-1)\right]\]

With the illustrative, separately declared level \(\ell_T=120\) orders, \(\alpha=0.30\) and innovation standard deviation \(\sigma=4\) orders, the horizon-one standard deviation is 4; the horizon-four deviation is \(4\sqrt{1+0.09(3)}=4.50777\). Under a conditional Gaussian assumption the approximate 95% intervals are \(120\pm1.96(4)=[112.16,127.84]\) at horizon one and \(120\pm1.96(4.50777)\approx[111.16,128.84]\) at horizon four. The forecasts remain 120 but uncertainty widens. This is not an interval estimated from the 24-week dataset; we stipulated \(\sigma\) and a different level to isolate the derivation. Unknown parameter, state and model-selection uncertainty are excluded from this simple conditional calculation.

22. Multiplicative errors and prediction intervals are not interchangeable

In a multiplicative innovations formulation, one possible level-only observation equation is \(y_t=\ell_{t-1}(1+\varepsilon_t)\), so the one-step error in absolute orders is \(\ell_{t-1}\varepsilon_t\). If the relative innovation variance is \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), its conditional absolute variance is approximately \(\ell_{t-1}^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), not a constant \(\sigma^2\) shared across all demand levels. This helps explain why dispersion often grows with the forecast in such a model. The model requires a positive forecast level and restrictions on innovations if negative orders are impossible. Simply placing symmetric fixed-width bands around a multiplicative fitted path would contradict the stated error structure.

With seasonal state-space models, future innovations can alter level, trend and seasonal states, so the forecast variance depends on the full transition equations and horizon; it is generally not the SES formula. A reproducible approach is to simulate many future paths from the explicitly declared ETS observation and state equations, preserving the same fitted origin state, parameter values and innovation law. For each horizon, sort simulated outcomes and take suitable predictive quantiles. A simulation interval is conditional on model assumptions and fitted parameter values; it is not automatically calibrated for model misspecification or parameter uncertainty. One should also check whether the ETS implementation reports a forecast mean or a traditional point forecast when those differ for multiplicative-error formulations. FPP3 discusses this subtlety explicitly.

23. Intervals must be checked, not merely drawn

After forecasting at legitimate past origins, construct intervals before revealing the corresponding outcomes and record whether each outcome falls between its lower and upper bound. Coverage is the number of successes divided by the number of evaluated cases. For example, suppose an explicitly pre-issued set of ten nominal 90% prediction intervals captures seven subsequent observations. Observed coverage is \(7/10=70\%\), not 90%. This small sample does not establish that the true coverage is exactly 70%, but it provides a reason to inspect assumptions, interval calculation and possible regime changes. Report both coverage and typical interval width. A method that always returns an absurdly wide interval can obtain high coverage while being useless operationally.

Because multi-step rolling forecasts may share training observations and target dates, interval coverage cases are not necessarily independent. Evaluating after selecting hyperparameters on the same test period is another source of optimistic evidence. Different forecast horizons should be reported separately, with their own widths and coverage rates. Point MAE and interval coverage measure distinct properties; neither substitutes for the other. If multiplicative errors or a transformed scale is used, back-transformation must respect the intended quantity (median versus mean), and naïvely exponentiating interval centers may change the interpretation. Forecast intervals are not frequentist confidence intervals for a fixed smoothing parameter, nor Bayesian credible intervals for a posterior parameter. They concern possible future observable \(y\) values.

24. Parameter constraints, zeros and transformations

In traditional weighted-average component equations, smoothing coefficients lie between zero and one, and a damping factor is often restricted to \((0,1)\). More general ETS state-space estimators can impose a different admissible parameter region for stable models. Therefore an optimizer result must be interpreted under the restrictions actually used, not judged solely from a textbook default. Seasonal multiplicative components require positivity of the relevant level and seasonal ratios. When zeros or returns occur, a multiplicative model may be undefined even if a software library attempts to coerce data. An additive seasonal model, carefully chosen transformation or a specialized count model may be safer.

Consider a demand count of zero in a week. An additive forecasting model may still issue a valid finite point forecast, although its Gaussian error assumption could assign probability to impossible negative outcomes. A multiplicative seasonal update involving \(y_t/\ell_t\) can produce a zero factor, which may cause division by zero in a later season; strict positivity requirements must be checked before fitting. Adding one to every observation just to satisfy a logarithm changes the scale and forecast question. If using \(\log(y_t)\), forecasts must be interpreted on the log scale and correctly mapped back, with any required bias adjustment explained. The choice is about data-generating constraints and business relevance, not simply software convenience.

25. Residual diagnostics, changing regimes and the meaning of a good fit

For each one-step-ahead training prediction, compute the residual in original order units. Plot residual versus fitted level, time and within-cycle position. If residuals exhibit a seasonal pattern, the chosen period or seasonal update may be inadequate. If large positive errors cluster in successive weeks, the residuals may still have exploitable autocorrelation, contradicting a simple independent-innovation assumption. If variance rises with level, investigate multiplicative errors or heteroscedastic innovations. A sudden warehouse expansion, labor strike or promotional campaign may change the process so that last year's level and seasonal states no longer apply. No smoothing equation guarantees stability through such interventions.

Avoid confusing standard residuals with backtest errors. The former are often used to fit the model, the latter evaluate forecasts issued earlier using only available information. A low in-sample SSE can coexist with poor prospective forecast accuracy. The strongest defensible conclusion from the worked synthetic example is that the arithmetic of additive Holt–Winters reproduces a data pattern constructed to suit it. It cannot tell us which method will win on an unknown real distribution center. Report assumptions, inspection of outliers and missing weeks, forecast horizons, units, interval model and performance against transparent baselines. These qualifications make the finished lesson scientifically credible instead of merely elegant.

26. End-to-end decision protocol: from a request to a defensible forecast

A practical workflow begins by specifying the decision: staffing next week, inventory over the next four weeks or expected workload during the next seasonal high. Identify observation time stamps, target unit (orders/week), forecast horizons and when data become available. Draw the series, check for trend and a plausible seasonal cycle, and reserve a later period for evaluation. Fit an SES baseline and an ordinary seasonal-naive baseline before adding trend and seasonality. For each candidate, log initial states, coefficient constraints and objective. Issue predictions at the same origins and horizons; compute MAE and relevant business cost, then examine residual diagnostics and interval coverage.

If a Holt–Winters method produces lower test MAE, that is evidence within the observed evaluation period, not a guarantee of tomorrow's advantage. An additive seasonal profile may be preferred for relatively constant absolute gaps; a multiplicative profile needs valid positive data and approximately proportional seasonal effects. Damped trend is a useful sensitivity candidate when extrapolated linear growth becomes implausible. To report prediction intervals, commit to an innovations model (or a specifically qualified empirical method), forecast distribution and variance assumptions. Finally describe operational actions that would differ between methods: overtime staffing, spare capacity, reorder thresholds and costs of underforecasting. A clear report separates observed evidence, model assumptions and hypothetical consequences.

27. Source map and method conventions

This guide follows the official project inventory for Guide 67 and its constrained curriculum scope; the main authoritative methodological references are Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice (3rd edition), chapters on exponential smoothing and ETS forecasting, and the statsmodels Holt–Winters documentation. The former explains ETS(A,N,N), additive/multiplicative errors, prediction intervals and method/model distinctions; the latter documents practical initialization, trend, damped trend, seasonal terms and fitted parameters. Our step-by-step calculations use the explicitly stated component-form \(\beta^*\) recursion; some other notations and implementations use \(\beta=\alpha\beta^*\) or alternative seasonal-state update conventions. Numerical comparisons across sources require matching the same parametrization, objective, initial states and residual definition.

Academic references: FPP3 exponential smoothing, FPP3 ETS forecasting, statsmodels Holt–Winters. All 24 observations in the present lesson are synthetic and generated for transparent instruction. The sources support the concepts and mathematical families, not the fictional distribution-center data or the particular selected smoothing coefficients. Treat numerical forecasts as derived examples and published statistical guarantees as conditional on their stated error models.

28. Solved exercise 1 — issue an SES forecast without future information

Question. Assume an isolated five-week series 108, 119, 129, 138, 116 orders, initialization \(\ell_0=100\) and \(\alpha=0.30\). Determine the one-step prediction for week 3, its forecast error and the forecast for week 6 after observing week 5. Explain whether this is an interval forecast.

Solution. Week 1 gives \(\ell_1=0.30(108)+0.70(100)=102.4\). Week 2 gives \(\ell_2=0.30(119)+0.70(102.4)=107.38\). That value is the week-3 forecast: it was available at the end of week 2. Upon observing week 3, its error is \(129-107.38=21.62\) orders. Continue: \(\ell_3=113.866\), \(\ell_4=121.1062\), and \(\ell_5=119.57434\). Consequently, \(\hat y_{6\mid5}=119.57434\). The intermediate predictions for weeks 4 and 5 should always use the states available immediately before those outcomes; do not put \(y_6\) into the computation.

Interpretation and verification. The forecast is a single number in orders/week. Without an explicit innovation model and variance it provides no probability interval. The week-3 prediction is much lower than 129 because the deliberately chosen initial level and small smoothing weight respond slowly to an increasing series; this illustrates model mismatch rather than a math error. Recomputing the level through the error-correction equation must give the same result as the weighted-average expression.

29. Solved exercise 2 — check the exponential weight sum

Question. With \(\alpha=0.30\), determine the weights on \(y_t\), \(y_{t-1}\) and \(y_{t-2}\) in SES and show why these weights cannot sum to more than one.

Solution. Substitute the recursion three times. The coefficients are \(0.30\), \(0.30(0.70)=0.21\) and \(0.30(0.70)^2=0.147\). They sum to \(0.657\). The remaining observations and initial level jointly receive \(1-0.657=0.343\) weight. To see why the total is one, note that \(\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}(1-\alpha)^j=1-(1-\alpha)^t\). Adding the initial-level weight \((1-\alpha)^t\) yields one identically. The proof uses a finite geometric series and assumes a fixed coefficient in \([0,1]\).

Interpretation. More recent observations carry greater weight, but the history is not discarded. At \(\alpha=0.3\) the three newest values influence approximately 65.7% of the state, a fact that can be explained without viewing a software-generated plot. If an observed sudden regime change makes older information misleading, the arithmetic still works but the forecasting assumptions may need revision.

30. Solved exercise 3 — reconstruct Holt's trend

Question. Use \(y_1=108\), \(y_2=119\), \(\ell_0=100\), \(b_0=2\), \(\alpha=0.40\) and \(\beta^*=0.30\). Find \(b_2\) and the week-3 forecast, showing every intermediate state.

Solution. Before week 1, forecast \(102\). The updated level is \(\ell_1=0.4(108)+0.6(102)=104.4\). Then \(b_1=0.3(104.4-100)+0.7(2)=2.72\). The week-2 forecast is \(104.4+2.72=107.12\). Update with 119: \(\ell_2=0.4(119)+0.6(107.12)=111.872\). Next \(b_2=0.3(111.872-104.4)+0.7(2.72)=2.2416+1.904=4.1456\). The week-3 forecast is \(111.872+4.1456=116.0176\) orders.

Interpretation and limit. The fitted trend rose from two to approximately 4.15 additional orders per future week because two recent errors were positive. That number is a local state, not an estimated causal effect. If the high values reflect a seasonal position, an unseasonal Holt method can mistakenly attribute it to momentum. State the coefficient notation used: substituting a different \(\beta\) convention without conversion would produce another answer.

31. Solved exercise 4 — compare damped and undamped horizons

Question. At origin \(T\), suppose \(\ell_T=140\), \(b_T=8\), \(\phi=0.8\). Find the damped forecasts for horizons one, two and four, and compare the horizon-four result with undamped Holt.

Solution. The damped forecast adds \(8\sum_{j=1}^h0.8^j\). For \(h=1\), the increment is \(8(0.8)=6.4\), giving 146.4. For \(h=2\), the accumulated increment is \(8(0.8+0.64)=11.52\), giving 151.52. For \(h=4\), the geometric sum is \(0.8+0.64+0.512+0.4096=2.3616\), so the increment is \(18.8928\) and the forecast is 158.8928. Undamped Holt at four weeks gives \(140+4(8)=172\).

Interpretation. Damping reduces the distance extrapolation while retaining positive momentum. Its long-run incremental limit is \(8\times0.8/(1-0.8)=32\). This does not show the damped forecast is more accurate: no future outcomes or validation errors were supplied for these particular states. The correct choice depends on observed prospective performance and plausible demand dynamics.

32. Solved exercise 5 — verify the first seasonal state transition

Question. For additive Holt–Winters take \(\ell_0=118\), \(b_0=2\), \(s_{-3}=-12\), \(y_1=108\), \(\alpha=0.35\), \(\beta^*=0.20\) and \(\gamma=0.25\). Derive forecast, level, trend and seasonal update.

Solution. Forecast \(118+2-12=108\), hence \(e_1=0\). Remove the seasonal effect before smoothing: \(y_1-s_{-3}=108-(-12)=120\). The updated level is \(0.35(120)+0.65(120)=120\). The latest level increment is \(120-118=2\), and therefore \(b_1=0.2(2)+0.8(2)=2\). For season, \(y_1-\ell_1=-12\), so \(s_1=0.25(-12)+0.75(-12)=-12\). All units remain orders/week except the smoothing weights.

Verification and meaning. Since the original observation matched the one-step prediction, the current combination of states required no forecast-error correction, though a state's index still advanced. If the season had been inserted as a ratio of 0.90 rather than an additive difference of −12, the calculation would have a different meaning and must not be mixed into this recursion.

33. Solved exercise 6 — complete the second seasonal transition

Question. Starting from \(\ell_1=120\) and \(b_1=2\) with matching prior seasonal state \(s_{-2}=-4\), observation \(y_2=119\) and the same parameters, calculate all new states and predict week 3.

Solution. Forecast week 2 as \(120+2-4=118\), error \(119-118=+1\). Seasonal adjustment gives \(119-(-4)=123\). Therefore \(\ell_2=0.35(123)+0.65(122)=122.35\) and \(b_2=0.20(122.35-120)+0.80(2)=2.07\). Next, \(s_2=0.25(119-122.35)+0.75(-4)=-0.8375-3=-3.8375\). To predict week 3, use the third initial seasonal position \(s_{-1}=6\), yielding \(122.35+2.07+6=130.42\).

Check. Do not use \(s_2\) for the third position; it belongs to position two of the cycle. When week 3 later arrives as 129, the resulting error is \(-1.42\), which is unavailable at the forecast issuance time. This distinction between state update and forecast is central to preventing time leakage.

34. Solved exercise 7 — a multiplicative seasonal adjustment

Question. Suppose \(\ell_0=120\), \(b_0=2\), \(s_{-3}=0.90\), \(y_1=110\), and \(\alpha=0.35\), \(\beta^*=0.20\), \(\gamma=0.25\). Compute multiplicative Holt–Winters first-step states.

Solution. Forecast \((120+2)(0.90)=109.8\). De-seasonalized volume is \(110/0.90=122.22222\). The new level is \(0.35(122.22222)+0.65(122)=122.07778\). The trend becomes \(0.20(122.07778-120)+0.80(2)=2.01556\). Update the seasonal ratio: \(s_1=0.25(110/122.07778)+0.75(0.90)\approx0.90027\).

Interpretation and validity. A factor of about 0.90 means that this seasonal position is approximately ten percent below its underlying level, not ten fewer orders. Because division is involved, the factor and the relevant levels must remain positive. These assumptions cannot be silently relaxed just because a software optimizer accepts a numerical array.

35. Solved exercise 8 — issue all four future seasonal forecasts at once

Question. At the end of week 20 the additive Holt–Winters state is \(\ell_{20}=158.5723926905\), \(b_{20}=2.0177632886\). The recorded week-17 to week-20 seasonal states are available. What are the four horizon forecasts, and why must none be updated using weeks 21–24 while issuing them?

Solution. Apply \(\widehat y_{20+h\mid20}=\ell_{20}+hb_{20}+s_{20+h-4}\). The verified results are, for \(h=1,2,3,4\), 148.2310, 158.9164, 169.6649 and 177.6962 orders. For example, the first uses \(s_{17}\), and the fourth uses \(s_{20}\). The seasonal states are from previously observed weeks and thus available at origin 20. The projection at horizon four includes four trend increments but no updated seasonal states based on future data.

Interpretation. These values are forecasts, not measurements. If after observing week 21 we reissue a forecast for week 22, it becomes a different origin-21 prediction and should be scored separately. Mixing the two forecast vintages would invalidate an otherwise correct evaluation table.

36. Solved exercise 9 — compare a seasonal benchmark fairly

Question. Given held-out weeks 21–24 of \(148,159,169,178\) orders and the issued Holt–Winters predictions \(148.2310,158.9164,169.6649,177.6962\), compare MAE with a seasonal naive forecast that repeats weeks 17–20: \(140,151,161,170\).

Solution. Holt–Winters absolute errors are \(0.2310\), \(0.0836\), \(0.6649\) and \(0.3038\) orders; sum \(1.2833\), divide by four to obtain MAE \(\approx0.3208\) orders. Seasonal naive makes four errors of eight orders, so \(\operatorname{MAE}=32/4=8\). Both methods are evaluated against the same four targets and the same origin. This condition avoids a misleading comparison based on different forecast difficulties.

Interpretation and limitation. The result favors Holt–Winters in this carefully constructed example. It does not show that Holt–Winters beats seasonal naive by a similar amount for real supply chains; the data were deliberately designed with smooth trend and fixed seasonal positions. Four test outcomes are not an independent field experiment, and one should not extrapolate a precise expected improvement from them.

37. Solved exercise 10 — derive an SES prediction interval

Question. In a separate ETS(A,N,N) model suppose \(\ell_T=120\), \(\alpha=0.30\), independent Gaussian innovations with standard deviation four orders and fixed states and parameters. Compute approximate 95% prediction intervals at horizons one and four.

Solution. At horizon one, variance is \(4^2=16\), standard deviation four, so \(120\pm1.96(4)=[112.16,127.84]\). At horizon four, variance is \(16[1+0.3^2(4-1)]=16(1.27)=20.32\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{20.32}\approx4.50777\). The interval is \(120\pm1.96(4.50777)\approx[111.16,128.84]\). Future innovations propagate into subsequent levels, so the horizon-four interval is wider despite unchanged point forecasts.

Limit. This formula requires the declared independent additive innovations model. It does not apply unchanged to Holt–Winters seasonal uncertainty, multiplicative innovations or parameter uncertainty. A forecast band based solely on an unexplained number of standard deviations has no defensible coverage claim.

38. Solved exercise 11 — diagnose a wrong seasonal specification

Question. A fitted series has large positive residuals at every fourth week and negative residuals at intervening weeks. A colleague proposes increasing \(\alpha\) until the residual plot looks flat. What is the statistically defensible response?

Solution. First verify time stamps, missing weeks, data definition and the chosen cycle length. Reconstruct residuals \(e_t=y_t-\widehat y_{t\mid t-1}\) for each original forecast origin; do not use smoothed fitted values computed after the target week. Repeated signed residual patterns indicate remaining systematic structure. Raising \(\alpha\) may make the level follow seasonal swings more rapidly but can incorrectly encode seasonal movement as level movement. Compare a legitimate seasonal period and an additive or multiplicative formulation, checking positivity and whether seasonal amplitude is roughly constant or proportional to level. Then validate alternatives on untouched later origins.

Interpretation. A residual plot can suggest misspecification, but cannot by itself establish a unique cause. Explicit review of holidays, business operations and changes in observation schedule is part of model diagnosis. The final choice should depend on prospective score and decision costs, not visual smoothness.

39. Solved exercise 12 — transfer the method to a nonpositive series

Question. A warehouse tracks net stock adjustment per week, including negative returns and some zeros. The weekly pattern may vary with absolute workload. A teammate requests multiplicative Holt–Winters with Gaussian relative errors because another company uses it. Explain the scientific problem and propose a defensible evaluation plan.

Solution. Relative seasonal factors and multiplicative innovation equations require positive denominators and generally positive observations or forecasts. When the measured target can be zero or negative, dividing by level or a seasonal factor may be undefined or unstable. First determine whether the decision target should be net adjustment, incoming volume, outgoing volume or separate components; this is a domain question that cannot be answered by smoothing alone. If net adjustment must remain the target, consider an additive model that admits signed values and evaluate the suitability of its innovation distribution. Alternatively model nonnegative incoming and outgoing flows separately, then reconcile their forecasts with uncertainty.

Transfer and limits. Choose chronological origins and horizons, compare a simple baseline to SES/Holt/additive seasonal candidates, inspect residual dependence and heteroscedasticity, and score both forecast accuracy and cost. Document assumptions and data provenance before quoting any interval coverage. This answer changes the modelling decision rather than merely replacing a coefficient: it demonstrates understanding of the boundary between an update equation and an admissible statistical model.

40. Solved exercise 13 — identify correct seasonal state at long horizon

Question. A four-week additive seasonal model at the end of week 20 stores \(s_{17}\), \(s_{18}\), \(s_{19}\) and \(s_{20}\). Which state belongs in a forecast for week 25, horizon five? Explain why \(s_{21}\) must not be used when issuing this forecast at origin 20, even if a fitting program would calculate such a state later.

Solution. With period \(m=4\) and \(h=5\), compute \(k=\lfloor(5-1)/4\rfloor=1\). The stored seasonal index is \(T+h-m(k+1)=20+5-4(2)=17\). Therefore \(\widehat y_{25\mid20}=\ell_{20}+5b_{20}+s_{17}\). The fifth horizon repeats the same seasonal position as the first horizon of the coming cycle. State \(s_{21}\) could be obtained only after observing week 21, which is not available at the end of week 20. Inserting it would convert a forecast created at week 20 into an incorrectly timestamped revised estimate.

Interpretation. A seasonal pattern is reused from the most recent known cycle, while the projected level advances by five trend increments. The answer depends on the origin and information cutoff, not simply on a mathematical remainder. This exact indexing rule extends to horizons nine and thirteen, but repeatedly recycling old seasonal effects may be less plausible at long horizons if the business process changes. The formula remains consistent, while the empirical assumption becomes increasingly uncertain.

41. Solved exercise 14 — distinguish two kinds of uncertainty

Question. Two analysts have identical SES states \(\ell_T=120\) and smoothing coefficient \(\alpha=0.30\). Analyst A assumes independent additive innovations with standard deviation four orders. Analyst B assumes a multiplicative relative innovation with standard deviation \(0.04\). Should they report identical horizon-one 95% intervals because the point forecast matches?

Solution. No. Under A, the forecast standard deviation is four orders, so a Gaussian approximate interval is \(120\pm1.96(4)=[112.16,127.84]\). Under B, the one-step observation is \(y_{T+1}=120(1+\varepsilon_{T+1})\), with relative innovation standard deviation 0.04. Its absolute standard deviation at this state is \(120(0.04)=4.8\) orders. Under an appropriate approximate Gaussian relative-error law the interval becomes \(120\pm1.96(4.8)=[110.592,129.408]\). The calculation holds only if the relative error distribution is compatible with valid positive demand; unrestricted Gaussian shocks technically permit sufficiently negative outcomes.

Interpretation. Equal point forecasts do not imply equal forecast distributions. At a larger level of 240, B's standard deviation would be 9.6 orders, while A's stipulated absolute innovation scale remains four. The comparison makes the difference between model method and error mechanism operational and exposes why fixed-width bands cannot be imported from an unrelated ETS model.

42. Solved exercise 15 — compare two initialization claims

Question. A colleague states: “We reported a valid forecast for week 1, with Holt–Winters initial seasonal factors computed from the first eight weeks.” Another team initializes from the first eight weeks, then reports its first prospective forecast at week 9. Which is logically valid and why?

Solution. Initializing from weeks 1–8 consumes their outcomes. The supposed week-1 forecast of the first team depends on observations from weeks 2–8 that were unknown at week 0. It is a retrospective fitted or reconstructed value, not a legitimate week-1 real-time forecast. The second team may use weeks 1–8 to establish initial level, trend and seasonal states, then issue a week-9 prediction using information through week 8. If its coefficients are also fitted on those eight weeks, their estimation must use only that window as well. A later test origin must preserve its own permitted information boundary.

Interpretation. An algorithm can compute a fitted value for every historical date without providing a historically available forecast for every date. For fair comparisons, log initialization window, first scored date, seasonal period and number of included observations. A method with a longer warm-up should not gain an artificial advantage by scoring only easier periods while its competitor is scored from the beginning. Time consistency is part of numerical reproducibility, not an optional reporting convention.

43. Solved exercise 16 — final operational transfer and decision

Question. A real distribution center processes a growing number of orders with an apparent four-week cycle. An additive Holt–Winters fit beats Seasonal Naive on four held-out weeks, but its residual scatter increases sharply with volume and a new warehouse will open in six weeks. The manager wants both staffing forecasts and 95% upper-capacity bounds. Produce an evidence-based plan, identify which results are presently justified, and explain what cannot yet be claimed.

Solution. First define the operational forecast origins, target (orders/week), horizons and the opening date. Record complete data, missing weeks, interventions and whether the four-week rhythm has a process explanation. Refit at legitimate expanding origins with initial states and smoothing parameters estimated from training-only data. Compare Seasonal Naive, SES, Holt, damped Holt and additive/multiplicative seasonal candidates on the same scored origin/horizon pairs. For each, report MAE by horizon, large-error behavior and a capacity shortage cost. The apparent variance increase suggests inspecting multiplicative innovations or another heteroscedastic error model, but not blindly applying multiplicative seasonality; these are separate decisions. Check that demand and seasonal factors satisfy positivity restrictions before attempting multiplicative formulations.

Uncertainty, interpretation and transfer. For upper-capacity bounds, declare and fit a specific innovations state-space model, simulate or calculate its horizon-wise predictive distribution, and assess empirical interval coverage and width at genuinely later forecast origins. The planned warehouse opening represents a likely regime change, so supplement statistical extrapolation with documented scenarios and managerial information; past states alone cannot establish the size or direction of its effect.
