1. The question before the coefficient

Suppose an occupational-health analyst records how many hours employees spend in moderate physical activity during a week and their fatigue score at the end of that week. The immediate question may be descriptive: do employees who report more activity also report more or less fatigue? A scatterplot and a correlation coefficient can answer parts of that question. A very different question is causal: would prescribing an additional hour of activity change an employee's fatigue if everything else relevant were held constant? A correlation alone cannot answer that second question. A third question is inferential: if these employees represent a sample, what can we conclude about an association in a defined population, allowing for sampling variability? This guide treats all three questions separately because the distinctions prevent some of the most costly interpretations in applied statistics.

A variable is a recorded characteristic that can differ across units. Here the unit is an employee-week, unless several weeks for the same employee are recorded; repeated employee-weeks would then require explicit handling of dependence. Let X denote hours of activity per week, and Y a fatigue score measured on a stated instrument. Hours have a physical unit; a score has whatever units the instrument defines. An observation pair (xᵢ,yᵢ) belongs to the same employee-week. The x and y values must never be matched by independently sorting their columns: doing so destroys the pairing from which correlation gets its meaning. The subscript i indexes a unit, not a time trend or an assumed causal direction.

The objectives are to reconstruct Pearson's r from actual paired observations, interpret magnitude and direction without pretending r is a slope, conduct a classical test and a Fisher-z confidence interval under appropriate assumptions, recognize nonlinear patterns and influential observations, calculate an illustrative Spearman correlation, and discover an aggregate association that reverses after stratifying by a workplace factor. The last objective is essential: a perfectly computed coefficient can still represent a scientifically misleading comparison.

2. What you need to know—and what we will rebuild

The essential prerequisites from the curriculum are Guides 04, 13, 30, and 37: random variables, expectation and variability, sampling-related uncertainty, and hypothesis testing. Guide 49 offers optional review of ranks. No previous course in causal inference is assumed. We will rebuild arithmetic means, deviations, sample covariance, standard deviation, the t distribution, confidence intervals, the natural logarithm, and the relevant distinction between conditional and marginal comparisons exactly where they are needed. Calculus I is sufficient; advanced matrix algebra is not required for any main computation.

A population is the set of units or a conceptual data-generating process to which a claim refers. A sample is the units actually measured. The sample correlation is r; the population correlation is ρ (Greek rho). The sample mean of X is x̄, and its sample standard deviation is sₓ. Sample covariance is sₓᵧ. The familiar number n counts independent paired units in classical inference, not necessarily rows of a spreadsheet. If one person contributes five repeated rows, n is not automatically five independent units. Define the sampling frame, selection rule, time window, inclusion criteria, and outcome instrument before interpreting a test statistic.

We also need to distinguish a statistic from a scientific estimand. r is a sample summary. ρ=Corr(X,Y) is a parameter about joint variation in a specified population. A causal effect, by contrast, compares outcomes under different interventions; it is not generally equal to either r or ρ. These mathematical objects answer different questions. Even when a study is large enough to estimate ρ extremely precisely, that precision does not manufacture identification of a causal effect. We will return to this separation at the end rather than adding a one-line warning after the calculations.

3. Visual intuition: what a scatterplot does and does not show

A scatterplot places every paired observation at (xᵢ,yᵢ). X normally occupies the horizontal axis and Y the vertical axis, but exchanging axes does not change Pearson's r. Look for direction, curvature, clusters, heavy tails, gaps, missing ranges, possible thresholds, and isolated points. A rising straight-ish cloud suggests positive linear association; a falling cloud suggests negative linear association; an arch may have substantial dependence but a small linear correlation. A tight cloud is more strongly linearly associated than a diffuse cloud only when the relevant patterns and scales are comparable. Color or facet by plausible third variables when the scientific setting suggests hidden groups.

The visual task comes before computing r. A scalar coefficient compresses many observations into one number, so several profoundly different clouds can share the same coefficient. In an occupational dataset, high activity may occur predominantly on the day shift and low activity predominantly on the night shift; two clusters may then dominate the overall trend. A scatterplot colored by shift could expose this immediately. Do not decide that an isolated point is an error merely because it changes r: check the source record, measurement process and eligibility rule, and document any correction or exclusion without looking only at the desired result.

Figure 1 plots the six complete synthetic pairs used below. Each dot is one constructed employee-week. The point (6,7) is not a claim about typical fatigue or a medical benchmark; it is an arithmetic illustration with a deliberately simple range. When you read the plot, try to anticipate the sign of the centered products before seeing the formula. Points whose x and y deviations have the same sign contribute positively to the numerator of r; points in opposite quadrants contribute negatively. The visual intuition and the algebra are the same argument in two languages.

5. Covariance is a first attempt, but it has units

The sample covariance is sₓᵧ=Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)/(n−1)=18/5=3.6 hour·score. A positive value says that joint deviations tend to share a sign; a negative value says that opposing signs dominate. The denominator n−1 reflects the standard sample covariance convention. Its particular value will cancel when constructing r from matching sample standard deviations. This is why the computational formula for correlation does not need three separate divisions by n−1.

Sample covariance · hours × fatigue points
\[s_{xy}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{n-1}=\frac{18}{5}=3.6\]

Covariance measures co-movement but changes numerically when units change.

Covariance is not directly comparable across instruments. Change activity from hours to minutes and the numerical covariance multiplies by 60, even though the relationship in the actual employee-weeks has not changed. Change a fatigue instrument from 0–10 to 0–100 and the covariance changes again. A metric that depends on arbitrary unit choices is not an attractive common scale for comparing associations. That motivates standardization: divide a centered value by its standard deviation, making it dimensionless, then examine how these standardized values move together.

Compute sₓ²=17.5/5=3.5 hours² and sᵧ²=(70/3)/5=14/3≈4.6667 score². Thus sₓ=√3.5≈1.8708 hours and sᵧ=√(14/3)≈2.1602 score points. Check the units: square roots turn squared units back into hours and score points. The product sₓsᵧ is about 4.0415 hour·score, so the ratio 3.6/4.0415 is dimensionless. If either standard deviation is zero, Pearson's correlation is undefined, not zero: a constant variable has no standardized deviations to compare.

6. Pearson's sample correlation: derivation and exact arithmetic

Define Pearson's r by \(r=\frac{s_{xy}}{s_xs_y}\). Substitute the sample quantities: r=(18/5)/[√(17.5/5)√((70/3)/5)]. Multiplying numerator and denominator by 5 yields the computationally cleaner identity r=Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)/√[Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)²]. In this table, r=18/√[17.5×(70/3)]=18/√(1225/3)=18√3/35≈0.890769. Every term in the fraction is shown; the rounded value should not replace full precision in further calculations.

Pearson correlation · dimensionless
\[\boxed{r=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})^2\;\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}}\]

Each pair contributes one centered cross-product; both centered sums in the denominator must be positive.

Why must −1≤r≤1? Imagine the two centered lists as vectors a=(x₁−x̄,…,xₙ−x̄) and b=(y₁−ȳ,…,yₙ−ȳ). Their dot product is the centered cross-product sum. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality states |a·b|≤||a||||b||, so dividing by the two positive lengths bounds the ratio between −1 and 1. Equality +1 occurs when one centered list is a positive constant multiple of the other, yielding an exact increasing straight-line relationship; −1 occurs with a negative multiple. This is a short mathematical proof, not an empirical generalization that all relationships must be linear.

Our r≈0.891 is strong positive linear association within these six constructed pairs. It does not mean that an additional hour increases fatigue by 0.891 points; r is dimensionless and cannot play the role of a slope. It does not mean 89.1% of employees experience an effect or that the variable causes 89.1% of variation. In a simple least-squares model with an intercept, r²≈0.7935 is the fraction of sample Y variation explained by its fitted linear projection on X. This descriptive identity does not by itself imply any causal mechanism or guarantee out-of-sample predictive performance.

7. Three independent checks of the coefficient

First, use the raw-sums identity: Σxy−(ΣxΣy)/n. From the pairs Σx=21, Σy=22, Σxy=95, so 95−21×22/6=95−77=18. For X, Σx²=91 and 91−21²/6=91−73.5=17.5. For Y, Σy²=104 and 104−22²/6=104−80.6667=23.3333. Substitution reproduces r≈0.890769. This check avoids rounding individual deviations and is especially useful with small integer datasets.

Independent numerical verification
\[\begin{aligned}S_{xy}&=\sum x_iy_i-\frac{(\sum x_i)(\sum y_i)}{n}=95-77=18\\r&=\frac{18}{\sqrt{17.5(70/3)}}=\frac{18\sqrt{3}}{35}\approx0.890769\end{aligned}\]

Using raw sums gives exactly the same Pearson coefficient as centered deviations.

Second, reverse the columns. The numerator Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ) is unchanged because multiplication is commutative, and the two squared sums simply exchange positions in a product. Therefore Corr(X,Y)=Corr(Y,X), even though causal language about X acting on Y is not symmetric. Third, translate and rescale measurements. If X=60X+10 and Y=2Y−3, both positive scale factors preserve r exactly. If a scale factor is negative, the sign flips. Squaring r eliminates the sign but also loses directional information; one should not report r² alone when a negative trend matters.

As a quality-control rule, compare the value calculated from deviations with the value calculated from raw sums and with a numerical implementation. If they differ beyond small rounding, stop and inspect the pairing, missing-value handling, data type, or transcription. This is more educationally informative than accepting a software printout whose provenance is unknown. Our three routes agree for these six pairs, but agreement about arithmetic does not establish external validity of a synthetic case.

8. Population correlation and the limits of sample evidence

The population parameter is ρ=Cov(X,Y)/[SD(X)SD(Y)] when the two standard deviations exist and are finite. A sample r estimates ρ under a relevant sampling model. If the six rows were merely the full population of six constructed points, there would be no uncertainty about that finite set's descriptive correlation. If they were an independently sampled subset of an enormous employee population, repeated samples could produce different r values. The inferential interpretation therefore depends on the sampling design, not on a formula alone.

A high r can appear by chance in a small sample; likewise a modest r can be estimated precisely with many independent pairs. A zero population Pearson correlation states that the standardized linear covariance is zero. It does not generally imply independence. For example, when X is symmetric about zero and Y=X², positive and negative X values can cancel in covariance even though Y is entirely determined by X. Independence implies zero covariance when the moments exist; the converse is false except under special joint models, notably a jointly normal pair.

Selection effects also matter. If a sample includes only employees with fatigue scores between 4 and 6, the range restriction may attenuate or distort observed correlation even if the population association is stronger. Conversely mixing populations with different baselines may create a striking r absent within-group trends. Identifying the intended population, missing-data mechanism, and measurement process is as important as identifying the sign and p-value. Do not use confidence intervals to solve an unaddressed selection problem.

9. Classical inference for H₀: ρ=0

For the familiar exact finite-sample test, assume n independent and identically distributed paired observations from a bivariate normal population with nonzero variances. The null hypothesis is H₀:ρ=0, and a common two-sided alternative is H₁:ρ≠0. The classical statistic is \(t=r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}}\), with n−2 degrees of freedom under the null. The t reference is exact under the stated joint normal model. Without those assumptions, using the same reference may be an approximation of uncertain calibration. The test addresses population linear correlation, not the effect of an intervention.

Classical test of zero population correlation
\[\boxed{t=r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}},\qquad \mathrm{df}=n-2}\]

Exact null reference requires independent, identically distributed bivariate-normal pairs.

In our six-point case, r²=(18√3/35)²=972/1225≈0.793469, hence 1−r²=253/1225≈0.206531. The statistic is t=(18√3/35)√[4/(253/1225)]=36√3/√253≈3.9200. Degrees of freedom: 6−2=4. The two-sided probability P(|T₄|≥|t|)≈0.0172455. At a prespecified α=0.05 we reject H₀ under the classical model. This is evidence against zero population correlation, conditional on a sampling model; it is not a probability that H₀ itself is true, nor a probability that a causal effect exists.

Notice the fragility of small n and assumptions. Six points provide only four degrees of freedom. A test may have a low p-value because these constructed observations align strongly, while remaining unrepresentative of any occupational population. If the observations are temporal, clustered by person, selectively reported, or strongly influenced by one point, the exact t reference no longer has its advertised justification. A valid statistical conclusion always includes the process that would have produced comparable samples, not just the inequality p<0.05.

10. What a p-value means and how to report it

A p-value is a tail probability calculated assuming the null and the statistical model used by the test. Here it asks: if the true Pearson ρ were zero under independent bivariate-normal sampling, how unusual would a sample r with absolute magnitude at least about 0.891 be? The numerical answer, about 0.0172, concerns repeated hypothetical samples. It is not the chance the observed data are wrong, not the chance that r is generated by “randomness alone,” and not the chance that activity is a cause of fatigue. Scientific decisions require effect size, precision, design and costs of errors.

A two-sided p-value, not a causal probability
\[\begin{aligned}t&=\frac{36\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{253}}\approx3.920\\p&=2\,P(T_4\geq |t|)\approx0.0172455\end{aligned}\]

The p-value assumes the null and its sampling model; it is not the probability that the null is true.

The conventional threshold α=0.05 is a rule chosen before seeing the evidence, not a boundary where a scientifically negligible relationship becomes large. A report should state the sample size, coefficient, test statistic or p-value, confidence interval and its assumptions. For example: “In six illustrative independent bivariate-normal pairs, Pearson r=0.891, two-sided t(4)≈3.920, p≈0.0172.” Add that the data are synthetic and no occupational conclusion follows. Reporting only “significant correlation” hides direction, size and the data provenance.

Consider what changes if the research team ran correlations against fifty outcomes and disclosed only the smallest p-value. The nominal 0.05 rule no longer describes the same opportunity for false discoveries without a multiplicity plan. Guide 46 develops familywise and false-discovery control. This guide's emphasis is complementary: before controlling the number of correlations, ensure each pair reflects a scientifically meaningful unit, instrument, population and comparison. Statistical multiplicity cannot repair confounding.

11. Fisher's z transformation: from a bounded r to approximate normality

A correlation lies between −1 and 1 and has a skewed sampling distribution, especially when the true association is strong. Fisher's transformation helps construct an approximate confidence interval. Define \(z=\operatorname{atanh}(r)=\frac12\ln\!\left(\frac{1+r}{1-r}\right)\), where ln is the natural logarithm—the inverse of the exponential function. For |r|<1 the ratio inside the logarithm is positive. Under independent bivariate-normal sampling and a sufficiently useful sample size, z is approximately normal with standard error \(\operatorname{SE}(z)=\frac1{\sqrt{n-3}}\). This approximation is not the same claim as the exact t test under H₀.

Fisher z confidence interval · approximate
\[\begin{aligned}z&=\operatorname{atanh}(r)=\frac12\ln\!\left(\frac{1+r}{1-r}\right)\\\operatorname{SE}(z)&\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-3}}\\\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\rho)&\approx\tanh\!\left(z\pm1.96\operatorname{SE}(z)\right)\end{aligned}\]

Use |r| < 1 and n > 3. The interval is an approximation, particularly fragile at n = 6.

For r≈0.890769, (1+r)/(1−r)≈17.31, so z=½ln(17.31)≈1.425. With n=6, SE=1/√3≈0.57735, a very large uncertainty on the transformed scale. For a nominal 95% interval use z±1.96SE. The lower endpoint is about 1.425−1.132=0.293, and the upper is about 2.557. Transform back using tanh(z)=[e^(2z)−1]/[e^(2z)+1]. The approximate interval for ρ is [0.28584,0.98805]. It is very wide, as we should expect from six units. The 1.96 multiplier is a standard-normal critical value, not a guarantee of exact 95% coverage at n=6.

A confidence interval is a repeated-sampling procedure with a stated long-run coverage target under assumptions. It is not a 95% probability statement that a fixed population ρ “moves around” inside this particular realized interval. Fisher intervals become especially questionable when n is tiny, samples are dependent, the joint distribution is far from bivariate normal, or r is exactly ±1. When n≤3 the usual 1/√(n−3) formula is unusable; when |r|=1 at finite n the logarithm diverges. State such domain restrictions rather than presenting the transformation as a universal trick.

12. A second uncertainty calculation with an explicitly hypothetical larger sample

Suppose, only as a numerical scenario, an independent study of n=30 bivariate-normal pairs produces r=0.50. These are summary values stipulated for instruction; there is no complete raw dataset behind this particular scenario, so it should not be portrayed as an observed case or a reproducible empirical analysis. The test statistic is t=0.5√[28/(1−0.25)]=0.5√(28/0.75)≈3.05505 with 28 degrees of freedom. Its two-sided p-value is roughly 0.00490. The calculation supplies an example of sample-size sensitivity, not a second real-world claim.

Second Fisher example · hypothetical sample of 30
\[\begin{aligned}z&=\operatorname{atanh}(0.50)\approx0.549306\\\operatorname{SE}(z)&=\frac1{\sqrt{27}}\approx0.192450\\\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\rho)&\approx\tanh(0.549306\pm1.96(0.192450))\end{aligned}\]

This is a stipulated summary scenario, not a second observed dataset.

For its Fisher interval, z=atanh(0.5)=½ln(3)≈0.549306. The transformed standard error is 1/√27≈0.192450. The 95% limits on the z scale are about 0.549306±0.377202, or [0.172104,0.926508]. Applying tanh gives a correlation interval of approximately [0.170,0.729]. Compared with the n=6 scenario, the uncertainty is narrower even though the observed coefficient is smaller. This is why classifying r values by adjectives alone, without n and uncertainty, can be misleading.

A responsible analyst would also ask whether the hypothetical sample size was chosen before seeing results, whether employees were independent, and whether linearity and outliers were assessed. The larger-n interval does not automatically protect against invalid sampling, omitted confounders, nonlinearity or exposure measurement errors. Better precision of an association estimate is not equivalent to higher credibility of a causal explanation. The interval's interpretation stays conditional on its statistical target and its data-generating conditions.

13. Outliers, leverage, and influential observations

An outlier has an unusual value on at least one dimension. A high-leverage observation has an unusually extreme X value relative to the other X values. An influential observation appreciably changes an estimated coefficient or substantive conclusion when included or removed. These concepts overlap but are not identical: a point can be far away vertically yet have ordinary X, or have extreme X but fall precisely on a line. Pearson's r depends on centered products and squared deviations, so a single extreme pair can dominate numerator and denominator.

Imagine five pairs with X=1,2,3,4,5 and Y=2,4,2,4,3, then add a sixth pair X=100,Y=100. The added point can create a very high positive correlation even though the first five do not display a strong monotone pattern. The correct response is not automatically to delete it. Identify whether it is a transcription error, a genuinely different population, or a valid extreme employee-week. Show the scatterplot with units and compare the coefficient with and without that point as a sensitivity analysis, transparently preserving the primary analysis.

Do not decide to exclude a point simply because it reverses statistical significance. That would turn a diagnostic into outcome-dependent selection. Predefine plausible data-quality rules, record exclusions and make both primary and sensitivity conclusions available. In complex designs, robust methods and substantive models may be more appropriate than one Pearson coefficient. A Spearman correlation can reduce sensitivity to large numerical distances, but it is not immune to influential ranks, measurement ties or confounding. Neither method is a substitute for understanding the observation mechanism.

14. Nonlinear association and the case of zero correlation with dependence

Use a five-point artificial example: X=−2,−1,0,1,2 and Y=X²=4,1,0,1,4. X̄=0 and ȳ=2. The centered cross-products are (−2)(2)=−4, (−1)(−1)=+1, 0(−2)=0, 1(−1)=−1, and 2(2)=+4. Their sum is zero, so Pearson r=0 because both variables vary and the covariance is zero. Yet knowing X reveals Y exactly; this is perfect nonlinear functional dependence. A plot shows a U shape rather than an unstructured cloud. Saying “no relationship” would be wrong; “no sample linear correlation” is exact.

Zero linear correlation does not imply independence
\[\begin{aligned}X&=(-2,-1,0,1,2)\\Y&=X^2=(4,1,0,1,4)\\\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)&=0\implies r=0\end{aligned}\]

The relationship is perfectly quadratic even though Pearson correlation is zero.

More generally, association can be curved, thresholded, seasonal, clustered or conditional on a third variable. The answer is not simply to calculate a dozen coefficients and choose the largest. Start from the shape and scientific question; consider transformations, flexible regression, categorized summaries, nonlinear measures or subject-matter models when justified. Do not interpret a change in r after a transformation as evidence that the original data were erroneous. Each transformation changes what is measured—for example, correlating log exposure and an outcome describes linearity in log exposure, not original exposure.

The quadratic example also clarifies an elementary logical statement. If X and Y are independent and their second moments exist, then E[XY]=E[X]E[Y], making covariance zero. But zero covariance does not establish independence: the quadratic table is a counterexample. In jointly normal populations, zero correlation is enough for independence, but that property belongs to the joint normal model, not to the definition of correlation. Recognizing when an implication has assumptions is part of statistical literacy.

15. Spearman rank correlation as a complementary description

Spearman's rho measures Pearson correlation of ranks, allowing a monotonic relationship to appear strongly associated even when it is not straight on the original scales. For the original six pairs X ranks are 1,2,3,4,5,6. Y=2,1,4,3,5,7 has ranks 2,1,4,3,5,6. The rank differences dᵢ=R(Xᵢ)−R(Yᵢ) are −1,+1,−1,+1,0,0. Hence Σdᵢ²=1+1+1+1+0+0=4. With no ties, Spearman's formula is \(\rho_s=1-\frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}\)=1−24/[6×35]=1−4/35=31/35≈0.885714.

Spearman rank correlation · no ties
\[\boxed{\rho_s=1-\frac{6\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}=1-\frac{24}{210}=\frac{31}{35}\approx0.885714}\]

This shortcut holds without ties; with ties use Pearson correlation of average ranks.

The rank coefficient is close to Pearson r≈0.890769 here, but that numerical agreement is contingent on this constructed sample. Spearman measures monotonic ordering: as X increases, does Y tend to increase or decrease, without requiring the increments to be constant? When ties occur, assign midranks and compute Pearson correlation of those ranks; the shortcut formula without tie adjustments no longer directly applies. A rank correlation is still descriptive and can still be confounded. Ranking cannot make a causal inference problem disappear.

Do not confuse Spearman's correlation with a nonparametric causal effect or a universal test of “any dependence.” A symmetric U shape is not monotonic, so Spearman can also be close to zero despite strong dependence. Compare scatterplots, Pearson r and rank summaries for sensitivity to shape and extremes. If the goal is inference for Spearman, the reference procedure should reflect sample size, ties and dependence rather than copying the t test for Pearson without justification. The full choice of rank-based tests belongs to Guide 49; here ranks mainly provide a contrasting association measure.

16. Conditional versus aggregate comparisons: a designed reversal

The second complete synthetic dataset contains six employee-weeks from two workplace shifts. In the high-strain shift A, X activity hours are 1,2,3 and Y fatigue scores are 8,9,10. In the lower-strain shift B, activity hours are 7,8,9 and fatigue scores are 1,2,3. Within each shift, fatigue rises by one point when activity rises by one hour, so each within-shift Pearson correlation is +1. Pool the shifts and the visual story changes: high activity is mostly found in the low-fatigue shift. This arrangement is purposefully constructed to demonstrate Simpson's paradox, not collected from employees.

Simpson reversal · within-group versus pooled
\[\begin{aligned}r_A&=+1,\qquad r_B=+1\\r_{\mathrm{pooled}}&=\frac{-59}{\sqrt{58(77.5)}}\approx-0.880009\end{aligned}\]

The direction changes because the pooled comparison mixes shifts with different average exposure and fatigue.

Across all six rows, x̄=(1+2+3+7+8+9)/6=30/6=5 hours. The overall fatigue mean is ȳ=(8+9+10+1+2+3)/6=33/6=5.5 points. The centered cross-product sum is −59 hour·points, the X squared-deviation sum is 58 hours², and the Y squared-deviation sum is 77.5 points². Therefore \(r_{\mathrm{pooled}}=-\frac{59}{\sqrt{58(77.5)}}\)≈−0.880009, a strong negative overall correlation. In contrast, r_A=+1 and r_B=+1 within the two shifts. The reversal does not arise from a mistake in arithmetic; it arises from changing which observations are compared.

The graphic should be read twice. First view both shifts using one color and recognize the downward overall pattern. Then compare the points within their two distinct color groups: each group rises. The pooled relationship answers “how do hours and fatigue co-vary among these six mixed-shift units?” The stratified relationships answer “how do hours and fatigue co-vary among units of a given shift?” They are not contradictory answers to one fixed question; they are answers to different comparisons. Confusing the comparisons is precisely the danger the example is designed to expose.

17. Prove the reversal with a covariance decomposition

The law of total covariance gives a deeper explanation: \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y\mid G)]+\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb E[X\mid G],\mathbb E[Y\mid G])\), where G is group or shift. The first term captures typical within-group co-movement; the second captures co-movement of group means. The identity assumes a well-defined mixture with the required moments. It shows how a negative between-group component can outweigh a positive within-group component, so the pooled covariance has the opposite sign.

Total covariance · the mechanism of Simpson reversal
\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)&=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y\mid G)]\\&\quad+\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb E[X\mid G],\mathbb E[Y\mid G])\\&=\frac23-10.5=-\frac{59}{6}\end{aligned}\]

Within-group positive covariance is outweighed by the negative covariance between group means.

For our six equally weighted observations, shift A has mean X=2, mean Y=9; shift B has mean X=8, mean Y=2. The within-shift population covariance in each three-point group is [(−1)(−1)+0+1×1]/3=2/3. Averaging the groups gives E[Cov(X,Y|G)]=2/3. The group means differ from the overall means (5,5.5) by (−3,+3.5) and (+3,−3.5). Their between-group covariance is [(-3)(+3.5)+(+3)(−3.5)]/2=−10.5. Thus pooled population covariance is 2/3−10.5=−9.833333. Multiply by n=6 to recover the centered cross-product total −59; divide by n−1=5 if you want the sample covariance −11.8.

This derivation is important because it replaces surprise with an auditable mechanism. Positive within-group patterns are overwhelmed by different group baselines and different X distributions. The data alone still cannot establish the causal roles of shift, activity and fatigue: shift might represent workload, selection into schedules, health status or several jointly varying influences. The Simpson phenomenon tells us that the aggregate coefficient is not invariant to conditioning; choosing which conditional comparison is causally appropriate requires domain knowledge and a causal design.

18. Confounding: beyond a mathematical grouping effect

A confounder is not merely any variable correlated with an outcome. In a causal question, a pre-exposure variable may create a noncausal path connecting exposure X and outcome Y when it affects both or is associated with their common causes. In the occupational scenario, shift G may affect opportunities for activity and fatigue through work intensity or sleep patterns. A diagram G→X and G→Y, alongside a possible X→Y pathway, expresses one hypothesized causal structure. This diagram is an assumption informed by scientific context, not something that the observed r proves.

An unadjusted association compares observations without conditioning on the candidate confounder. An adjusted association conditions or standardizes across covariate values using a suitable model or design. A conditional correlation is not automatically the causal effect, however: there may be unmeasured confounders, noisy instruments, misspecified functional forms, selection bias, or reverse causality. More importantly, conditioning on the wrong variable—such as a collider affected by both X and Y—can create bias that was not present before conditioning. “Adjust for everything available” is not a valid universal strategy.

Temporal ordering matters but does not by itself identify cause. If activity happens after fatigue rather than before it, the direction of any causal mechanism may be reversed. Randomization, when feasible and properly implemented, helps balance pre-treatment confounders by design; observational analyses require defensible identification assumptions and sensitivity to violations. This introductory guide does not claim to teach a full causal identification framework. Its narrower achievement is to show why a descriptive correlation, even with a tiny p-value, cannot be promoted directly into an intervention recommendation.

19. Causal diagrams, mediators, and colliders as conceptual safeguards

Consider three simple conceptual graphs. First, G→X and G→Y is the common-cause structure: G can confound the association between activity and fatigue. Second, X→M→Y describes a mediator M through which an effect of X might operate. Adjusting for M may remove part of a total causal effect, so the right adjustment depends on whether the target is total effect or a controlled/direct effect. Third, X→C←Y describes a collider C receiving arrows from both variables. Conditioning on C can induce an association even if X and Y were otherwise independent. These diagrams are hypotheses about data generation, not algorithms for reading causality off a scatterplot.

A practical example of a collider is selecting only workers who visit an occupational clinic when both high fatigue and high activity-related injury risk affect clinic attendance. The selected sample can show unexpected relationships solely because inclusion depends on two causes. A confounding diagram differs: there the concern is a common upstream driver of both recorded activity and fatigue. Two analyses might require different responses even though each contains a third variable. The diagram clarifies which variables are safe to adjust for and which require cautious reasoning.

Counterfactual language makes the causal target explicit: how would the same worker's fatigue differ under activity level x versus a different activity level x′, under otherwise defined conditions? We cannot directly observe both potential outcomes simultaneously for one worker in one period. A design or a set of identification assumptions is needed to infer their contrast from observable groups. Pearson correlation, Spearman correlation, Fisher intervals and even perfect prediction do not supply that design. Keep statistical evidence and the causal claim in separate sentences throughout your report.

20. Diagnostics and reporting workflow for genuine occupational data

A reproducible analysis begins with a written question and a unit definition. Specify whether X is recorded device-measured activity hours, a survey response, or a treatment assignment; define the fatigue instrument, scoring direction, reference period and clinically relevant threshold if one exists. State the target population, whether weeks are independent, and missingness rules. Then inspect raw paired observations, missing pairs, distributions, scatterplots with axes and units, and plausible group variables. Data errors may appear as impossible values, rounded clusters or duplicate identifiers. Resolve them with an auditable rule, not a goal of increasing correlation.

For descriptive analysis report n, Pearson r, a scatterplot and a check of sensitivity to extreme points and restricted ranges. If monotonic but nonlinear patterns are plausible, add Spearman as a complementary measure and explain what changed. If shift, age or job type may alter both exposure and outcome, provide stratified graphics and group summaries before writing causal language. Do not pool distinct populations merely because the software accepts a merged table. If performing inference, explain the sampling mechanism and whether bivariate normality and independence are defensible for the exact test and Fisher approximation; otherwise use an appropriately justified alternative and disclose the different assumptions.

A transparent report includes limitations and a decision statement. Example: “The illustrative data show a positive within-shift association but a negative pooled association. Because shift membership is strongly associated with both X and Y, the pooled coefficient is unsuitable as a standalone behavioral recommendation. These synthetic values cannot quantify effects for actual workers.” This conclusion is defensible without claiming that either within-group slope is the true causal effect. The essential habit is to report exactly what was estimated, from which units and comparison, and what additional evidence would be required for intervention.

20A. An additional worked example: the slope and r answer different questions

The sample slope of a simple least-squares regression of fatigue Y on activity X is b₁=Sxy/Sxx=18/17.5=36/35≈1.028571 score points per additional hour. Its intercept is b₀=ȳ−b₁x̄=11/3−(36/35)(3.5)=11/3−3.6=1/15≈0.066667 points. Thus the fitted line is ŷ≈0.066667+1.028571x. This is an algebraic projection onto a straight line: the line minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals for the six sample pairs. It is not a physical law, a causal structural equation, or proof that imposing activity changes a person's fatigue.

Regression slope and correlation are different
\[\begin{aligned}b_1&=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\frac{18}{17.5}=\frac{36}{35}\\b_0&=\bar y-b_1\bar x=\frac1{15}\\\hat y&=\frac1{15}+\frac{36}{35}x\end{aligned}\]

The slope has fatigue-points-per-hour units; Pearson r has no units.

The relationship between slope and correlation makes their roles transparent: \(b_1=r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x}\). Here sᵧ/sₓ≈2.1602/1.8708≈1.1547 points/hour and r≈0.89077, yielding b₁≈1.02857 points/hour. If X were converted from hours to minutes, the numerical slope would divide by 60 because one minute is one-sixtieth of an hour; r would remain 0.89077. If we instead fit X as a response to Y, the new slope would be Sxy/Syy=18/(70/3)=27/35≈0.77143 hours/point. The two regression slopes are not reciprocal unless |r|=1. Neither change affects the symmetry of Pearson correlation, but it does affect the question answered by a regression.

Calculate the total centered variation Syy=70/3. The linear model's regression sum of squares is SSR=Sxy²/Sxx=18²/17.5=648/35≈18.514286. Its residual sum of squares is SSE=Syy−SSR≈23.333333−18.514286≈4.819048. Therefore R²=SSR/Syy≈0.793469=r². The residuals are deviations between observed Y values and the fitted values, not unexplained causal influences. When presenting R², say “fraction of observed sample Y sum of squares captured by this one fitted linear predictor with intercept,” rather than “fraction of fatigue caused by activity.” With a different sample, nonlinear model or validation population, this number may change appreciably.

20B. Why missingness and repeated measurements can change the estimand

An apparently innocuous data-cleaning decision can create a different population comparison. Suppose an activity tracker records missing values preferentially for employees working the most demanding shifts. A complete-case calculation uses only pairs with both X and Y present. If completeness depends on shift, fatigue or their joint behavior, the correlation among complete cases can differ from the target-population correlation. Merely increasing n after imputation does not repair the issue unless the missingness model and uncertainty are appropriately handled. Document how many pairs were available, which units were excluded, why data were missing if known, and which assumptions justify any missing-data method.

Repeated measures introduce another distinction. Imagine ten employees each measured weekly for twelve weeks. A pooled correlation over 120 rows combines between-person differences (some people typically exercise more than others) and within-person changes (a particular person exercises more than usual this week). These can have different signs, just as the two shifts did. The independence assumption of the simple t test fails when several weeks belong to the same individual. A scientifically meaningful analysis might distinguish person-mean activity from deviations around each person's mean, with methods designed for longitudinal or clustered data. A conventional Pearson r over the 120 rows can remain a descriptive statistic, but it should not be labeled the same estimand as a within-person association or used with ordinary independent-pair standard errors.

This is not a technicality that disappears with a sufficiently small p-value. The target question determines whether we should compare different people, the same person over time, shifts at a given time, or a designed intervention. Some of these questions require additional models, some require more independent units, and some cannot be resolved from the recorded variables. Before seeking more precision, define the estimand in words. If the scientific target is a within-person change, a between-person association, however precise, is not a substitute.

20C. Partial association is not automatically causal adjustment

One common response to suspected confounding is to estimate a partial correlation, that is, association between X and Y after removing certain linear relationships with a third variable Z. In an elementary linear residualization approach, regress X on Z and keep its residuals, then regress Y on Z and keep its residuals; calculate Pearson correlation of the two residual lists. This produces a conditional linear association under the chosen model. It does not automatically control nonlinear confounding, measurement error, unrecorded common causes, selection bias or inappropriate adjustments. In particular, if Z is a collider or a mediator relative to the causal question, residualization can target the wrong comparison.

Partial correlation · algebra is not causal identification
\[\boxed{r_{XY\cdot Z}=\frac{r_{XY}-r_{XZ}r_{YZ}}{\sqrt{(1-r_{XZ}^2)(1-r_{YZ}^2)}}}\]

Positive denominator factors are required; conditioning on the wrong variable can introduce bias.

A formula for one control variable helps illustrate both power and limitations: \(r_{XY\cdot Z}=\frac{r_{XY}-r_{XZ}r_{YZ}}{\sqrt{(1-r_{XZ}^2)(1-r_{YZ}^2)}}\), provided both denominator factors are positive. Here the centered dots denote correlations, not causal arrows. Imagine an illustrative correlation triple rₓᵧ=.60, rₓz=.80, rᵧz=.75. The numerator is .60−(.80)(.75)=0; the denominator is √[(1−.64)(1−.5625)]>0, so the partial correlation is zero. All three numbers are stipulated for this algebra illustration, not calculated from a hidden dataset. The removal of an association after controlling Z might be consistent with confounding, but it is not a proof that Z was a common cause.

In practice, examine whether Z was recorded before exposure, how reliably it was measured, whether there is overlap of X values within Z strata, whether Z might be caused by X, and whether the study selected observations by a consequence of both variables. Even a perfectly computed partial correlation does not equal a causal intervention effect. For a valid causal interpretation, the assumptions connecting the adjusted observable comparison to potential outcomes must be stated and defended independently of arithmetic. This is why “the correlation disappeared after adjustment” is a useful diagnostic statement but not, by itself, a discovery of the true mechanism.

20D. Sensitivity, sign, and decisions in context

Correlations are sometimes evaluated using generic labels such as weak, moderate and strong. Those labels have no invariant policy meaning. In a low-noise laboratory setting, r=.20 could be scientifically important; in a high-stakes medical recommendation, r=.90 from biased data could be practically unusable. The magnitude must be related to measured units, reliability, target population, uncertainty, consequences and plausible mechanisms. A negative correlation is not inherently undesirable: for an effective training program, increased practice and fewer errors might be welcome. A positive correlation can be beneficial, harmful or irrelevant depending on variable definitions.

Consider measurement noise. If true physical activity and true fatigue are measured imprecisely, observed correlations may be attenuated or otherwise distorted depending on the error structure. If a fatigue instrument has an upper ceiling, distinct high-fatigue values may be compressed into identical scores, hiding variation. If one subgroup has virtually no variation in X, its correlation may be unstable or undefined; comparing it with a broad-range subgroup needs caution. Reporting confidence intervals does not correct these instrument problems. A well-designed study reports the reliability, range, resolution, and direction of both variables and examines whether systematic errors vary by shift.

Finally, decisions are not determined by the sign of r alone. An employer considering a voluntary activity program would need evidence about safety, possible heterogeneous effects, costs, equity across shifts, compliance, and outcomes over time. A randomized or otherwise defensible causal study may help estimate the intervention effect; an observational correlation may help identify questions worth studying. Reporting a carefully bounded descriptive result is a successful statistical outcome, not a failure to reach an exciting conclusion. Good reasoning specifies the question that the available evidence does answer and names the missing assumptions before making a stronger claim.

21. Exercise 1 — rebuild Pearson r without software

Problem. Given X=(1,2,3,4,5,6) hours and Y=(2,1,4,3,5,7) points, construct means, centered sums, covariance, standard deviations and Pearson r. Do not simply quote the answer from a software package. Solution. First x̄=3.5 and ȳ=11/3. Next obtain Sxy=Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)=18, Sxx=17.5, and Syy=70/3. With n=6, sample covariance equals Sxy/(n−1)=18/5=3.6 hour·points. Sample standard deviations are √(17.5/5)=√3.5 hours and √[(70/3)/5]=√(14/3) points. Therefore r=18/√[17.5×(70/3)]=18√3/35≈0.890769.

Worked exercise 1 · reproducible result
\[\begin{aligned}S_{xy}&=18,\quad S_{xx}=17.5,\quad S_{yy}=\frac{70}{3}\\r&=\frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}=\frac{18\sqrt3}{35}\approx0.890769\end{aligned}\]

The numerator and both squared sums can be rebuilt from the complete six-row dataset.

Verification. Raw sums give Σxy=95 and ΣxΣy/n=77, producing 18. Interpretation. The data contain a strong positive linear sample association. It does not imply 0.891 fatigue points per activity hour; that would require a slope in points/hour, not a unitless correlation. Transfer. If fatigue score units change by a positive factor, r remains the same because the standardization cancels the factor. If activity becomes a constant for all six workers, r is undefined because Sxx=0. Reproduce both conclusions verbally without calculating a new coefficient.

22. Exercise 2 — test the population correlation under a defined model

Problem. Treat the six pairs as independent draws from a bivariate-normal population, set α=0.05 and test H₀:ρ=0 against H₁:ρ≠0. Solution. With r=18√3/35, r²=972/1225. Since n−2=4, t=r√[4/(1−r²)]=(18√3/35)√[4/(253/1225)]=36√3/√253≈3.920. The test uses T with four degrees of freedom under H₀. The two-sided p-value is approximately 0.0172455. Because 0.0172455<0.05, reject the null within the assumed model.

Worked exercise 2 · null test
\[\begin{aligned}t&=\frac{18\sqrt3}{35}\sqrt{\frac4{1-972/1225}}\\&=\frac{36\sqrt3}{\sqrt{253}}\approx3.920,\quad p\approx0.0172455\end{aligned}\]

This is a model-conditional significance test, not evidence that an intervention would work.

Verification and interpretation. A numerical t survival function should reproduce the probability. Rejection means that the observations are inconsistent with exactly zero population linear correlation under the stated sample/model assumptions. It does not rule out hidden workplace confounding or make the relationship causal. Transfer. If the six rows came from two workers measured on three weeks each, n=6 independent pairs would be unjustified and the p-value would not retain its claimed calibration. State which assumption failed before trying a different test.

23. Exercise 3 — construct and interpret a Fisher interval

Problem. Compute the approximate 95% Fisher-z interval for ρ using the same six pairs. Solution. First z=½ln[(1+r)/(1−r)]≈1.425. Next SE=1/√(6−3)=1/√3≈0.57735. The endpoints on the z scale are z−1.96SE and z+1.96SE. Transform each via tanh; the resulting interval is approximately [0.28584,0.98805]. The endpoints remain in [−1,1], as a correlation interval must. The very high upper limit and relatively modest lower limit disclose substantial imprecision despite the visually strong sample association.

Worked exercise 3 · approximate interval
\[\begin{aligned}z&\approx1.425,\quad \operatorname{SE}(z)=\frac1{\sqrt3}\\\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\rho)&\approx\tanh(z\pm1.96/\sqrt3)\\&\approx[0.28584,\;0.98805]\end{aligned}\]

Both endpoints come from back-transforming the Fisher-z limits.

Interpretation and transfer. The claimed 95% coverage is approximate under the Fisher model, and n=6 is small enough to warrant caution. Do not say there is a 95% posterior probability that this fixed ρ lies in the observed interval. If a second study has n=30 and r=0.50, recompute z=0.549306 and SE=0.19245; its interval is roughly [0.170,0.729]. Greater n can improve precision even when observed r is smaller. Neither interval estimates a causal effect.

24. Exercise 4 — recognize dependence without Pearson correlation

Problem. Examine X=(−2,−1,0,1,2) and Y=(4,1,0,1,4). Is there no relationship because Pearson's r is zero? Solution. Compute x̄=0 and ȳ=2. The centered products are −4,+1,0,−1,+4, summing to zero. Both variables have nonzero sums of squared deviations, hence r=0. However Y=X² for every pair, so the relationship is exact and nonlinear. A scatterplot would form a symmetric U shape.

Verification and interpretation. Explain why a positive X deviation can occur with either a high or low Y deviation depending on which part of the curve is visited. The covariance cancellations are real, not a computational error. Transfer. If the scientific question concerns predictability or dependence rather than straight-line co-movement, choose diagnostics or models sensitive to curvature. Neither zero Pearson r nor zero Spearman rho provides a universal certificate of independence.

25. Exercise 5 — calculate Spearman by ranks

Problem. Using the original six pairs, calculate Spearman's rank correlation and compare it with Pearson r. Solution. X ranks are (1,2,3,4,5,6); Y ranks are (2,1,4,3,5,6). Their paired rank differences are (−1,1,−1,1,0,0), with squared sum 4. With no tied ranks, ρₛ=1−6×4/[6×(36−1)]=1−24/210=31/35≈0.885714. Pearson is approximately 0.890769. Both show a positive association of order, and both are descriptive summaries of these constructed observations.

Worked exercise 5 · ranks
\[\rho_s=1-\frac{6(4)}{6(6^2-1)}=\frac{31}{35}\approx0.885714\]

The rank differences are −1,+1,−1,+1,0,0 and their squares add to 4.

Verification and transfer. Correlate the two rank vectors using the same centered-product Pearson formula to check the result. If two fatigue scores tie, replace their integer positions with the average rank and recompute Pearson on ranks; do not blindly reuse the untied shortcut. If an observation has enormous measured X but only the largest rank, rank correlation uses ordinal position rather than physical distance, but selection and confounding can still bias interpretation.

26. Exercise 6 — expose Simpson's paradox numerically

Problem. Shift A contains pairs (1,8),(2,9),(3,10); shift B contains (7,1),(8,2),(9,3). Compute within-shift and pooled Pearson correlations. Solution. In each shift Y=X+7 for A and Y=X−6 for B, so centered deviations are identical up to positive scaling: r_A=r_B=+1. For the six pooled observations, x̄=5 and ȳ=5.5. The centered cross-product sum is −59; squared sums are 58 and 77.5. Thus \(r_{\mathrm{pooled}}=-\frac{59}{\sqrt{58(77.5)}}\)≈−0.880009. Each calculation is correct for its specific comparison.

Worked exercise 6 · group reversal
\[\begin{aligned}r_A&=r_B=1\\r_{\mathrm{pooled}}&=\frac{-59}{\sqrt{58\times77.5}}\approx-0.880009\end{aligned}\]

The within-shift and pooled comparisons answer different descriptive questions.

Interpretation. Pooled activity and fatigue are negatively associated because the high-activity shift has a much lower fatigue baseline. Within each shift the constructed relation is positive. Transfer. Make a two-panel plot, one panel per shift, and compare it with the pooled plot. A causal recommendation to increase or decrease activity does not follow from either view without identifying what shift represents and how workers were assigned to it.

27. Exercise 7 — independently verify the covariance decomposition

Problem. For the same six shift observations, confirm the pooled centered cross-product sum using within- and between-shift components. Solution. Each group has mean-centered X and Y vectors (−1,0,1), with within-group population covariance [(−1)(−1)+0+1×1]/3=2/3. The average within component is 2/3. Group means are (2,9) and (8,2); overall means are (5,5.5). Hence between-group covariance is [(−3)(3.5)+(3)(−3.5)]/2=−10.5. Add 2/3−10.5=−59/6. Multiply by n=6 to get Sxy=−59.

Worked exercise 7 · check total covariance
\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)&=\frac23-10.5=-\frac{59}{6}\\S_{xy}&=6\!\left(-\frac{59}{6}\right)=-59\end{aligned}\]

The result independently matches the raw centered cross-product sum −59.

Verification. The raw-sum identity independently gives Σxy=106 and ΣxΣy/n=(30×33)/6=165, yielding 106−165=−59. Interpretation and transfer. A negative component from differences between groups overwhelms positive co-movement within them. Explain in your own words what an aggregate correlation hides when groups differ in their typical exposure and baseline outcome.

28. Exercise 8 — distinguish confounder, mediator and collider

Problem. Consider the statements (a) night shift causes both reduced exercise time and increased fatigue; (b) exercise may change sleep quality, which changes fatigue; (c) fatigue and injury each increase the probability of visiting a workplace clinic. Sketch causal arrows and decide whether an automatic “adjust for all variables” rule is justified. Solution. (a) corresponds to G→X and G→Y, a possible common cause. (b) corresponds to X→M→Y, a mediation pathway; controlling for M may remove part of the total effect. (c) corresponds to X→C←Y if X represents an injury-related exposure; selecting clinic visitors conditions on a collider C and can induce bias.

Interpretation and transfer. The arrows are scientific assumptions that require external justification and perhaps revision. Data alone cannot tell us that a third variable is always a confounder. The adjustment decision follows the target causal effect and graph. If the goal is simply to describe clinic visitors, conditional correlation can be legitimate as description, but it still should not be mistaken for the population-wide causal effect of activity.

29. Exercise 9 — a responsible inference report

Problem. A colleague writes, “Activity causes fatigue: r=0.89, p<0.05. Therefore prescribe less activity.” Rewrite the conclusion using the six-point synthetic example and state what information a real study would need. Solution. “In six synthetic employee-week pairs, activity and fatigue have Pearson r≈0.891. Assuming independent bivariate-normal sampling, the two-sided test of zero population correlation gives t(4)≈3.920, p≈0.0172; an approximate Fisher 95% confidence interval is [0.286,0.988]. These numbers illustrate a positive association, not a causal effect; no actual employees were sampled.” This version states design assumptions and measurement provenance rather than asserting an intervention recommendation.

Transfer. In real data, request the sampling frame, activity instrument, fatigue scale, missingness, repeated observations, workplace shift and workload measures, scatterplot, sensitivity to unusual points and a defensible identification strategy before suggesting any change to workplace policy. A low p-value does not repair a causal identification failure. The strongest scientific communication is often one that precisely limits its claim.

30. Exercise 10 — final independent transfer and Student Understanding Test

Problem. A company measures physical activity and a stress scale in 120 weekly records from 20 employees. The pooled Pearson r is −0.35. On a shift-colored plot, both shifts show gently positive trends; several workers contribute many records, and one instrument has a ceiling at 10 points. Draft a complete analysis plan that distinguishes description, population inference and a possible causal effect. Solution. First establish that the true independent sampling units may be 20 people rather than 120 employee-weeks; list record counts by worker, shift and time. Describe the scale's ceiling, because truncation can distort linear association. Plot paired observations with worker identifiers or appropriate aggregation and shift groups. Report pooled and shift-specific associations, group sizes, available measurement ranges, missingness and sensitivity to extreme records. Recheck whether X and Y are measured in the same time window and whether activity follows or precedes stress.

For inference, do not automatically use t with 118 degrees of freedom: within-worker correlation violates the simple independent-pair assumptions. Choose an approach justified for clustered/repeated measures and document its uncertainty; ordinary Fisher-z standard errors are not automatically valid. For causality, identify plausible common causes such as workload, shift assignment and baseline health, plus mediators and selection variables; specify the target intervention and consider randomization or defensible observational assumptions. End by reporting association as association unless identification warrants a stronger claim. Student Understanding Test: “If I were learning this subject for the first time, could I follow every reasoning step, reproduce the calculations and explain in my own words why it works?” Verify that you can calculate the six-point r, reproduce the t test and Fisher interval, derive the sign reversal, and explain why none of them alone identifies a causal effect.

31. Final synthesis: three distinct layers of a statistical claim

A descriptive layer summarizes the observed paired data: its scatterplot, centered cross-products, Pearson r, perhaps Spearman's rho, subgroup associations and unusual observations. A population-inference layer asks about a target population parameter and requires a defensible sampling and model story, with a test or interval calibrated to the actual dependence structure. A causal layer asks what would happen under an intervention and requires additional design or identification assumptions. These layers can coexist in a single study, but they cannot be collapsed into the statement “the coefficient is significant, therefore X causes Y.” Their separation is the central skill of this guide.

The complete first dataset leads to x̄=3.5, ȳ=11/3, Sxy=18, Sxx=17.5, Syy=70/3 and r=18√3/35≈0.890769. The classical null test gives t(4)≈3.920 and p≈0.0172455, and the approximate Fisher interval spans [0.28584,0.98805]. The second dataset produces two positive within-shift correlations of +1 but an overall r≈−0.880009; its covariance decomposition shows a positive within component 2/3 and a negative between component −10.5. All values come from explicitly constructed teaching data. Their purpose is to make distinctions reproducible, not to establish facts about workers.

Before author approval, repeat the final Student Understanding Test without consulting a formula sheet: can you explain why standardization removes units, why r cannot exceed one, when t is exact, why Fisher is approximate, why zero r can coexist with X² dependence, and why a conditioning decision requires causal reasoning? If any answer relies only on remembered phrasing, reconstruct the numbers and reason from the paired units. That is the intended transition from mechanical calculation to defensible statistical interpretation.
