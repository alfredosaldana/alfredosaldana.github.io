01. Why ARIMA is needed after ARMA

A distribution center counts the number of parcels processed each month. It is tempting to use yesterday's statistical models unchanged on those counts, but AR and MA formulas from Guide 68 were introduced for a series whose average behavior was stable over time. Parcel volumes often drift upward as a business grows, even when short-term rises and falls remain correlated. ARIMA combines a transformation of the observed series—usually differencing—with a stochastic model for the resulting fluctuations. Its letters mean Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average; “integrated” refers to reversing differences to return to the level of the original measurements, not to integrating an area under a curve.

A useful forecasting model must answer four questions. What is the quantity being forecast, and when is it observed? Which transformation makes its dependence amenable to a relatively stable ARMA description? Which values of p, d, and q are supported by the data and by a parsimonious explanation? Finally, does the model produce better forecasts than a sensible simple alternative at the same horizons, with uncertainty that is honestly described? These questions organize the entire guide. None can be replaced by accepting the smallest reported AIC.

Our numerical case uses 48 synthetic, illustrative monthly observations of parcel volume, expressed in thousands of parcels. The observations were constructed by integrating serially dependent changes; they were not collected from a real warehouse. Months 1–36 are the training period and months 37–48 are held out for forecast evaluation. The generating recipe and all observations are supplied so every numerical claim can be reconstructed. A favorable result on this one series is a demonstration of method, not evidence of universal superiority or a causal effect of business decisions.

Core equation
\[\operatorname{ARIMA}(p,d,q):\quad (1-B)^dY_t\ \text{follows an ARMA}(p,q)\]

02. Prerequisites: indexes, conditional information and innovations

Write Y_t for monthly volume in thousands of parcels and t for the month index. Y_{t-1} is the previous month's volume; it is not a second independently sampled warehouse. The lag operator B takes a series one time step backward, BY_t=Y_{t-1}. A difference is therefore (1-B)Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}. The information set available when making a prediction at month T includes Y_1 through Y_T and permitted external information, but never Y_{T+1}. Students who have taken Calculus I can interpret the difference as a discrete change, analogous to—but distinct from—a derivative.

A stationary short-run model has a stable mean and covariance structure. An innovation ε_t has mean zero, variance σ², and no linear predictability from the designated past. Independent Gaussian innovations form a stronger assumption than uncorrelated finite-variance innovations. ARIMA prediction equations often rely on conditional means and covariances even when Gaussianity is merely an estimation convenience. Distinguish true unobserved innovations ε_t from the residuals calculated after fitting finite data. Residual tests reveal evidence about a fitted model, not a logical proof of its adequacy.

The three model orders answer different questions: p is the number of autoregressive lags in the transformed series, d the number of ordinary differences, and q the number of moving-average innovation lags. All are nonnegative integers. The model described here is nonseasonal; dependence recurring every twelve months is the separate subject of Guide 70. The mean and drift conventions vary across statistical packages, so an intercept must always be interpreted in the exact parameterization being used.

Core equation
\[BY_t=Y_{t-1},\qquad \Delta Y_t=(1-B)Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}\]

04. First differences: level versus increment

Differencing removes one accumulated unit-root component, but does not magically make every dataset stationary. Define W_t=ΔY_t=Y_t−Y_{t-1} for t from 2 onward. Using the displayed numbers, W_36≈291.680−293.523=−1.843 thousand parcels and W_37≈285.745−291.680=−5.934 thousand parcels. The apparent slight disagreement with the last decimal arises because the table displays rounded levels. The exact values in the verification script should be used when checking identities to higher precision.

A process can have an upward overall history while occasional differences are negative. ARIMA(d=1) models the conditional dynamics of W_t and then *integrates* future differences by addition. A stationary ARMA model for W_t is a statement about a hypothetical repeatable process, not merely a pleasing difference plot. Examine possible changes in average change, changing volatility, unusual interventions and residual dependence before calling the transformation sufficient.

Differencing twice yields Δ²Y_t=Y_t−2Y_{t-1}+Y_{t-2}, which describes changes in changes, not the original level. Choosing d=2 without evidence can increase forecast uncertainty and create spurious negative first-lag autocorrelation. Conversely, if the actual fluctuations are stationary around a deterministic trend, fitting a regression with trend and stationary errors may be more appropriate than differencing. Unit-root diagnostics, visual analysis, domain knowledge and predictive checks must be considered together.

Core equation
\[\Delta^2Y_t=(1-B)^2Y_t=Y_t-2Y_{t-1}+Y_{t-2}\]

05. The full ARIMA equation and its symbols

After taking d differences, let W_t=(1−B)^dY_t. One common centered specification is (1−φ_1B−⋯−φ_pB^p)(W_t−μ)=(1+θ_1B+⋯+θ_qB^q)ε_t. The φ coefficients describe persistence in differenced observations; θ coefficients describe how current and earlier unpredictable shocks combine; μ is the unconditional mean of the stationary differenced process when it exists; ε_t has innovation variance σ². The polynomial notation is simply an organized version of ordinary equations indexed by time.

For example, an ARIMA(1,1,0) with mean change c satisfies W_t−c=φ(W_{t−1}−c)+ε_t. Expanding gives W_t=c(1−φ)+φW_{t−1}+ε_t. Students should notice that c is the long-run mean difference, whereas the intercept in the expanded autoregression is c(1−φ). Those two numbers are generally unequal. In some software, a coefficient called trend or Drift plays the role of c; in other implementations an intercept is attached to a different transformation. Reporting only 'intercept=3' without giving the model equation is ambiguous.

The corresponding ARIMA(0,1,1) has W_t=c+ε_t+θε_{t−1}. A past shock is not itself a past observed volume. The innovation must be filtered from the model using observations and estimated parameters. These distinctions explain why the one-step forecast for an MA component needs a fitted residual or filtered state; simply plugging Y_{t−1} into its place is mathematically incorrect.

Core equation
\[(1-\phi B)(\Delta Y_t-c)=(1+\theta B)\varepsilon_t\]

06. Drift, constants and the random-walk baseline

The simplest ARIMA(0,1,0) without Drift is Y_t=Y_{t−1}+ε_t, a random walk. Its best conditional mean forecast at every horizon h is the last observed level Y_T; its forecast-error variance is hσ² under uncorrelated innovations of common variance. With Drift c, Y_t=Y_{t−1}+c+ε_t and forecast Y_{T+h|T}=Y_T+hc. The growth is linear in horizon, while uncertainty increases with √h. This is a direct consequence of summing future shocks, not an arbitrary feature of a software command.

A drift estimate can be obtained from the average training differences, (Y_36−Y_1)/35 in the present sample if fitting by that simple sample average. In our training data, Y_1≈189.200 and Y_36≈291.680, so the average change is approximately 102.480/35=2.928 thousand parcels per month. ARIMA(0,1,0) with a linear trend fitted by maximum likelihood returns approximately the same 2.928. At the month-36 forecast origin, the one-step prediction is approximately 294.608 thousand parcels, whereas the actual month 37 is 285.745. The error is approximately −8.862 thousand parcels (actual minus forecast).

The no-Drift and Drift baselines answer different practical questions. The first predicts no expected level change; the second extends historical average growth. Neither requires estimating AR or MA persistence. They are essential comparators because an elaborate ARIMA is useful only if its additional complexity produces a defensible improvement in the relevant evaluation protocol. A baseline is not a straw man: it sets the minimum standard of practical evidence.

Core equation
\[\widehat Y_{T+h\mid T}=Y_T+hc,\quad\operatorname{Var}(Y_{T+h}-\widehat Y_{T+h\mid T})=h\sigma^2\]

07. One-step and multi-step ARIMA(1,1,0) forecasts

Let W_t=ΔY_t and suppose W_t−c=φ(W_{t−1}−c)+ε_t with |φ|<1. At an origin T, the future innovation has conditional expectation zero. Therefore the next predicted change is c+φ(W_T−c), while the h-step predicted change is c+φ^h(W_T−c). The level forecast accumulates predicted changes: Yhat_{T+h|T}=Y_T+hc+(W_T−c)Σ_{j=1}^hφ^j. For φ≠1 the sum equals φ(1−φ^h)/(1−φ). Each term has units of thousands of parcels, and φ is dimensionless.

A self-contained teaching calculation uses Y_T=291.680, latest change W_T=−1.843, c=2.9 and φ=0.4. The next change forecast is 2.9+0.4(−1.843−2.9)=1.0028; the next level is 292.6828. The second change forecast is 2.9+0.4²(−4.743)=2.14112, so Yhat_{T+2|T}=294.82392. The second forecast does *not* use the actual Y_{T+1}, which was unknown at origin T. If one updates after observing that month, one is conducting a new one-step forecast, not the original two-step forecast.

These arithmetic values illustrate a chosen parameter set, not the numerical fitted estimates from the 48-month table. In the independent fitted training comparison, ARIMA(1,1,0) estimates long-run change around 2.9185, AR coefficient about 0.1004, and produces first held-out forecast approximately 294.120. Keeping the hand-calculation example separate from the fitted model avoids hiding how estimation affects the actual prediction.

Core equation
\[\widehat{Y}_{T+h\mid T}=Y_T+hc+(W_T-c)\sum_{j=1}^{h}\phi^j\]

08. ARIMA(0,1,1) and filtered innovations

For ARIMA(0,1,1), W_t=c+ε_t+θε_{t−1}. Given information through month T and a usable filtered innovation estimate e_T, the one-step expected change is c+θe_T. At horizons two and beyond, the expected change is c because new future innovations have conditional mean zero. The level forecasts are consequently Yhat_{T+h|T}=Y_T+hc+θe_T for all h≥1 under this convention. The shock adjustment enters the cumulative level once, not h times.

Suppose c=3, θ=0.5, e_T=−4 and Y_T=291.680, all in their appropriate units. At h=1 the expected change is 3+0.5(−4)=1, yielding 292.680. At h=2, the next predicted change is 3, so the two-month-ahead level is 295.680. A common incorrect computation repeats the −2 shock contribution every month and obtains 293.680 at h=2. The mistake comes from confusing a one-period innovation effect with a permanent drift shift.

Estimating the innovation e_T requires an initialization or a likelihood-based state-filtering convention. With finite observations, the conditional residual, exact innovations and smoothed state are not automatically identical. This is why the worked forecast explains its conditioning assumptions and why the fitted model output must be checked using the statistical package's definition. A forecast cannot simply substitute the previous volume for ε_T; those quantities differ in their meaning, measurement and expected behavior.

Core equation
\[\widehat{Y}_{T+h\mid T}=Y_T+hc+\theta\widehat\varepsilon_T,\qquad h\geq1\]

09. Identification: ACF and PACF are clues, not verdicts

When the level drifts, its sample autocorrelation often decays slowly for reasons related to integration rather than a high-order stationary AR mechanism. First inspect the time plot, first differences, sample ACF and PACF of the transformed series. A stationary AR(1) commonly produces an ACF that tails off geometrically and PACF that cuts off after the first lag in the ideal population model. A stationary MA(1) has a population ACF that cuts off after one lag and a PACF that tails off. An ARMA process commonly shows tails in both. These are *population patterns* under specific assumptions, not rigid templates in 35 noisy sample differences.

For the fitted logistics data, considering p and q up to two is a deliberately limited, transparent candidate search, not a guarantee that higher orders are wrong. Differencing d=1 is motivated by the integrated generating construction and inspected changes; in a real dataset one would evaluate unit roots, breaks, trend alternatives and over-differencing. An order is a modeling hypothesis. The useful evidence comes from fitted coefficients, residuals, holdout forecasts and sensitivity, considered together.

Two candidate models can have similar ACF shapes and produce quite different parameter estimates or interval widths. In small samples, sample autocorrelations are correlated estimates and approximate ±1.96/√n visual bands are only rough guides under restricted null assumptions. Counting a few stems outside the bands should not automatically determine p or q. If the pattern is seasonal, ordinary ARIMA cannot be assumed sufficient; investigate the seasonal model family only when the appropriate curriculum and evidence justify it.

10. Maximum likelihood and initialization

For Gaussian innovations, a fitted ARIMA uses the joint probability of the observed time series under a particular model to estimate unknown parameters. The Likelihood is a function of candidate parameters with the observations fixed; it is not the probability that a parameter value is true. For a simple conditional AR(1) process of changes, residuals after an initial state are e_t=W_t−c−φ(W_{t−1}−c). The conditional Gaussian log-likelihood contains a term proportional to −(n/2)log(σ²) and −Σe_t²/(2σ²), plus constants and terms determined by initialization. Minimizing a simple sum of squares and maximizing the exact state-space Gaussian likelihood are not automatically identical in short samples.

Exact or state-space likelihood methods integrate uncertainty about initial states under specified conventions. Conditional sum-of-squares methods condition on selected starting values. The number of initial observations effectively used can change both the likelihood and a model's reported AIC. Therefore comparing information criteria requires compatible data windows, transformations, response variables and likelihood conventions. If one candidate silently drops more starting observations, a smaller AIC might reflect a different comparison problem rather than a better model.

In the accompanying verification script we fit using the `statsmodels.tsa.arima.model.ARIMA` class, with a linear time trend for d=1 to represent mean changes. It returns coefficient estimates, innovation variance, log-likelihood and information criteria. These are estimated outputs, not algebraic consequences of the recipe alone. A numerical optimizer may fail to converge, meet parameter boundaries or return fragile standard errors; the responsible report records warnings, initialization choices and whether the optimization reached a credible solution before treating the resulting model as usable.

11. Information criteria: derive AIC and AICc

Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) balances in-sample fit against parameter complexity: AIC=−2ℓ(θhat)+2k, where ℓ is the maximized log-likelihood and k counts the free estimated parameters included in the criterion. Its smaller-is-better interpretation is relative among comparable models, not a universal goodness certificate. When samples are modest, a common small-sample correction is AICc=AIC+2k(k+1)/(n−k−1), provided the denominator is positive. The particular n appropriate to an ARIMA likelihood must be documented. Software can differ in effective sample treatment and parameter counts.

For a numerical check, one fitted ARIMA(1,1,1) candidate in our training data has AIC≈211.008 and k=4 reported parameters (linear trend, AR1, MA1, innovation variance). With n=36 under our explicitly declared textbook correction, the added penalty is 2×4×5/(36−4−1)=40/31≈1.2903; AICc≈212.298. This is a transparent *illustrative correction convention*, not a claim that every software library calculates AICc on integrated data identically. For ARIMA(0,1,2), AIC≈208.049, k=4 and AICc≈209.340 using the same convention.

That lower AICc means the latter model is preferred by that training-only information criterion among these candidates. It says nothing by itself about performance at one-month or twelve-month horizons. Furthermore, a difference of two AIC points is not a p-value, a probability of causality or an automatically meaningful economic improvement. Models whose likelihoods are calculated on different response scales or samples should not have their AIC values compared naively. We will test forecasting claims on a reserved chronological period next.

Core equation
\[\mathrm{AIC}=-2\ell(\widehat\theta)+2k,\quad\mathrm{AICc}=\mathrm{AIC}+\frac{2k(k+1)}{n-k-1}\]

12. Candidate model table and conflicting criteria

The following table compares six nonseasonal specifications, each estimated using only months 1–36 and the same implementation. Every candidate uses d=1 and a linear time trend representing a potential Drift. The AIC values and estimates were obtained by an independent `statsmodels` calculation; they are rounded displays rather than hand-chosen targets. ARIMA(0,1,2) gives the lowest AIC among these six, around 208.049. ARIMA(1,1,1) gives approximately 211.008; the simple Drift model gives about 213.933.

The table deliberately places training AIC beside two different holdout errors. The first is a fixed-origin twelve-month forecast: fit at month 36, forecast months 37–48 without updating, and then compute MAE on those twelve horizons. The second is rolling one-step evaluation: for each origin through month 47, refit using all observations actually available and forecast only the next month. These are not interchangeable error estimates. A fixed-origin twelve-month MAE weights long-horizon errors, whereas rolling one-step MAE measures the performance of an updating operation.

Our lesson does not declare a universal winner. ARIMA(0,1,2), despite leading in training AIC, has rolling one-step MAE around 4.7 thousand parcels. ARIMA(1,1,1) has rolling one-step MAE around 4.02. But a naive forecast based on the last observation has rolling one-step MAE around 4.53. Whether the 0.51 reduction versus naive is worth an optimized model, monitoring cost and parameter fragility requires repeated datasets, uncertainty assessment and operational context. The holdout is too short to claim stable dominance.

13. Chronological backtesting and information leakage

A time-series backtest is defined by its forecast origins, training history, horizon and update policy. At origin month 36, the model must be fitted using months 1–36 only. For one-step rolling evaluation, the next origin includes month 37 *after it has happened*, then predicts month 38. The model at origin 37 must not learn from month 38 or any later months. This seems obvious until preprocessing, automatic order selection or standardized features are computed using the whole 48-month file.

We evaluate months 37–48 as a holdout that was conceptually reserved before model specification. Our verification calculation refits the candidate parameters at each origin, which is an expanding-window scheme. A fixed-window design would discard the oldest month as the origin advances; that can be valuable with drift in model parameters but changes the training problem and should be specified in advance. The model orders in the rolling comparison are held fixed, rather than secretly reselected using the complete holdout. Even if fit is recomputed every month, hyperparameter decisions must be separated from final evaluation.

The fixed twelve-month comparison emphasizes why decision context matters. A warehouse planner choosing next month's staffing needs one-step performance; one planning twelve months of equipment capacity needs longer-horizon performance and uncertainty. One cannot select the favorable metric after seeing results and then call it a predeclared success. The safest report shows both evaluation designs, identifies which corresponds to the actual operational decision, and distinguishes point accuracy from interval calibration and stability.

Core equation
\[\mathrm{MAE}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}|Y_{t_i}-\widehat Y_{t_i\mid o_i}|\]

14. Residual diagnostics and Ljung–Box

After fitting, plot residuals over time, examine their ACF and compare changes in dispersion. For a correctly specified conditional-mean model, residuals should have little remaining serial predictability at the lags relevant to the application. But uncorrelated fitted residuals do not imply independent innovations, stable variance, Gaussianity, causal validity or correct forecasts after a structural break. A model can pass an omnibus autocorrelation test and still systematically underpredict high-volume months.

The Ljung–Box statistic for m selected residual lags is Q=n(n+2)Σ_{k=1}^m r_k²/(n−k). The reference chi-square degrees of freedom may be adjusted for estimated ARMA parameters, and the treatment of initial residuals and fitted trend matters. At a test level α=0.05, a large p-value means insufficient evidence to reject the no-autocorrelation null *at the tested lags*, not a 95% probability the model is correct. Small samples and many estimated parameters limit the approximation.

For illustration, ARIMA(1,1,1) fitted to our first 36 months has a Ljung–Box p-value around 0.62 at lag 6 when calculated on the returned residual series after excluding the initial level residual as specified in the verification. Different initialization and degrees-of-freedom conventions can produce different outputs; the example explicitly reports its convention. The residual plot may reveal isolated shocks even when that p-value is high. Check outliers, heteroscedasticity, unusual months, model convergence and rolling errors before accepting operational adequacy.

Core equation
\[Q=n(n+2)\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{r_k^2}{n-k}\]

15. Forecast intervals and horizon-dependent risk

Point forecasts summarize conditional centers, but a logistics decision needs a distribution of possible outcomes. Under a well specified Gaussian ARIMA model, software can provide a forecast standard error s_h and a nominal 95% interval Yhat_{T+h|T}±1.96s_h. Its validity depends on the assumed innovation process, estimated parameters, conditioning and model stability. An interval for a future individual month's volume differs from a confidence interval for a population mean parameter. In an integrated process, future shocks accumulate and s_h usually grows with horizon.

For the transparent ARIMA(0,1,0) random-walk model with σ=5 thousand parcels, a one-month standard error is 5, and an approximate 95% half-width is 9.8 thousand parcels. At h=4, the error standard deviation is √4×5=10, so the half-width is 19.6. These arithmetic intervals follow from a *specified stochastic model* and known σ. If σ has to be estimated from just 35 differences, additional uncertainty and model risk remain; the formula is therefore an illustration, not a guaranteed 95% coverage statement for future warehouses.

A nominal interval can fail under structural breaks, heavy tails, changing volatility or incorrect d, p and q. Evaluating empirical coverage requires many truly out-of-sample forecast origins and comparisons by horizon. Twelve held-out months are too few to certify 95% coverage at multiple horizons. Report both average width and observed coverage, and examine asymmetric risks: underestimating volumes may impose overtime and service costs that differ from excess-capacity costs.

Core equation
\[\widehat{Y}_{T+h\mid T}\pm z_{0.975}s_h\approx\widehat{Y}_{T+h\mid T}\pm1.96s_h\]

16. Returning to levels: the inversion identity

For d=1, if the model predicts future differences What_{T+1}, ..., What_{T+h}, the forecasted level is Yhat_{T+h|T}=Y_T+Σ_{j=1}^h What_{T+j|T}. This is an exact *algebraic inversion of the chosen point predictions*. It requires retaining the most recent observed level and respecting the time origin. For d=2, both the last level and last difference must be retained, because predicting changes in differences alone is insufficient to reconstruct future levels.

Use a teaching forecast with Y_T=291.680 and three predicted differences 2.5, 3.0 and 2.0 thousand parcels. The level forecasts are 294.180, 297.180 and 299.180. It would be incorrect to report 2.0 as the third month's demand or to add all three predicted differences separately to the original Y_T without accumulating the earlier ones. A useful independent check takes successive differences of the reconstructed levels: 294.180−291.680=2.5, 297.180−294.180=3.0, and 299.180−297.180=2.0.

The word “inverse” sometimes causes confusion. Here we are reversing a linear differencing operator on the *forecasted process*, not undoing statistical uncertainty. The distribution of the sum depends on covariance among future predicted errors, so simply adding three marginal variances is generally wrong. Transformations like logarithms create another issue: exponentiating a forecast of log(Y) yields a median-like scale summary in lognormal settings, not automatically the mean on the original scale. Such bias adjustments need explicit distribution assumptions.

Core equation
\[\widehat{Y}_{T+h\mid T}=Y_T+\sum_{j=1}^{h}\widehat{\Delta Y}_{T+j\mid T}\]

17. Back-transform bias and modeling positive volumes

For positive series, analysts sometimes fit an ARIMA on log(Y) rather than Y. If Z=log(Y) is conditionally Gaussian with forecast mean m and variance v, then its conditional median on the original scale is exp(m), but conditional mean is exp(m+v/2). This follows from the moment-generating function of the Normal distribution, not from a universal identity applicable to arbitrary innovations. Log transformation can help when variability increases with the level, but it changes the estimand and makes residual interpretation multiplicative rather than additive.

Suppose log volume has forecast mean 5 and variance 0.04. Then exp(5)≈148.413 in the volume units and exp(5+0.02)≈151.411. The difference, about 2.998, is not a numerical rounding artifact; it reflects the asymmetry induced by exponentiation. The original volume units must be defined consistently before reporting either result. If the conditional distribution is not lognormal, the exp(m+v/2) correction is not justified by those two moments alone.

Our main case models the level differences directly and remains in thousands of parcels, avoiding an unnecessary log transform. This section is included to develop the curriculum requirement that students recognize when an inverse transformation is biased. It does not imply every positive volume needs logarithms or that ARIMA guarantees positive forecasts. If volumes can approach zero, additive Gaussian ARIMA may predict negative values; domain constraints, an appropriate link or a different count model may be needed.

Core equation
\[E(Y\mid\mathcal F_T)=\exp\left(m+\frac v2\right)\quad\text{if }\log Y\mid\mathcal F_T\sim N(m,v)\]

18. Sensitivity, breaks and model governance

A fitted ARIMA is a provisional description of a period, not a permanent law. Re-estimating after a promotion, strike, facility expansion or reporting-system change can alter parameters and forecast errors. An abrupt structural break may mimic a unit root; a temporary outlier may mimic a moving-average shock. Therefore model validation includes understanding the data-generating process and documenting interventions, not just inspecting ACF plots. The point of a sensitivity analysis is to ask how robust the practical conclusion is to plausible alternative windows, orders and diagnostic conventions.

A useful robustness protocol compares at least the last-observation Naive baseline, Random Walk with Drift, a parsimonious ARIMA and a slightly more flexible candidate under identical origins. Inspect MAE, RMSE, error direction, forecast intervals, convergence warnings and residual structure. Report how often each method wins by origin, not only the average. If no model offers stable improvement, the simple baseline may be the rational operational choice. Complexity is a cost and an overfitting risk, not an academic achievement in itself.

The dataset has been deliberately constructed so a seasonal explanation is unnecessary. It does not establish the absence of all conceivable calendar effects in real logistics. Actual shipping systems have promotions, weekdays, holidays, staffing changes and capacity constraints. Those extra predictors lead to regression with ARIMA errors or other extensions; seasonal dependence leads to SARIMA in Guide 70. We do not preempt those topics here. The final decision must state what was tested, what is synthetic and what the evidence cannot yet support.

19. Reading and verifying the fitted-model report

A reproducible results table must state order, data window, trend, estimated parameters, optimizer and version, AIC convention, training diagnostic and holdout protocol. Without that metadata, the same label “ARIMA(1,1,1)” can hide different likelihoods and forecasts. In our independent calculation on months 1–36, the estimated AR coefficient of that model is approximately −0.5273, the MA coefficient approximately 0.9482, long-run differenced trend coefficient approximately 2.9094 and innovation variance approximately 18.6828 in squared thousand-parcel units. The large MA coefficient makes boundary sensitivity particularly worth checking.

The Ljung–Box calculation at lag six on the specified post-initial residuals gives a p-value around 0.619. Interpreting that number as proof of white noise would overstate the evidence. A fitted near-boundary MA term and a short training series are material qualifications. If the model produced a convergence warning or different results under a second initialization, a careful analyst would test alternative parameterizations or retain a simpler baseline. We report the calculation as one reproducible run, not a certified production model.

The independent rolling backtest gives approximately 4.022 thousand parcels MAE and 4.872 RMSE for ARIMA(1,1,1), versus naive MAE approximately 4.535. A reduction of about 0.513 thousand parcels corresponds to roughly 513 parcels on average per one-month forecast in this constructed holdout. This could be operationally meaningful if the reduction were stable and not offset by maintenance costs; one short synthetic history cannot justify that conclusion. The proper output is a qualified comparison and a monitoring plan.

20. Model-selection checklist and transfer to real data

A real analyst should begin by making an immutable copy of the raw time stamps, documenting the data owner, units, calendar frequency and revision policy. Next comes the question of whether a prediction is needed at the end of a month or during it; partially accumulated counts are not interchangeable with completed monthly totals. Choose a holdout period that reflects real operational forecasting. Create a transparent baseline before searching model orders, and preserve preprocessing operations inside each training window to prevent leakage.

Then inspect level and differenced plots, possible changes in variance and breaks, and the ACF/PACF as evidence rather than automatic rules. Declare candidate orders and whether Drift is allowed. Fit on comparable data with compatible likelihood conventions, document failures and inspect residuals. If comparing AICc, state the sample-size and parameter-count convention. When evaluating point and interval forecasts, use exactly the same origins and horizons across models. If model ranking changes with the metric, report that conflict rather than concealing it.

Finally, convert statistical error into the operational units the decision maker uses: parcels, staffing hours, overtime cost or unused capacity. A 500-parcel mean reduction can be small for a giant warehouse and crucial for a small sorting shift. This conversion needs a cost model, which our synthetic dataset does not provide. The Student Understanding Test asks whether a first-time learner could reconstruct an ARIMA forecast, state its assumptions and explain why a training fit statistic cannot replace chronological evaluation. The worked exercises below deliberately require that full chain of reasoning.

21. Unit roots versus deterministic trends

A rising graph does not uniquely identify a unit root. Consider Y_t=a+bt+u_t where u_t is stationary around zero. Removing the deterministic linear component leaves u_t, whose variance does not grow indefinitely simply because time advances. Compare this with Y_t=Y_{t−1}+c+ε_t, where each new innovation changes the level permanently. Regressing a random walk on time and treating the residuals as independent can conceal the accumulating forecast risk. Conversely, differencing a trend-stationary process can replace its stable deviation structure with unnecessary moving-average-like dependence.

A helpful diagnostic thought experiment is to imagine a one-time shock of +10 thousand parcels at month 20. Under the stationary-around-trend model, the shock is transitory if u_t subsequently reverts to zero. In a pure random walk, it permanently shifts expected future levels by +10 unless offset by another shock. That is why the two models make different long-horizon forecasts and intervals even if their short observed paths look similar. The choice matters to warehouse planning: a temporary festival spike should not always increase the projected capacity permanently.

An Augmented Dickey–Fuller test can provide evidence against a unit root under a selected regression specification, while other tests have different null hypotheses. The results depend on whether a constant or trend is included, lag selection and structural breaks. A short p-value report without these decisions is inadequate. Our synthetic generating recipe intentionally contains one integration, which grounds d=1 in this constructed teaching case. For real data, diagnosing d is an inference problem rather than a known fact; the test cannot replace examination of the process and of out-of-sample performance.

22. Forecast error covariance for an integrated AR model

For ARIMA(1,1,0) with W_t−c=φ(W_{t−1}−c)+ε_t, each future innovation affects several subsequent predicted changes and hence several later levels. At horizon one, level forecast error is ε_{T+1}. At horizon two, W_{T+2} error contains φ ε_{T+1}+ε_{T+2}; adding the first and second change errors yields (1+φ)ε_{T+1}+ε_{T+2}. If innovations are mutually uncorrelated with common variance σ², its variance is σ²[(1+φ)²+1]. This differs from the random-walk value 2σ² whenever φ is nonzero.

Take φ=0.4 and σ=5 thousand parcels. At one step the forecast standard error is 5. At two steps, variance is 25[(1.4)²+1]=25(2.96)=74 in squared thousand-parcel units, giving standard error √74≈8.6023. A nominal 95% Gaussian interval would use half-width 1.96×8.6023≈16.86 thousand parcels, conditional on known parameters and the model assumptions. Simply using √2×5≈7.071 would understate the second-step standard error here because it ignores propagation of earlier shocks through positive autocorrelation in the differences.

The calculation also explains the relationship between point forecast reconstruction and interval construction. Adding forecasts is straightforward linear arithmetic; computing the variance of a sum requires every relevant covariance. With estimated parameters, the full predictive variance may include parameter uncertainty beyond this known-parameter illustration. Software prediction intervals incorporate their stated modeling conventions, but should be validated empirically across comparable origins. Students should be able to say not only how large a band is but why its width depends on the model's dynamic structure.

Core equation
\[\operatorname{Var}(e_{T+2\mid T})=\sigma^2\bigl[(1+\phi)^2+1\bigr]\]

23. Near-boundary parameters and forecast fragility

An MA parameter near an invertibility boundary can complicate estimation. For MA(1), the usual invertibility condition under our positive-sign convention is |θ|<1. The fitted ARIMA(1,1,1) reports θ≈0.9482, which satisfies the inequality but lies near its positive boundary. That is not by itself evidence of an invalid fit, yet it invites examination of likelihood shape, optimizer sensitivity, standard errors and whether a less complicated candidate gives similar forecasts. A sample of only 36 training levels is not large enough to ignore these issues.

A model may also contain AR and MA terms that partially offset each other, creating parameter-identification problems. Similar observed autocorrelation patterns can be produced by several order combinations. Higher-order candidates may fit historical shocks well but forecast no better than a stable low-order alternative. This is why our candidate table includes models with more than one lag but the recommendation emphasizes holdout behavior and interpretability. Information criteria discourage some complexity, but they do not remove the need to examine boundary and convergence behavior.

For a realistic review, an analyst might refit with alternative initialization, inspect reported standard errors, perturb the training cutoff by several months and observe whether the one-step forecasts change sharply. Those are sensitivity checks, not a license to tune repeatedly on the final holdout. If sensitivity is high, communication must say so explicitly and retain an operational fallback. In the synthetic example, a relatively favorable rolling MAE is grounds for additional evaluation, not immediate deployment or a claim of economic causality.

24. Why a diagnostic pass is not forecasting certification

Residual diagnostics answer conditional questions about what remains unexplained on the fitted history. Forecast evaluation answers a different question: how accurately and reliably the procedure anticipates observations that were unavailable when it ran. A white-noise-looking residual series is compatible with several model structures and does not guarantee an unbiased or well calibrated forecast after the environment changes. Conversely, a small residual imperfection at a lag may be practically unimportant if a simple forecasting rule is stable and an alternative offers no useful improvement.

A complete ARIMA review therefore joins at least four streams of evidence. First, the assumed integration order should have a defensible story about the level and its differences. Second, the fitted stochastic part should be reasonably parsimonious and computationally stable. Third, residual autocorrelation, changing variance, outliers and unusual months must be examined with appropriate caution. Fourth, errors and interval coverage must be evaluated chronologically at the horizons the business actually uses. None of these streams alone is conclusive; consistency among them supports a conditional recommendation.

Suppose a manager sees a Ljung–Box p-value of 0.62 and a low training AIC. The responsible interpretation is: within the specified test and fitted period, we lack evidence of residual autocorrelation and one candidate fits the training data favorably after a complexity penalty. We have not thereby established that the next year's forecasts will be accurate, that residuals are independent, that the model captures a causal mechanism, or that a costly deployment is justified. A forecast system needs monitoring and a fallback even after prospective validation.

25. Communication example: from coefficients to a decision memo

A technical report can be mathematically correct and still useless to its reader. Start by naming the forecast target: completed monthly parcel volume, in thousands, issued at the end of each preceding month. Then describe the data provenance: 48 synthetic months, 36 for initial training, 12 held out; no observed operational costs or service-level targets are supplied. Report the candidate set rather than pretending a global exhaustive search, and show the predefined comparison metric and horizon. Next state the result: ARIMA(1,1,1) gave rolling one-step MAE about 4.022 thousand parcels versus Naive 4.535, while a different ARIMA order had lower training AIC.

Follow the point estimate with uncertainty and conditions. Explain that the modest MAE reduction is an observed difference on this artificial holdout, not an uncertainty-adjusted performance guarantee. The candidate's MA coefficient lies near an invertibility boundary and the residual omnibus test has limited power. Recommend more chronological testing and monitoring before deployment, with Naive preserved as a fallback. If the decision maker instead needs twelve-month capacity planning, explicitly redo the comparison at twelve-month horizon instead of reusing the one-step ranking.

The final paragraph should translate errors into concrete units and ask which mistakes matter. One unit in our reported scale equals 1,000 parcels. A one-month MAE of 4.022 therefore corresponds to about 4,022 parcels of absolute error on average across the twelve evaluated months; it does not mean every forecast missed by exactly 4,022, nor that a service objective was met. A meaningful business recommendation needs capacity, staffing and cost information absent from this teaching dataset. Accurate statistical language leaves room for that missing decision evidence.

26. Exercise 1: Reconstruct the first difference

Problem. Given months 35 and 36 at 293.523 and 291.680 thousand parcels, calculate the month-36 first difference and explain whether the result establishes a downward long-term trend.

Hint. Subtract the earlier level from the later one, preserving units. Distinguish a single increment from the process mean.

Solution. ΔY_36=291.680−293.523=−1.843 thousand parcels. The negative sign says approximately 1,843 fewer parcels were processed in month 36 than in month 35. It does not prove a long-term downward trend: other months of the displayed series show positive increments, and the level has generally risen across training. If the unrounded data are used, the computed difference agrees to the three displayed decimals. The distinction matters because ARIMA(1,1,0) describes the dynamics of changes; it does not assume every monthly difference is positive. A manager should not extrapolate one negative difference into a staffing reduction for the entire coming year without evidence about uncertainty and persistence.

27. Exercise 2: Compute the drift baseline

Problem. Use Y_1=189.200 and Y_36=291.680 thousand parcels. Calculate the training average change and a one-step random-walk-with-drift forecast.

Hint. Thirty-six levels contain 35 consecutive differences; do not divide by 36.

Solution. The telescoping sum of increments is Y_36−Y_1=102.480 thousand parcels. Divide by 35 to obtain 2.928 per month. One-step prediction is 291.680+2.928=294.608 thousand parcels. Actual Y_37≈285.745, so actual-minus-forecast error is −8.863 using displayed rounding (the full-precision script gives approximately −8.862). The baseline was not trained on Y_37; the actual is used only after forecasting to evaluate error. This verifies both the arithmetic and the temporal protocol. Its error is one observation, not an estimate of the long-run expected performance.

28. Exercise 3: Rebuild ARIMA(1,1,0) forecasts

Problem. At a forecast origin, Y_T=291.680, latest change W_T=−1.843, c=2.9 and φ=0.4. Predict the next two levels without revealing Y_{T+1}.

Hint. Forecast changes first and add them sequentially. Use φ² for the second expected deviation from c.

Solution. Wforecast_1=2.9+0.4(−1.843−2.9)=1.0028, so Yforecast_1=292.6828. The second expected change is 2.9+0.16(−4.743)=2.14112, hence Yforecast_2=292.6828+2.14112=294.82392 thousand parcels. Check with the closed-form sum: 291.680+2(2.9)+(−4.743)(0.4+0.16)=294.82392. Two independent arithmetic routes agree. Plugging actual Y_{T+1} into the second step would redefine the forecast origin and violate the stated two-step problem. All computations here use illustrative chosen parameters, not fitted coefficients.

29. Exercise 4: Interpret an MA innovation

Problem. An ARIMA(0,1,1) has c=3, θ=0.5, last filtered innovation e_T=−4 and level Y_T=291.680. Find forecasts at horizons one and two.

Hint. The old shock contributes to the next change once; its influence on the cumulative level persists thereafter.

Solution. The one-step expected change is 3+0.5(−4)=1, yielding 292.680. The second future expected change is 3 because the next innovation has conditional mean zero. Thus Yforecast_2=292.680+3=295.680. Equivalently, at h=2, 291.680+2(3)−2=295.680. The shock term is −2 exactly once. It would be wrong to reuse e_T as though it represented an observable parcel count. In a real fitted model, e_T is an estimated filtered innovation and depends on initial state and likelihood convention; the exercise assumes it is already supplied.

30. Exercise 5: Recover levels from three predictions

Problem. Beginning at Y_T=291.680, a differenced model predicts 2.5, 3.0 and 2.0 thousand parcels as the next three monthly changes. Calculate all forecasted levels and verify by differencing back.

Solution. The first level is 291.680+2.5=294.180. The second is 294.180+3.0=297.180. The third is 297.180+2.0=299.180. Subtract adjacent predictions: 294.180−291.680=2.5, 297.180−294.180=3.0, and 299.180−297.180=2.0. Reconstruction matches every input change. This check is essential because a forecast of a differenced process cannot be communicated as monthly volume. The exercise contains no interval calculation: recovering level point forecasts is not the same as recovering uncertainty about their sums.

31. Exercise 6: Calculate AICc transparently

Problem. Compare AIC values 211.008 and 208.049 for two training candidates, each with k=4 parameters and nominal n=36. Calculate AICc using the supplied correction and name its limitation.

Solution. Both add 2×4×5/(36−4−1)=40/31≈1.29032. The first becomes 212.29832 and the second 209.33932. The second therefore has the smaller AICc under exactly this convention. That ranking does not establish better future one-step MAE, and integrated-series implementations may use a different effective sample n. A valid comparison requires compatible likelihood definitions and training data. AICc is not itself a probability that a model is true. The exercise separates a correct numerical ranking from an unjustified forecasting claim.

32. Exercise 7: Check a likelihood comparison

Problem. Two models use identical observations and likelihood conventions. Model A has log-likelihood −102 and k=3; model B has log-likelihood −99 and k=6. Calculate AIC and choose by this criterion.

Solution. AIC_A=−2(−102)+2(3)=210. AIC_B=−2(−99)+2(6)=210. The models tie by AIC even though B has a larger maximized likelihood; its three extra parameters absorb the fit improvement in the penalty. A numerical tie cannot be turned into a claim of equal forecasting ability. The exercise reinforces the exact role of −2ℓ and 2k, and why one should evaluate a parsimonious model rather than equating higher training likelihood with better future forecasts.

33. Exercise 8: Compute a two-model holdout MAE

Problem. For three future months, actual volumes are 100, 110, 108 thousand parcels. Forecasts A are 102, 108, 111, and forecasts B are 99, 105, 110. Compute MAE and compare.

Solution. A absolute errors are |100−102|=2, |110−108|=2, |108−111|=3. Hence MAE_A=(2+2+3)/3=7/3≈2.333. B errors are 1, 5 and 2, hence MAE_B=8/3≈2.667. A wins on this tiny fixed set under MAE, but B wins on the first month and its performance could change at other horizons. The example illustrates why averages hide error timing, which can be crucial for capacity planning. Neither model is guaranteed to win on another dataset.

34. Exercise 9: Distinguish the two backtests

Problem. A forecaster estimates a model through month 36, predicts months 37–48, and does not update. Another re-estimates each month after observing its outcome and makes a new one-step forecast. Are these equivalent twelve-month evaluations?

Solution. No. The first is one fixed origin with horizons h=1 to 12, so long-horizon uncertainty accumulates and the model never receives new levels. The second uses twelve distinct origins, all at horizon h=1; later origins legitimately include earlier holdout observations that have already occurred. These forecasts answer different operations. Comparisons should use one protocol consistently across models. A method's fixed-origin MAE and rolling MAE can disagree without either computation being wrong. The lesson's AIC ranking cannot decide the most relevant backtest; the staffing or equipment decision must determine the horizon in advance.

35. Exercise 10: Read Ljung–Box without overclaiming

Problem. A fitted ARIMA reports Ljung–Box p=0.62 at residual lag six under the stated calculation. What is the conclusion at α=0.05 and what is not established?

Solution. Because 0.62>0.05, we do not reject the null of no residual autocorrelation over the lags included in the test, under its reference approximation. It is incorrect to claim a 62% chance that residuals are white noise or that the model is valid. The test says little about time-varying variance, isolated outliers, wrong conditional distributions, structural breaks and forecast calibration. We would examine residual plots, ACF, error distributions and independent chronological predictions. With only 36 training levels and several estimated parameters, power may be limited; failure to reject can reflect lack of information.

36. Exercise 11: Calculate an interval and invert a log forecast

Problem. (a) In a random walk with known shock standard deviation 5, calculate the nominal 95% half-width at h=4. (b) Under a conditional lognormal assumption with log-mean 5 and variance 0.04, compare original-scale median and mean.

Solution. (a) The four future innovations add; if uncorrelated and equal-variance, total forecast-error variance is 4×25=100, standard deviation 10, and normal half-width 1.96×10=19.6 thousand parcels. (b) Median exp(5)≈148.413; mean exp(5+0.04/2)=exp(5.02)≈151.411. Their difference is about 2.998. The logarithmic result assumes conditional Gaussian log values; without that assumption we cannot use this correction automatically. The interval exercise assumes known σ and model validity, not estimated coverage guarantees.

37. Exercise 12: Final transfer: recommend a model responsibly

Problem. A manager requests an automated next-month volume forecast. Training AIC favors ARIMA(0,1,2). Rolling one-step MAE favors ARIMA(1,1,1) at 4.022 thousand parcels versus Naive 4.535, but training is short and one MA coefficient is near a boundary. Write a defensible decision.

Complete solution. First identify the operational target: the *next completed month's total*, issued after the current month closes. That makes rolling one-step evaluation more directly relevant than a fixed-origin 12-month comparison. Second, describe the evidence: among examined candidates, ARIMA(1,1,1) has the lowest reported rolling one-step MAE, improving on Naive by approximately 0.513 thousand parcels. Third, state the limits: the difference is observed in a twelve-month synthetic holdout; residual Ljung–Box nonrejection is not proof; the fitted MA parameter near a boundary warrants stability and convergence checks; the comparison needs independent real data and interval evaluation. Fourth, propose an operational plan: retain Naive as a challenger, review errors and interval coverage monthly, monitor breaks, and deploy the complex model only if additional chronological periods show stable practical value. This is a reproducible recommendation, not a claim that ARIMA caused higher productivity or universally outperforms simple forecasts.
