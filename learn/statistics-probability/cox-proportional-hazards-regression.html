Cox Proportional Hazards Regression

Study Guide 76 · Statistics & Probability · English

1. The statistical problem: not every employee has left

Imagine an organization that wants to understand how workload and access to mentoring are associated with the timing of voluntary departure. A conventional regression of months worked against employee characteristics seems tempting, yet it fails when several employees are still employed at the analysis date. We do not know their eventual departure times. Discarding those employees would remove information and would favor people with shorter observed careers. Treating the duration of follow-up as if departure had happened would invent events. Survival analysis is designed for this mixture of observed event times and incomplete follow-up.

The outcome is time until a precisely defined event, not merely a yes/no indicator and not a complete duration for everyone. In this lesson the event is recorded departure from the organization, time zero is the beginning of employment, the duration is measured in months, and a right-censored record means that departure was not observed before follow-up ended. A right-censored employee contributes information while observed, but no departure at the censoring time. This distinction controls who belongs to the denominator of every comparison.

Our question is associational: among employees still at risk at a given month, how does the instantaneous departure rate compare between groups with different recorded initial characteristics? To answer, Cox regression adjusts a hazard comparison for several predictors without requiring us to choose a parametric shape for the underlying time pattern at the start. That flexibility does not eliminate assumptions, confounding, sampling error, or missing information. The worked numbers are wholly synthetic educational data, constructed for transparent arithmetic rather than claims about a real employer.

Prerequisites: probability and conditional probability, logarithms and exponentials, introductory regression, the survival and cumulative hazard definitions from Guide 73, and risk sets from Guides 72 and 74. We review every extra concept needed, especially an optimization objective called partial likelihood. A reader familiar only with Calculus I can follow the arithmetic; optimization with multiple coefficients is explained conceptually rather than assumed.

2. Hazard is a conditional rate, not a probability

Let the nonnegative random variable T denote the time from hiring to departure. The survival function S(t)=P(T>t) is the probability the employee has not departed by time t. The cumulative distribution function F(t)=P(T≤t) equals 1−S(t). With continuous event times and a density f(t), hazard is the instantaneous conditional departure rate among those who have survived until t.

Hazard definition
\[h(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0^+}\frac{P(t\le T<t+\Delta t\mid T\ge t)}{\Delta t}=\frac{f(t)}{S(t)}\]

The limit matters. If hazard is 0.08 per month, it is incorrect to claim that an employee necessarily has an eight-percent probability of leaving during a month. Over a sufficiently short interval of length Δt, the conditional event probability is approximately h(t)Δt, provided the hazard changes little. For a longer interval one needs the integral of hazard; the exact conditional event probability is 1−exp{−[H(t+Δt)−H(t)]}. Units also matter: hazard measured per month must be multiplied by a duration in months.

Cumulative hazard is H(t)=∫₀ᵗh(u)du; it is dimensionless, can exceed one, and is not itself a probability. Survival is S(t)=exp[−H(t)]. This identity is the bridge between the Cox regression coefficient, the estimated baseline cumulative hazard, and the eventual probability that an employee remains employed beyond a chosen month. We will use this bridge numerically, not merely quote it.

4. How the risk set changes with time

Immediately before each observed departure time t_j, the risk set R(t_j) contains employees who have entered observation and have not yet departed or been censored. At the first departure, month 2, all sixteen employees remain at risk. At month 3, fifteen remain; at month 5, thirteen remain because one previous departure occurred and one other employee was censored at month 4. Risk sets are defined just before each event, never after it.

The eight risk-set sizes in order are 16, 15, 13, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4. These counts are not the same as the number of observed events. They are the candidate set from which a particular observed departure could arise. Censored employees contribute up to their censoring date, but after that date they leave future denominators. If an event and censoring occurred at the same recorded time, a declared convention about event ordering would be required; our main example avoids such ambiguity.

A survival curve compares the probability of being event-free over time. Cox regression instead asks how predictors change the instantaneous hazard within each risk set. Observations at later event times do not re-enter earlier denominators in a backward-looking way; the calculation respects the temporal ordering. This is why sorting dates, applying the same time origin, and checking risk-set membership are essential data-integrity steps rather than clerical details.

5. From proportional hazards to a regression equation

The Cox proportional hazards model expresses the hazard for employee i with covariate vector xᵢ as a baseline hazard multiplied by an exponential relative-risk factor. With two binary predictors and no interaction, the equation is:

Cox model
\[h(t\mid W,M)=h_0(t)\exp(\beta_W W+\beta_M M)\]

Here h₀(t) is the hazard at time t for the reference employee (W=0, M=0), and β_W, β_M are coefficients measured on the log-hazard-ratio scale. Unlike an ordinary linear model, the Cox regression does not put time on the left and try to predict it by an additive linear formula. Instead, it multiplies a baseline hazard by a positive factor. Because exp(z)>0 for any finite real z, the resulting hazard cannot become negative.

The model assumes that the coefficient attached to a predictor does not change with time. If two otherwise comparable profiles differ only in workload, the hazard ratio is exp(β_W), the same at month 2 and month 10. This proportional hazards assumption is stronger than merely assuming that two survival curves differ. The baseline hazard can rise, fall, or be irregular; it is not assumed constant. A model with an interaction W×M would allow workload's hazard ratio to depend on mentoring, but this first case uses the simpler additive linear predictor.

6. Derive a hazard ratio rather than memorizing it

Consider two employees A and B with covariate vectors x_A and x_B. Divide their Cox hazards at the same event time. The baseline function cancels:

Hazard ratio
\[\frac{h(t\mid x_A)}{h(t\mid x_B)}=\frac{h_0(t)e^{x_A^\top\beta}}{h_0(t)e^{x_B^\top\beta}}=e^{(x_A-x_B)^\top\beta}\]

This calculation explains why we exponentiate a coefficient. The coefficient is the natural logarithm of a conditional hazard ratio, not a difference of probabilities. For example, a coefficient of ln(2) corresponds to a hazard ratio of two. A coefficient of −ln(2) corresponds to a hazard ratio of one-half; a coefficient of zero corresponds to a ratio of one. The interpretation is conditional on the other predictors held fixed and on the proportional-hazards assumption.

It is incorrect to interpret a hazard ratio of two as “twice as many employees leave by the end of a year,” “twice the probability of leaving,” or “half the median employment duration.” None of those statements follows from the ratio of instantaneous hazards alone. Absolute survival differences require the baseline hazard and a specific time; median differences depend on the shape of the entire curves. Later we will derive the relevant absolute probabilities using H₀(t).

7. Why ordinary likelihood would be harder

A full likelihood for censored survival data ordinarily involves a density contribution when an event occurs and a survival contribution when observation is censored. For one individual with event indicator δᵢ, this is f(tᵢ|xᵢ)^δᵢ S(tᵢ|xᵢ)^(1−δᵢ), under noninformative right censoring. When the Cox hazard is substituted into that expression, the unknown baseline function appears. Modeling it parametrically would require selecting a shape, for example exponential or Weibull, and then estimating that shape along with the covariate coefficients.

Cox's strategy is different: use the ordering of observed events to learn the relative-risk coefficients without first specifying the baseline. At an event time, condition on exactly one event occurring among those in the risk set, and ask which employee is the one observed to depart. The relative hazard weights determine the conditional probability assigned to each eligible employee. The same unknown baseline multiplies every competitor at that instant and therefore cancels in the conditional ratio. The resulting partial likelihood does not represent a complete probability model for all original follow-up times, but it contains the information needed for the regression coefficients under the model and censoring assumptions.

This distinction matters: leaving the baseline unspecified is not a proof that it is irrelevant. The baseline cancels from coefficient estimation at event times; we must estimate it after fitting the coefficients if we want absolute survival probabilities. We must also specify the handling of tied event times, since simultaneous failures require a more careful approximation than a simple one-event denominator.

8. One partial-likelihood contribution, calculated fully

Suppose employee E01 leaves at month 2 and has W=0, M=0. The model assigns this employee the relative-risk weight exp(0)=1. Every employee in the initial risk set has a weight rᵢ=exp(β_W Wᵢ+β_M Mᵢ). Conditional on one observed event among these sixteen employees, employee E01's likelihood contribution is its own weight divided by the total weight of all possible departing employees.

First partial likelihood factor
\[L_2(\beta)=\frac{e^{0}}{\sum_{i\in R(2)}e^{\beta_W W_i+\beta_M M_i}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{16}r_i}\]

At the fitted coefficients, β_W=1.158605 and β_M=−0.220812, the four profile weights are 1, 3.185486, 0.801867, and 2.554337 for (W,M)=(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1). The data contain four employees of each profile. Consequently the initial denominator is 4×1+4×3.185486+4×0.801867+4×2.554337=30.166763, and the first event's fitted contribution is 1/30.166763≈0.033149.

This number is not the probability that E01 leaves in month 2. It is a conditional model weight for which member of the risk set is the event, given that a departure occurs there. The small number reflects competition among sixteen at-risk individuals. Substituting a different coefficient pair would change the relative weights and thus the likelihood. That sensitivity is precisely how the coefficients can be estimated from the ordering of events.

9. Multiply all event contributions and take logarithms

With distinct observed departure times t₁,...,t₈, and j(k) identifying the employee who departs at the kth time, multiply the eight conditional factors:

Partial likelihood
\[L(\beta)=\prod_{k=1}^{8}\frac{\exp(x_{j(k)}^\top\beta)}{\sum_{i\in R(t_k)}\exp(x_i^\top\beta)}\]

Because multiplying many fractions can give small numbers and obscure differentiation, numerical algorithms maximize the natural logarithm instead. The logarithm turns products into sums and ratios into differences:

Log-partial likelihood
\[\ell(\beta)=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\left[x_{j(k)}^\top\beta-\log\!\left(\sum_{i\in R(t_k)}e^{x_i^\top\beta}\right)\right]\]

Each term has an intuitive interpretation: reward the model when it assigns high relative-risk weight to the person who departed and compare that weight against everybody else who could have departed at the same time. The fitted coefficients maximize the sum of these comparisons. One can differentiate the log-partial likelihood with respect to each coefficient, obtaining a score (gradient), and solve the two score equations numerically. The negative Hessian describes local curvature and contributes to estimated standard errors.

A student does not need to run the optimization blindly. The full table, every risk set, the formula above, and the supplied independent check allow reconstruction of the objective. Because the coefficients require solving nonlinear equations, the guide cites the verified computational fit rather than pretending that a closed-form elementary solution exists. This is a declared numerical estimation step, unlike the hand-calculated first factor.

11. Coefficients, hazard ratios and adjusted interpretation

Maximization of the partial likelihood gives β_W=1.158605 with estimated standard error 0.818825, and β_M=−0.220812 with standard error 0.711927. Exponentiating gives adjusted hazard ratios HR_W=3.185486 and HR_M=0.801867. The first number means that employees classified as high workload have an estimated instantaneous departure hazard about 3.19 times that of low-workload employees at the same month and with the same mentoring status, under this fitted proportional-hazards model.

The mentoring hazard ratio of 0.802 describes an estimated 19.8% lower hazard because 1−0.801867=0.198133. It is an adjusted association, not an estimate of the probability of retaining an employee or of the causal benefit of a mentoring program. With only eight departures, both coefficient estimates are imprecise. Strong selection into high workload or mentoring could distort the associations; even perfect numerical fitting cannot repair omitted causes such as prior job satisfaction or salary.

For a profile with both high workload and mentoring, the risk score is exp(1.158605−0.220812)=2.554337. Comparing it with the reference profile yields a hazard ratio of 2.554. Comparing two high-workload employees differing only in mentoring gives 0.802, the same mentoring HR. Comparing a high-workload employee without mentoring against a low-workload employee with mentoring gives 3.185486/0.801867≈3.9725, because both predictors change. Always identify both the numerator and denominator profiles before stating an HR.

12. Confidence intervals and tests from log coefficients

A large-sample Wald confidence interval is easiest to compute for the log-hazard coefficient and then transform both endpoints by the exponential function. For workload, the approximate 95% limits are 1.158605 ± 1.959964×0.818825, that is, −0.4463 and 2.7635. Exponentiating gives approximately [0.6400,15.8548] for the hazard ratio. The interval is asymmetric on the HR scale because the exponential transformation is nonlinear.

Wald interval for HR
\[\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(HR_j)=\left[\exp(\widehat\beta_j-1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(\widehat\beta_j)),\;\exp(\widehat\beta_j+1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(\widehat\beta_j))\right]\]

For mentoring the corresponding interval is [0.1987,3.2366]. Both intervals include HR=1, so neither coefficient meets the two-sided 5% Wald significance criterion in this small constructed cohort. The workload z-statistic is 1.158605/0.818825≈1.4150, with p≈0.1571; the mentoring z-statistic is approximately −0.3102, with p≈0.7564. A failure to reject a null hypothesis does not prove no association; the broad intervals express large uncertainty.

Wald calculations rely on a large-sample approximation. A likelihood-ratio test or score test can behave differently in small samples; all require appropriate censoring and model assumptions. In applied research, report the estimate, interval, event count, test choice and assumptions rather than giving only an asterisk for significance. Model-based standard errors also may be inappropriate when employees are clustered into teams or repeated events are mistakenly treated as independent.

13. Breslow estimation of the baseline cumulative hazard

Partial likelihood gives regression coefficients but does not yet tell us the absolute chance that a reference employee remains employed. Once β is estimated, a common Breslow estimator adds an event contribution to the baseline cumulative hazard at each distinct failure time. With exactly one event at each time, the increment is the reciprocal of the fitted risk-weight denominator:

Breslow baseline estimator
\[\widehat H_0(t)=\sum_{t_j\le t}\frac{d_j}{\sum_{i\in R(t_j)}e^{x_i^\top\widehat\beta}} \quad(d_j=1\text{ in our data})\]

At month 2, the increment is 1/30.166763≈0.033149. By month 3 the cumulative estimate is 0.033149+1/29.166763≈0.067435. By month 10 the six increments accumulate to Ĥ₀(10)≈0.281235. By the final observed event, month 14, the cumulative value is approximately 0.503954. Between observed departures, this estimator stays constant. This staircase behavior is a feature of the estimated cumulative hazard, not a claim that an individual's underlying physical hazard truly jumps at those exact dates.

The Breslow baseline estimator here refers to estimating baseline cumulative hazard after the coefficients are fitted. Breslow ties refers to an approximation for simultaneous events in partial likelihood. The two ideas are related but not interchangeable. Our main dataset has no event ties. A reference profile with no high workload and no mentoring has linear predictor zero; its estimated cumulative hazard equals Ĥ₀(t) by definition of our reference coding.

14. Convert baseline hazard to a survival probability

Because H(t|x)=H₀(t)exp(xᵀβ) under the Cox model, the fitted survival for any fixed profile is:

Conditional survival
\[\widehat S(t\mid x)=\exp\!\left[-\widehat H_0(t)e^{x^\top\widehat\beta}\right]=\widehat S_0(t)^{e^{x^\top\widehat\beta}}\]

For the reference profile, Ŝ(10|0,0)=exp(−0.281235)≈0.754851: approximately 75.5% modeled probability of remaining employed beyond month 10. For high workload without mentoring, the risk multiplier is 3.185486, so Ŝ(10|1,0)=exp(−0.281235×3.185486)≈0.408252. The absolute difference in predicted survival is about 0.347, or 34.7 percentage points. This numerical difference cannot be read directly from the HR; it required both the baseline estimate and the chosen time.

With mentoring but no high workload, the fitted month-10 survival is exp(−0.281235×0.801867)≈0.798106. With both characteristics it is exp(−0.281235×2.554337)≈0.487546. These are conditional model-based predictions for four fixed profiles, not independently validated estimates of real-world retention. Extrapolating beyond month 14 would require assumptions about baseline hazard where no further events were observed; the step estimate itself does not provide credible future survival behavior indefinitely.

15. Why the model is called semiparametric

A fully parametric proportional-hazards model would specify a formula for baseline hazard, perhaps h₀(t)=λ or a Weibull-shaped function. Cox leaves the shape of h₀ unrestricted during estimation of β, while imposing the finite-dimensional linear predictor and proportional hazard ratios. That combination is why Cox is typically called semiparametric. It trades the stronger assumptions of a parametric time law for a weaker assumption about hazard ratios across profiles.

“Semiparametric” does not mean assumption-free. The log-hazard is assumed linear in the supplied predictors; a numerical predictor might need a nonlinear term, spline or transformation. Covariate values must be known at the times used; hidden temporal updates can create immortal-time or information leakage. Independent right censoring and adequate observations in risk sets are needed. The hazard ratio is interpreted conditionally: two profiles must be compared at the same time, within an appropriate support region of the data.

The shape of the baseline may vary arbitrarily with time under the mathematical model, but the fixed coefficient means that ratios remain proportional. One can have two strongly declining survival curves without their hazards having a constant ratio. Conversely, proportional hazards does not require that survival curves be proportional. This prevents a common conceptual error: the letters “PH” describe the hazards, not probabilities or expected durations.

16. Tied departure times and the estimation convention

Real administrative datasets often record dates rounded to days, weeks or months, so several departures can share a recorded event time. With d simultaneous failures, the exact probability that a set of employees failed involves multiple selections from the risk set. Software therefore offers tie-handling conventions. The Breslow approximation keeps the same original risk-set denominator for each of the tied departures: roughly, the tied group's numerator divided by R^d, where R is the sum of risk weights before that time.

The Efron approximation progressively discounts fractions of the departing employees' total risk weight from the denominator. For a hypothetical pair of simultaneous events with risk weights a and b, total risk weight R, and d=2, Breslow's denominator is R² while Efron's denominator is R[R−(a+b)/2]. The numerator in both is ab. Suppose R=12, a=2, and b=3: Breslow gives 6/144≈0.04167; Efron gives 6/[12×9.5]≈0.05263. Neither number is the full probability of a two-event time under every conceivable continuous-time observation process; these are declared tied-event likelihood approximations.

The exact discrete-time conditional likelihood is another choice when computationally appropriate. Efron is often preferred for moderate ties, while software defaults vary. Reporting a fitted HR without documenting the tie treatment makes reproducibility harder. The main sixteen-employee case deliberately uses distinct event times, allowing its partial likelihood factors to be reconstructed without a tie approximation. The miniature tied example is clearly a separate hypothetical calculation, not a modification of the dataset.

17. Censoring, delayed entry, and incorrect risk sets

A censored employee contributes a hazard-weighted denominator until their censoring time. Imagine that E03, censored at month 4, is mistakenly kept in the risk set at month 5. The denominator becomes 22.795790+3.185486=25.981276, artificially lowering the event factor for E04 and changing the estimated coefficient. Conversely, dropping every censored employee from all risk sets destroys information they provided before censoring. Both errors can substantially alter the conclusion.

With delayed entry, employees join the risk set only after their entry time Lᵢ; the correct condition is Lᵢ<t≤Yᵢ under the declared event-time convention. Cox software can handle entry times when explicitly supplied, but our current main dataset has Lᵢ=0 for all. Left truncation differs from ordinary right censoring: it conditions observation on survival until entry and needs a correctly constructed risk set. It is incorrect to relabel late hires as having been observed from month zero merely to simplify the spreadsheet.

Informative censoring is more subtle. If employees who are most likely to leave are systematically lost to follow-up because of a data migration, ordinary Cox estimates may be biased even when formulas and code are error-free. Censoring independent of future event time, conditional on modeled variables, is a substantive assumption requiring domain knowledge and sensitivity analysis. A model cannot infer unobserved departures from arithmetic alone.

18. Adjusted association, confounding and decision-making

The adjusted hazard ratio answers a within-model comparison with other included predictors held constant. It does not automatically identify the effect of assigning workload or mentoring as an intervention. Workload may be correlated with department, pay, tenure prospects or job satisfaction. Mentoring may preferentially be assigned to employees who were already at greater risk of leaving. Conditioning only on the variables displayed in our tiny table does not remove these unobserved differences.

A randomized intervention would strengthen causal identification if its implementation and follow-up were sound, but even then the estimand and assumptions must be defined. In observational data, present Cox coefficients as associations. If a business decision depends on expected numbers retained, absolute survival by a relevant horizon is often more interpretable than a single HR. Predictions also depend on the population used to estimate the baseline, measurement quality, temporal stability and uncertainty. A dramatic estimated HR with a very broad interval is a reason to gather better data, not necessarily to announce a policy effect.

The sample size here is 16 and only eight events occurred, far too little to claim robust generalization about an organization. We deliberately keep those limits visible. A scientifically responsible report might state: “In this synthetic illustrative cohort, high workload was associated with an estimated HR of 3.19, 95% CI 0.64–15.85; the interval is wide and the association is inconclusive.” It should not claim that reducing workload would necessarily prevent a particular percentage of exits.

19. Proportional hazards and functional form: what to inspect

The Cox coefficient is assumed time-constant. In a real dataset, the effect of mentoring might be strongest early in employment and fade later; workload might be associated with departures primarily after a stressful season. Those patterns violate simple proportional hazards even if the overall fitted coefficient looks convincing. One should inspect scaled Schoenfeld residuals against event time, consider formal tests, compare time-stratified estimates, and examine plausible time interactions. However, a small p-value in a diagnostic is not itself a complete explanation, and a non-significant test with eight events has very little power.

Martingale and deviance residuals help examine functional form and poorly fit observations; influence diagnostics help assess sensitivity. A continuous salary predictor might have a nonlinear log-hazard effect, requiring a spline or other planned function. A time-varying covariate is not the same as a time-varying coefficient: the first records changes in an employee's characteristics, while the second allows a predictor's effect to change with follow-up time. The next curriculum guide addresses these diagnostics in detail; here we introduce their purpose and explain how a violated assumption limits interpretation.

A thoughtful analyst prespecifies which predictors are needed, checks event counts and covariate support, and avoids adding interactions until a small dataset yields a desirable p-value. Model selection conducted repeatedly on the same small cohort can make uncertainty understated. The baseline survival estimation does not repair violations of the proportional-hazards assumption; it uses the fitted coefficients and therefore inherits their weaknesses.

20. Uncertainty in individual survival predictions

The displayed conditional survival values are point predictions, not 95% prediction intervals or guarantees for individuals. There are at least two sources of estimation uncertainty: the fitted coefficient vector and the baseline cumulative-hazard estimate. A proper confidence interval for a predicted survival curve needs to propagate both sources, often through software-supported asymptotic calculations or resampling that respects the original sampling structure.

It would be incorrect to exponentiate the lower and upper confidence limits of the workload HR independently and insert them as if the baseline were known perfectly. Likewise, a cohort prediction describes a probability for a profile under the model, not the exact departure month of a particular employee. Distinguish confidence intervals for model parameters, uncertainty intervals for population survival curves, and prediction of an individual's event process. These are related, but different, inference targets.

With the present sample, the wide coefficient interval and small late risk sets imply particularly fragile tail predictions. At month 14 only four employees remain in the event risk set. After the last event we have little support for forecasting beyond observed times. A cautious report emphasizes the observed horizon, uncertainty, and external validation needs rather than drawing an endlessly smooth survival curve to an arbitrary future month.

22. A disciplined report: estimates, assumptions and limitations

For an applied report, first define cohort eligibility, time zero, event, censoring, follow-up window and units. Next show covariate definitions and their temporal availability, the number of participants and events, and how the risk sets are built. State the Cox formula, tie-handling convention, fitting method and proportional-hazards assumption. Report log coefficients if relevant, but prioritize adjusted HRs with confidence intervals and clearly labeled reference categories.

When absolute probabilities matter, state the baseline estimator, reference profile and prediction horizon; supply uncertainty estimates if available and do not extend curves beyond supported follow-up. Add a paragraph about censoring assumptions, possible confounding, small late risk sets and diagnostics not yet completed. Finally explain the operational decision: what is supported by the sample, what is not, what additional data or verification would change confidence, and whether an intervention is justified by evidence beyond association.

Our illustrative sample supports calculation of a Cox model and demonstration of its mechanics. It does not support a causal mentoring policy recommendation, reliable tail forecasts, or a claim that high workload is significant at conventional levels. Respecting that distinction is part of the correct interpretation of the model rather than an optional disclaimer.

22A. The score equation: why event order identifies coefficients

A common question is how Cox can estimate two slopes when the baseline hazard has not been specified. The answer is more precise than “software finds the best HR.” Differentiating the log-partial likelihood yields, for each coefficient, a sum of observed covariate minus weighted average covariate within the risk set. At an event with covariate vector x_event, the contribution to the score vector is x_event−Σrᵢxᵢ/Σrᵢ. This derivative follows because the derivative of log(Σexp(xᵢᵀβ)) equals the weighted average of the covariates. The algebra is simply the chain rule for the logarithm combined with the derivative of the exponential.

Score of partial log-likelihood
\[U(\beta)=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta}=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\left[x_{j(k)}-\frac{\sum_{i\in R(t_k)}x_i e^{x_i^\top\beta}}{\sum_{i\in R(t_k)}e^{x_i^\top\beta}}\right]\]

Calculate the very first contribution before fitting, with β_W=β_M=0. Every relative weight is one. At month 2, there are eight people with high workload among sixteen, and eight with mentoring among sixteen, so the risk-set mean covariate vector is (8/16,8/16)=(0.5,0.5). Employee E01 has (W,M)=(0,0), giving score contribution (0−0.5,0−0.5)=(−0.5,−0.5). This says that, for this single event, an increase in either coefficient would reduce the relative probability assigned to E01, because E01 does not possess that characteristic. But one event cannot determine the final sign: the score aggregates all eight distinct event times and their changing risk sets.

At β=(0,0), independently summing every event contribution yields the overall score approximately (2.088462,−0.332051). Thus zero is not the optimum: the likelihood initially increases when workload coefficient rises, while the mentoring direction is slightly negative. After numerical optimization, the score is approximately (0,0) to computational precision, producing β=(1.158605,−0.220812). The log-partial likelihood increases from about −18.090575 at zero to −16.900825 at the fitted point. Because the log-partial likelihood is concave for the standard Cox model with distinct events and finite identifiable coefficients, finding its maximum is a coherent optimization problem. An approximately zero gradient is a useful computational check, not evidence that the model assumptions are true.

The observed information matrix—the negative Hessian—is built from weighted covariate variability within the risk sets. If workload almost never varied among those remaining, its coefficient would be weakly identified and the standard error could be very large. That connects matrix curvature to a practical concern: there must be meaningful covariate variation among employees still eligible to fail at the event times. A large dataset can still provide weak information for a specific effect if that contrast is absent from most informative risk sets.

22B. A second full arithmetic audit: from weights to a survival profile

We can reconstruct a longer chain without relying on a fitted summary table. First encode low workload/no mentoring as (0,0) and high workload/no mentoring as (1,0); the latter risk score is exp(1.158605)=3.185486. Second define risk-set membership directly from the observed durations and event indicators. Third calculate each weighted denominator as the sum of risk scores for all people present immediately before the departure. At month 8, for instance, ten people remain and the sum of their relative weights is approximately 18.439586. Because exactly one employee leaves at month 8, the Breslow baseline increment is 1/18.439586≈0.054231.

Fourth accumulate all event increments up to the chosen horizon. At month 8, previous cumulative baseline hazard is about 0.160706 at month 6, so Ĥ₀(8)=0.160706+0.054231≈0.214937. At month 10, one more departure occurs, with denominator 15.083381 and increment 1/15.083381≈0.066298, giving Ĥ₀(10)=0.214937+0.066298≈0.281235. Fifth translate from cumulative hazard to survival by evaluating exp(−Ĥ₀×risk score). That produces 0.754851 for (0,0) and 0.408252 for (1,0).

Finally check logical constraints: both probabilities must be between zero and one; survival should not increase as the horizon becomes longer; and the higher-risk profile should have lower survival when the baseline cumulative hazard is positive. At month 8, the reference survival is exp(−0.214937)≈0.80659, exceeding its month-10 survival 0.75485. A calculation yielding the opposite ordering would indicate a sign error, incorrect cumulative addition or time-index problem. We have thus independently checked six steps: encoding, risk sets, weights, baseline increments, cumulative hazard and survival transformation.

This chain is especially useful when building a webpage chart. A smooth decorative curve could look appealing while hiding incorrect mathematics. Our plotted step curves instead use the eight computed baseline increments. They show the four profiles at the same event times and identify the month-10 comparison. Graphics are evidence only when the underlying numbers, transformation and interpretation are auditable.

22C. Conditional versus marginal survival: an important distinction

The Cox equation supplies survival conditional on a particular predictor vector. The function S(t|W=1,M=0) describes a specified high-workload, non-mentored profile. It is not automatically the average survival among everyone who currently works in a high-workload department, because such a department may contain different mentoring arrangements and other covariate mixtures. A population-level survival summary may require averaging individualized predictions over a defined covariate distribution, often called standardization.

Consider a hypothetical future department with exactly equal shares of high-workload employees with and without mentoring, while other predictors match our illustrative model. If we used this mixture only for demonstration, the model-based month-10 average survival would be (0.408252+0.487546)/2≈0.447899. This differs from the survival calculated at the average mentoring variable M=0.5, which is exp[−0.281235×exp(1.158605−0.110406)]≈0.448330 and need not agree exactly, due to nonlinear exponentiation. Averages of predictions and predictions evaluated at average covariates are different mathematical operations.

This matters for decisions about teams, clinics or customer cohorts. An HR for a predictor is a conditional contrast, whereas the expected proportion still event-free in a population depends on that population's distribution of predictors and the baseline. Even if the coefficients were estimated precisely, transporting the predictions to another employer might fail if baseline departure rates, measurement definitions or workforce composition differ. Extrapolation is a research question, not an automatic property of exponential regression.

The same reasoning clarifies why an adjusted Cox HR is generally not a risk ratio at a chosen time. A risk ratio at month ten would compare (1−S_A(10))/(1−S_B(10)), while the HR compares instantaneous hazard rates. Using the two illustrative profiles, the modeled event probabilities by ten months are 1−0.408252=0.591748 and 1−0.754851=0.245149. Their ratio is approximately 2.414, not 3.185. The difference is not a mistake; the two ratios answer different questions. This numerical contrast should be remembered whenever HRs are communicated to readers outside statistics.

22D. Data collection and design pitfalls that formulas cannot fix

Before a Cox model can be meaningful, investigators must decide what the event actually is. A resignation, an involuntary termination, a transfer to another department and the expiration of a temporary contract can represent distinct processes. Combining them into one event changes the target of inference; treating one event type as censoring while it prevents the event of interest introduces competing risks, whose interpretation requires additional care. The current fictional cohort assumes a single unambiguous event and no competing event. This assumption belongs in the study description and must not be smuggled into a real analysis.

Time zero must also be aligned. If the observation starts on the date an employee becomes eligible for mentoring rather than the hiring date, the analysis may select survivors who remained long enough to receive mentoring. A comparison that labels someone “mentored” using information received after month six but counts their first six months as mentored creates immortal-time bias. We avoided it here by defining mentoring at hiring and keeping it fixed. A more realistic investigation should either define the exposure at baseline or use a properly specified time-varying covariate representation.

Another problem is administrative censoring by calendar date. Employees hired recently have shorter potential follow-up even if their characteristics are unrelated to eventual departure. That fact by itself does not invalidate survival methods, because censoring is expected. But if hiring season or department influences both the covariates and departure risk, additional modeling and careful assessment are needed. A reported dataset should distinguish time since hiring from calendar date, identify gaps in human-resources records and verify that no employee appears twice inadvertently.

Finally, consider whether observations are independent. Employees sharing a supervisor, shift or facility may have correlated outcomes. Ignoring this dependence can make conventional standard errors misleading even if point estimates remain interpretable. Cluster-robust variance procedures, frailty models or carefully designed hierarchical analyses may be appropriate depending on the research question. With sixteen constructed employees, we cannot honestly demonstrate their empirical reliability; the lesson instead makes the necessary assumptions explicit.

22E. A practical validation protocol and release decision

A model that fits a dataset is not automatically a good predictive instrument. For validation, prespecify the outcome and predictor definitions, preserve a chronological or otherwise defensible external evaluation sample, and ensure no predictors are measured after the forecast origin. In a new sample, assess discrimination, calibration at meaningful horizons, and whether censoring is handled correctly in the evaluation. Avoid reporting ordinary classification accuracy on censored records as if their eventual labels were known. An employee censored before twelve months may not have a known twelve-month departure status.

Calibration asks whether groups assigned a predicted survival near 0.70 actually show roughly that survival frequency after appropriate censoring adjustment. Discrimination asks whether employees at higher modeled risk tend to experience earlier events than lower-risk employees, as measured with survival-aware methods. Neither is equivalent to testing whether a coefficient has p<0.05. An HR can be large but poorly estimated, a significant predictor can offer little predictive improvement, and a model can discriminate while systematically exaggerating event probabilities.

Suppose the intended decision is to allocate mentoring resources next quarter. The sample's mentoring HR is 0.802 with a CI spanning roughly 0.20 to 3.24, so this cohort does not establish a favorable effect, let alone a cost-effective causal policy. A defensible next step would be a study with a larger number of observed events, appropriate measurement at baseline, planned adjustment for relevant confounders, evaluation of proportional hazards and perhaps a randomized program comparison if feasible. The operational recommendation should state what is unknown, not just what numerical point estimate looks favorable.

Final understanding check: Can you explain, without viewing source code, why the month-5 denominator excludes E03, why the first likelihood factor is 1/30.166763, why exp(β_W) compares instantaneous hazards but not event probabilities, and why month-10 survival uses Ĥ₀(10)? Can you then say why those estimates are not sufficient evidence that mentoring causally prevents departures? If any answer is unclear, revisit the corresponding derivation and reproduce it from the full CSV before claiming mastery.

23. Worked exercises and transfer

Exercise 1 — Identify the outcome and the censoring indicator

Problem. E03 has observed follow-up of four months and event=0; E04 has five months and event=1. Does either record supply a complete time to departure? Which employees can enter the risk set at month 3?

Solution. E04 has a complete observed event time of five months. E03 only tells us the true departure time exceeds four months under the observation convention, so replacing it by a failure at month 4 would be wrong. Both E03 and E04 are still at risk just before month 3. E03 is removed from future risk sets after its censoring at month 4, while E04 contributes at month 5 immediately before its own event. Interpret the event indicator together with duration, not independently of it.

Exercise 2 — Compute a risk set rather than guessing its size

Problem. Reconstruct the risk set size at month 5 using the full cohort.

Solution. Start with sixteen people at month zero. E01 departed at month 2, E02 departed at month 3, and E03 was censored at month 4. These three can no longer be at risk at month 5. Therefore n(5)=16−1−1−1=13. E04, departing at month 5, is still included immediately before the event. If we excluded E04 too soon we would calculate twelve and use an incorrect likelihood denominator.

Exercise 3 — Translate a coefficient to an HR and back

Problem. Using β_W=1.158605, obtain the adjusted HR. What coefficient would correspond to HR=0.5?

Solution. Exponentiating the fitted coefficient gives exp(1.158605)≈3.185486. Conversely β=ln(0.5)≈−0.693147. The first is associated with higher conditional instantaneous departure hazard for high workload, holding mentoring fixed; the second would represent half the hazard. Neither number alone reveals a six-month departure probability, because that requires baseline cumulative hazard.

Exercise 4 — Verify the first likelihood factor

Problem. At month 2 all sixteen employees are at risk, with four of each profile. Compute the conditional contribution for E01.

Solution. The relative weights for (0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1) are 1,3.185486,0.801867,2.554337. Multiply each by its frequency four and sum: 4+12.741944+3.207469+10.217349=30.166762. E01 has weight one, so its contribution is 1/30.166762≈0.033149. This is the relative probability of being the observed event conditional on one event in that risk set, not the unconditional probability of leaving in that month.

Exercise 5 — Compare two non-reference profiles

Problem. Estimate the hazard ratio for a high-workload employee without mentoring versus a low-workload employee with mentoring.

Solution. The first profile's score is exp(β_W)=3.185486; the second's is exp(β_M)=0.801867. The ratio is 3.185486/0.801867≈3.9726, equivalently exp(β_W−β_M)=exp(1.379417). Both profiles differ in two predictors, so it would be wrong to label this contrast simply “the workload HR.” State numerator, denominator, and which values are held fixed.

Exercise 6 — Confidence interval and inferential limits

Problem. Construct the approximate 95% CI for workload HR, with SE=0.818825 and β=1.158605.

Solution. The normal critical value is approximately 1.96. The log-scale margin is 1.96×0.818825≈1.6049; the limits are approximately −0.4463 and 2.7635. Exponentiation gives [0.6400,15.8548]. Because the interval covers one, a two-sided 5% Wald test does not reject the no-workload-association null. Its width also signals that ratios both below one and very far above one remain plausible under the approximation and model.

Exercise 7 — Breslow baseline increment at month 3

Problem. Given denominators 30.166763 at month 2 and 29.166763 at month 3, compute Ĥ₀(3).

Solution. Each event is unique, so its contribution is one divided by the risk-weight total. Calculate 1/30.166763≈0.0331491 and 1/29.166763≈0.0342856. Sum to get 0.0674347. This value is cumulative hazard rather than probability. To obtain reference survival at month 3, evaluate exp(−0.0674347)≈0.934789.

Exercise 8 — Translate HR into two month-10 probabilities

Problem. At month 10, baseline cumulative hazard is 0.281235. Compute survival for the reference profile and for high workload without mentoring.

Solution. Reference risk score is one, giving exp(−0.281235)=0.754851. High workload multiplies cumulative hazard by 3.185486, giving exp(−0.281235×3.185486)=0.408252. The difference is 0.754851−0.408252≈0.346599, or 34.66 percentage points of modeled survival by this horizon. It is a conditional association-based prediction for synthetic profiles, not the estimated causal effect of eliminating workload.

Exercise 9 — Understand tied-event handling

Problem. At a separate hypothetical tied time, R=12, with two departures having weights 2 and 3. Calculate the Breslow and Efron contributions.

Solution. Their numerator is 2×3=6. Breslow uses denominator 12²=144, so the factor is 6/144≈0.041667. Efron subtracts half of the failed group's total weight for the second denominator: 12×[12−(2+3)/2]=12×9.5=114, giving 6/114≈0.052632. These are alternative approximations under a declared convention. The example is not part of the original cohort and should never be silently mixed with the original eight-event likelihood.

Exercise 10 — Repair an incorrect censoring denominator

Problem. Someone includes E03 in the month-5 denominator despite its censoring at month 4. Their denominator is 25.981276 instead of 22.795790. Explain the error and the corrected operation.

Solution. E03 supplied valid information before month 4, but not beyond the censoring time. Its fitted weight, 3.185486, must be subtracted before constructing the month-5 risk set. Hence 25.981276−3.185486=22.795790. Keeping a censored employee at risk indefinitely changes every later competing event factor. The calculation demonstrates why the time index is part of the likelihood definition, not just a display column.

Exercise 11 — Contrast model predictions with causes

Problem. A manager reads HR=0.802 for mentoring and announces that mentoring prevents 19.8% of employee departures. Identify all unsupported steps.

Solution. First, 1−0.802=19.8% describes a proportional difference in instantaneous hazard, not an absolute number or proportion of departures prevented. Second, this is a conditional association adjusted for workload only; mentoring selection and unobserved variables may confound it. Third, the CI [0.1987,3.2366] is very broad and includes one. Fourth, predicting survival at a concrete horizon requires baseline hazard. A defensible report would describe the estimated HR, its uncertainty, and the need for stronger design and validation before a causal claim.

Exercise 12 — Calculate survival for a combined profile

Problem. Predict survival at ten months for employees with high workload and mentoring.

Solution. The combined linear predictor is β_W+β_M=1.158605−0.220812=0.937793. Its risk factor is exp(0.937793)≈2.554337. Multiply Ĥ₀(10)=0.281235 by that factor to obtain about 0.71831; exponentiate the negative to get Ŝ≈0.487546. The result lies between the high-workload/no-mentoring profile's 0.408252 and the reference profile's 0.754851. That qualitative ordering is a useful numerical sanity check, but not a causal conclusion.

Exercise 13 — Apply the student understanding test

Problem. A fellow student says: “Cox assumes constant hazard because it reports one HR, and it cannot calculate survival because it has no baseline.” Correct both statements using equations and the example.

Solution. Cox permits time-varying h₀(t) but assumes fixed relative multipliers exp(xᵀβ), so only the ratio of hazards is constant across time for two fixed profiles. The baseline is absent from the partial-likelihood coefficient estimation, not from the survival model. After fitting, the Breslow estimator yields Ĥ₀(t), allowing Ŝ(t|x)=exp[−Ĥ₀(t)exp(xᵀβ)]. At ten months it predicts about 0.754851 for the reference profile. A student who can derive both distinctions understands more than the software syntax.

Exercise 14 — Independent transfer: a new organization

Problem. A maintenance business follows replacement technicians for a year, records early training and shift assignment at hiring, and observes only six departures. Design the minimum defensible Cox analysis and state what it cannot conclude.

Solution. Define cohort eligibility, hiring as time zero, departure as event and administrative follow-up as right censoring. Record duration in consistent months, baseline training and shift assignment, with one record per technician; verify event counts and noninformative censoring assumptions. Construct risk sets immediately before every departure and fit a model with prespecified predictors, declaring the tie rule. Report the HR for training holding shift fixed, with its CI and the number of events; estimate the baseline and a one-year survival prediction only if the horizon is supported. With six events, fitting many predictors is precarious, and broad uncertainty should be expected. Assess plausible time-varying effects, clustering and confounding. Do not claim the training caused retention improvements without a design that supports causal identification. This solution transfers the workflow while preserving its substantive limits.
