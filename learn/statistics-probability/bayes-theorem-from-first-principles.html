01An alert changes probability; it does not identify certainty

A security detector alerts on 90% of intrusions. A new session triggers an alert. Does that mean the session has a 90% probability of being an intrusion? No: the first percentage concerns alerts among intrusions, whereas the question concerns intrusions among alerts. Legitimate sessions can also generate alerts, and they may greatly outnumber intrusive sessions.

Bayes' Theorem connects these two directions of conditioning through the joint probability and the overall probability of the evidence. It updates the probability assigned to a hypothesis after an observed result. It does not change the underlying session or prove that an alert causes an intrusion.

You will derive the theorem from Guide 07's multiplication rule, build the evidence denominator by total probability, and reproduce a posterior with natural frequencies. You will define Prior, Likelihood, Posterior and Evidence before using them, distinguish sensitivity and specificity from predictive values, and explain how a different base rate changes the answer. A multiple-hypothesis example and sequential-evidence contrast extend the same reasoning.

Preparation and scope

We recall conditional probability P(H|E) = P(H∩E)/P(E), with P(E)>0. Basic fractions and algebra suffice; calculus and programming are unnecessary. This guide updates probabilities of finite hypotheses. Bayesian estimation of continuous parameters, prior distributions over parameters and posterior intervals are developed in Guide 57. Knowing Bayes' formula now is a foundation for that later work, not a substitute for its modeling choices.

02Specify the hypothesis, evidence and target population

The main example is an original constructed cybersecurity model, not measured performance of a real product. One unit is a network session during a defined monitoring period. H means the session is classified as an intrusion under a stated reference rule; Hᶜ means it is not. A means the detector emits an alert, and Aᶜ means no alert. Both classifications are binary and complete in the illustrative model.

The target is a session selected from the stated population before consulting its alert. Its intrusion probability is 2%. Among intrusions, the alert probability is 90%. Among nonintrusions, the no-alert probability is 95%. These probabilities all refer to the same population, time definition and detector configuration.

p = P(H)Prior intrusion0.02
s = P(A | H)Sensitivity0.90
c = P(Aᶜ | Hᶜ)Specificity0.95

An alert is not the definition of intrusion. Otherwise the model would confuse a prediction with its reference classification and could not represent false alerts. The complement Hᶜ includes every session outside the reference intrusion definition; it is not a separate unknown category.

We will translate these probabilities into a fully constructed cohort of 10,000 sessions to make the arithmetic visible. The cohort is an exact illustration of stipulated probabilities, not a forecast that every real group of that size must have those counts. Real prevalence and detection rates would require data, evaluation and uncertainty analysis.

03Define detector rates before interpreting them

Sensitivity, also called the true-positive rate, is P(A|H): the probability of alert among intrusive sessions. Here s = 0.90. Specificity is P(Aᶜ|Hᶜ): the probability of no alert among nonintrusive sessions. Here c = 0.95. Both condition on the reference state rather than on what the detector reports.

The false-positive rate is f = P(A|Hᶜ) = 1 − c = 0.05. The false-negative rate is P(Aᶜ|H) = 1 − s = 0.10. These complements retain their respective conditioning group. A 5% false-positive rate does not mean 5% of alerts are false; that latter probability conditions on A, not Hᶜ.

The positive predictive value, PPV, is P(H|A), the probability of intrusion among alerts. The negative predictive value, NPV, is P(Hᶜ|Aᶜ), the probability of no intrusion among sessions without alerts. Their denominators are detector-result groups. In classification terminology, positive-class Precision is the same conditional quantity as PPV under the corresponding definitions.

We have not yet calculated PPV or NPV. The two within-class detector rates alone do not determine them without class proportions. Likewise Accuracy means overall correct classification probability, P(H∩A) + P(Hᶜ∩Aᶜ); it is neither sensitivity nor specificity. A precise metric name and denominator are more informative than calling a detector “90% accurate.”

04A prior is relative to the evidence not yet used

A Prior probability is the probability assigned to a hypothesis before incorporating the specific evidence currently being processed. Write p = P(H) = 0.02 and P(Hᶜ) = 1 − p = 0.98. The base rate, or prevalence in this population, supplies the prior class proportion in the example.

“Prior” does not mean unsupported guess or permanently fixed belief. A prior can summarize earlier studies, earlier monitoring, design information or a stated model. The word describes its position relative to new evidence. Once an alert has been incorporated, using that alert again as if it were new would count information twice.

Background information must be consistent across all inputs. If p refers to all monitored sessions while the detector rates refer only to a preselected high-risk group, combining them may be invalid. The relevant prior is for the population from which the session was selected before the current alert was observed.

A high-risk queue selected using the same alert cannot supply an untouched prior for that alert. It may define a legitimate new reference population, but the information already used in selection must then be accounted for. Bayes' arithmetic does not remove selection bias or identify missing reference labels.

The prior gives the relative size of competing hypotheses before the current result. The next ingredient explains how compatible that result is with each hypothesis; neither ingredient alone answers the posterior question.

05Likelihood describes evidence under a hypothesis

For the observed alert A, the Likelihood of that result under H is P(A|H) = 0.90; under Hᶜ it is P(A|Hᶜ) = 0.05. Viewed across hypotheses, these values describe how compatible the same observed result is with each hypothesis. They are not probabilities assigned to the hypotheses.

For fixed H, P(A|H) + P(Aᶜ|H) = 1 because A and Aᶜ partition possible detector results. But P(A|H) + P(A|Hᶜ) need not equal one: the conditioning hypothesis changes. Here 0.90 + 0.05 = 0.95. Normalizing those two likelihoods alone would silently impose equal prior weights, not the actual 2% and 98% weights.

Multiplying a prior by its likelihood produces joint mass. For intrusions and alert, P(H)P(A|H) = 0.02 × 0.90 = 0.018. For nonintrusions and alert, 0.98 × 0.05 = 0.049. These are the two disjoint ways the observed evidence can arise.

Despite the much smaller false-positive rate, the nonintrusion alert mass is larger: 0.049 versus 0.018. The comparison reflects both compatibility and population size. Likelihood alone favors H, whereas the prior greatly favors Hᶜ. Bayes combines them and then rescales the resulting masses.

Later likelihood-based estimation treats likelihood as a function of model parameters with the observed data fixed. The present finite-hypothesis usage introduces that distinction without requiring parameter estimation.

06Derive Bayes from the same joint event in two directions

From conditional probability, P(H|E) = P(H∩E)/P(E), provided P(E)>0. From the multiplication rule, P(H∩E) = P(H)P(E|H), provided P(H)>0 for this conditional factor. Substitute the second expression into the first:

P(H | E) = P(E | H)P(H) / P(E)

This is Bayes' Theorem. It is not a new independence assumption. It follows from the symmetry H∩E = E∩H and ordinary conditioning. We use E for generic evidence and A for the alert in the running example.

The Posterior probability P(H|E) is the hypothesis probability after observing E. The numerator combines prior and likelihood; the denominator makes all posterior hypothesis weights sum to one. It is called the Evidence probability, or marginal probability of the observation. It does not mean a probability that “the evidence is true.”

The practical challenge is usually finding P(E), not memorizing the ratio. Every hypothesis capable of producing E contributes to that denominator. Omitting the nonintrusion route in the alert example would normalize H against itself and falsely return certainty.

For positive-prior hypotheses the displayed likelihood factor is defined by ordinary conditioning. A zero-prior event has zero joint mass with every E and therefore remains posterior-zero whenever P(E)>0, without needing to evaluate an undefined P(E|H). This separates a valid support conclusion from invalid division by zero.

07Build the evidence denominator by total probability

H and Hᶜ form a disjoint, exhaustive partition. Every alert comes from exactly one of them. Add their joint masses:

P(A) = P(H)P(A | H) + P(Hᶜ)P(A | Hᶜ) = 0.02 × 0.90 + 0.98 × 0.05 = 0.018 + 0.049 = 0.067

Thus 6.7% of all sessions alert under the model. This probability is neither the 90% sensitivity nor the 5% false-positive rate. It is their prior-weighted mixture across the whole population.

The binary Bayes formula can now be written with p = P(H), s = P(A|H) and f = P(A|Hᶜ), assuming 0<p<1 and positive evidence mass:

P(H | A) = sp / [sp + f(1 − p)]

Square brackets group the entire denominator. The false-alert contribution is f(1 − p), not f alone: only the nonintrusion share can generate that contribution. Also, s and f are probabilities conditional on different classes; adding them without class weights does not produce P(A).

An immediate check is P(A) between f and s, since it is a weighted average. Here 0.05 ≤ 0.067 ≤ 0.90. This does not prove every input is valid, but it catches some denominator errors. The four joint masses must also be nonnegative and sum to one.

08Finish the update and interpret the result

Substitute the joint intrusion-alert mass and evidence mass:

P(H | A) = 0.018 / 0.067 = 18/67 ≈ 0.268657 ≈ 26.87%

The posterior is about 26.87%, not 90%. It is nevertheless much higher than the prior 2%. An informative alert can substantially increase a rare hypothesis's probability without making that hypothesis more likely than its complement.

The competing posterior is P(Hᶜ|A) = 0.049/0.067 = 49/67 ≈ 73.13%. The exact sum 18/67 + 49/67 = 1 checks normalization. Approximately 73.13% of alerts are nonintrusions in this model; that false-discovery proportion among alerts is not the 5% false-positive rate among nonintrusions.

There is no contradiction between sensitivity 90% and posterior below 50%. The former describes performance inside a relatively small class; the latter weighs both sources of alerts. They answer different conditional questions.

Reconstruct the joint as a check

Multiplying back, (18/67)(67/1000) = 18/1000 = 0.018 = P(H∩A). This confirms that the posterior and evidence reconstruct the original joint. Retain the exact fraction 18/67 in later calculations rather than treating the rounded 26.87% as exact.

09Recover the posterior from natural frequencies

Natural frequencies keep the nested reference groups visible instead of presenting only isolated percentages. Construct 10,000 sessions: 0.02 × 10,000 = 200 are intrusive and 9,800 are not. Within the 200 intrusions, 0.90 × 200 = 180 alert and 20 do not. Within the 9,800 nonintrusions, 0.05 × 9,800 = 490 alert and 9,310 do not.

There are 180 + 490 = 670 alerts. Among those, 180 are intrusions, so P(H|A) = 180/670 = 18/67. This agrees with Bayes' formula by a different representation. The denominator includes all alerts, not all intrusions and not all sessions.

The four cells sum to 180 + 20 + 490 + 9,310 = 10,000. Row totals are 200 and 9,800; column totals are 670 and 9,330. These checks make omissions and denominator changes visible.

If these were real observed counts, 180/670 would be an observed conditional proportion. In our constructed cohort it exactly represents the specified finite selection model, where a session is selected uniformly from the cohort. If instead probabilities generate random future sessions, these counts describe an illustrative scale, not guaranteed realized frequencies. Noninteger scaled counts for other parameter choices should be interpreted as expected numbers, not rounded into fabricated observations.

10Read the tree forward and normalize the alert leaves

A tree separates prior class weights, within-class alert probabilities and final joint masses. Multiply along a path; add the two alert leaves; then divide the intrusion-alert leaf by that total. The final division reverses the question from class-to-alert to alert-to-class.

The four terminal masses are 0.018,0.002,0.049,0.931. Their sum is one. The alert leaves sum to 0.067, and the no-alert leaves sum to 0.933. Branch labels such as 0.90 and 0.05 are conditional; leaf labels are joint and use the original whole-population reference.

Do not add likelihoods from different class nodes as if they were disjoint population masses. The paths must first receive their prior weights. Nor should the visual size of a node be interpreted as probability: this is a schematic tree, and its numerical labels carry the masses.

The common-length bars compare the same two hypotheses before and after evidence. The intrusion share increases from 2% to about 26.87%; the complement decreases accordingly. Normalization reallocates weight between hypotheses within a model, rather than creating more observed intrusions.

11No alert is different evidence and needs a new denominator

For evidence Aᶜ, use its likelihood under each class. The two no-alert contributions are P(H)P(Aᶜ|H) = 0.02 × 0.10 = 0.002 and P(Hᶜ)P(Aᶜ|Hᶜ) = 0.98 × 0.95 = 0.931. Therefore:

P(H | Aᶜ) = 0.002 / (0.002 + 0.931) = 0.002/0.933 = 2/933 ≈ 0.2144%

The probability of no intrusion after no alert is NPV = P(Hᶜ|Aᶜ) = 931/933 ≈ 99.7856%. It complements P(H|Aᶜ), not P(H|A). PPV and NPV are not complements because they condition on different detector results.

No-alert evidence lowers intrusion probability from the prior 2%, but does not make it zero. Twenty illustrative intrusions fall into the no-alert group. A conclusion about an actual session would additionally depend on whether the stated model and reference population are appropriate.

12Change the base rate while holding detector rates fixed

Suppose the detector retains s = 0.90 and f = 0.05 in another population, but p = 0.10. The joint intrusion-alert contribution becomes 0.10 × 0.90 = 0.09; the nonintrusion-alert contribution becomes 0.90 × 0.05 = 0.045. Thus P(H|A) = 0.09/0.135 = 2/3 ≈ 66.67%, rather than 26.87%.

At a 1% base rate, the contributions are 0.009 and 0.0495, giving 0.009/0.0585 = 2/13 ≈ 15.38%. The same within-class rates can therefore support quite different posterior probabilities in populations with different class shares.

These are comparisons of stipulated models, not evidence that one intervention is available or less costly. In real detector design, changing a threshold may change both s and f together; choosing one rate freely while ignoring the other can describe an unattainable configuration.

If s = f = 0.05, an alert has the same probability in both classes. Its posterior is 10/(10 + 490) = 0.02, unchanged from the prior. An outcome provides no class-discriminating information when its likelihood is equal under the two positive-prior hypotheses.

14Boundary cases reveal the conditions of the formula

For fixed s>0 and f>0, sp/[sp + f(1 − p)] increases with p on 0<p<1 and approaches zero or one as p approaches zero or one. These are limits; do not use them to pretend that a conditional on a zero-mass class was defined by a ratio. The qualitative conclusion is that a fixed likelihood contrast does not erase an arbitrarily strong prior imbalance.

If f = 0, s>0 and p>0 with a defined intrusion likelihood, every alert has positive mass only under H, so P(H|A) = 1. If s = 0, f>0 and p<1 with a defined nonintrusion likelihood, alerts instead have posterior intrusion probability zero. If both alert contributions vanish, P(A) = 0 and conditioning on A is undefined; a software output of zero would conceal a model-support problem.

When does the posterior exceed one half? For positive evidence mass, compare sp with f(1 − p): sp>f(1 − p) implies p>f/(s + f), assuming s + f>0. Here the threshold is 0.05/0.95 = 1/19 ≈ 5.26%. A prior 2% lies below it even though an alert favors H.

This 50% boundary is a probability comparison, not a universal action threshold. A costly missed intrusion and a costly false response can justify different operational decisions at the same posterior. Bayes computes uncertainty; a decision requires an additional objective.

15Update an exhaustive partition of several hypotheses

Let H₁,…,Hₘ be disjoint hypotheses covering the sample space, with positive priors pᵢ = P(Hᵢ). For fixed observed E, let ℓᵢ = P(E|Hᵢ). The joint contribution is wᵢ = pᵢℓᵢ. Summing gives P(E) = Σᵢwᵢ; if this sum is positive:

P(Hⱼ | E) = pⱼℓⱼ / Σᵢ pᵢℓᵢ

The index j identifies the hypothesis being updated; i runs over all competing parts. The hypotheses must not overlap or omit possible routes to E. A residual “other” category can be needed, with its own justified prior and likelihood rather than an invented zero.

Consider a separate constructed population of 1,000 sessions. H₁ means intrusion; H₂ means nonintrusive authorized automation; H₃ means all other nonintrusive sessions. These definitions are disjoint and exhaustive. Priors are 0.10,0.30,0.60; alert likelihoods are 0.80,0.20,0.10. This changes both priors and rates from the main binary case; it is not a breakdown of that original cohort.

16Normalization compares hypotheses, not just raw scores

The expression “posterior proportional to prior times likelihood” means that wᵢ = pᵢℓᵢ are unnormalized weights. They become probabilities only after division by their positive total. In the three-hypothesis case, 0.08 is joint mass, not posterior probability 8%; after conditioning on the alert, its posterior is 40%.

To check normalization algebraically, Σⱼ[wⱼ/Σᵢwᵢ] = (Σⱼwⱼ)/(Σᵢwᵢ) = 1. The common denominator matters. Normalizing each hypothesis against a different set of competitors would not produce a coherent posterior distribution over one partition.

Multiplying every unnormalized weight by the same positive constant leaves the posterior unchanged because the constant cancels. This explains why natural counts and joint probabilities produce the same answer: counts scale all contributions by the same cohort size. It does not permit rescaling one hypothesis alone without changing the model.

If the evidence has equal positive likelihood t under all hypotheses, wᵢ = tpᵢ and their sum is t; the posterior returns pᵢ. If an observation has zero total weight, there is no posterior ratio in this elementary model. Check data definitions and model support instead of forcing the scores to sum to one with arbitrary replacements.

17Posterior odds separate prior balance from evidence strength

For probability q with 0<q<1, Odds in favor mean q/(1 − q); conversely q = odds/(1 + odds). Odds compare a hypothesis with its complement and can exceed one. They are not themselves a probability.

Divide the two binary posterior expressions. The evidence denominator cancels, giving, when the quantities are finite and their denominators positive:

P(H | E)/P(Hᶜ | E) = [P(H)/P(Hᶜ)] × [P(E | H)/P(E | Hᶜ)]

The second ratio is the Likelihood Ratio for that observation. For an alert, LR + = s/f = 0.90/0.05 = 18. The prior odds are 0.02/0.98 = 1/49. Posterior odds are (1/49) × 18 = 18/49, and converting gives (18/49)/(1 + 18/49) = 18/67.

The alert multiplies prior odds by 18, but posterior probability is not 18 × 2% = 36%. Probability and odds use different transformations. For no alert, LR − = (1 − s)/c = 0.10/0.95 = 2/19; odds become (1/49)(2/19) = 2/931, yielding probability 2/933. This independently checks the earlier no-alert result.

An LR above one favors H relative to Hᶜ; below one favors the complement; equal to one leaves their odds unchanged. These statements concern relative evidence in the specified model. They do not guarantee that H has posterior probability above one half.

18Sequential updating must retain the previous evidence

After observing E₁, its posterior can serve as prior for new E₂. The new likelihoods must be P(E₂|H∩E₁) and P(E₂|Hᶜ∩E₁), not automatically P(E₂|H) and P(E₂|Hᶜ). This follows from applying the same theorem inside the information supplied by E₁.

For a separate two-alert model, suppose each detector has s = 0.90 and f = 0.05, and their alerts are conditionally independent within H and within Hᶜ. Then P(A₁∩A₂|H) = 0.90² = 0.81 and P(A₁∩A₂|Hᶜ) = 0.05² = 0.0025. The within-class independence assumptions justify these products.

With prior 0.02, the contributions are 0.02 × 0.81 = 0.0162 and 0.98 × 0.0025 = 0.00245. Normalize: 0.0162/(0.0162 + 0.00245) = 324/373 ≈ 86.86%. The odds calculation gives (1/49) × 18 × 18 = 324/49, producing the same posterior.

Conditional independence within classes does not mean the two alerts are independent overall. Their joint probability 0.01865 differs from 0.067² = 0.004489. The common hidden class can make their population-level outcomes dependent.

19A duplicated alert is not a second independent observation

Suppose A₂ is an exact copy of A₁, perhaps the same event forwarded to another dashboard. Given A₁, P(A₂|H∩A₁) = P(A₂|Hᶜ∩A₁) = 1 for the positive-mass classes in our model. The second observation has conditional likelihood ratio 1, so the posterior remains 18/67, not 324/373.

Equivalently, A₁∩A₂ = A₁ as sets. The joint likelihoods are 0.90 and 0.05, not their squares. Repeating an event's name does not create another probability experiment. This is a concrete way to detect double counting.

Two genuinely different detectors can also share errors because they use the same logs, signature rules or upstream signal. “Different software” is not a proof of conditional independence. If their dependence is known, use their joint likelihoods or likelihoods conditional on the earlier observation. If it is unknown, describe the limitation instead of automatically multiplying marginal detection rates.

The theorem itself remains valid under dependence; it is the substituted likelihood model that can be wrong. Separating a universally valid probability identity from a particular independence assumption prevents an accurate formula from giving unjustified confidence.

20Choose and disclose a prior rather than hide it

The prior should correspond to the selection process and background information relevant to the case. A base rate for all sessions may differ from that for a preselected server group, a geographic region or a specified time window. A prior chosen after inspecting the same alert may already incorporate the evidence being used.

In a constructed exercise, the prior is part of the model contract. In an applied analysis, report its source, population, time period, measurement rule and uncertainty. An estimated prevalence is not an exact known population constant merely because it is inserted into an exact probability identity.

If plausible priors vary, calculate a range of posterior scenarios while stating which likelihoods are held fixed. The table earlier does this for six priors. Such a range is a sensitivity analysis, not automatically a confidence interval or a Bayesian credible interval. Those intervals require additional inferential models and definitions.

A prior of zero assigns no probability mass to H. With positive evidence probability, P(H|E) = 0 follows directly from its zero joint mass. New evidence cannot restore excluded support within that same model. If a real event contradicts the model's support, revise the modeling assumptions rather than claim that Bayes has independently repaired them.

Being explicit about a prior makes disagreement inspectable: readers can distinguish a disagreement about prevalence from one about detector performance or the event definition. Concealing a prior as “no assumptions” does not remove its effect.

21Audit normalization, units and model support

A finite Bayesian update needs nonnegative priors summing to one, likelihoods valid for their defined observation, a disjoint exhaustive hypothesis partition and positive total evidence mass. The posterior must be nonnegative and sum to one over that partition.

Suppose someone reports p = 0.02, P(A|H) = 0.90 and P(A) = 0.01. The intrusion-alert mass would be 0.018, larger than the total alert mass 0.01. Their proposed posterior 0.018/0.01 = 1.8 exposes incoherence. Do not clip 1.8 down to 1; repair the inconsistent inputs or populations. With our actual f = 0.05, total probability requires P(A) = 0.067.

For a partition, check priors before multiplying. Three weights 0.10,0.30,0.70 sum to 1.10, so they are not a prior probability distribution as written. Normalizing them would define a different prior; it should be an explicit modeling choice, not a silent correction to an empirical claim.

Counts and probabilities are different units of representation. The ratio 180/0.067 mixes a count with a probability and is invalid as a posterior. Use 180/670 or 0.018/0.067. When all counts are multiplied by the same positive cohort size, the ratio is unchanged, which is why the two correct forms agree.

A coherent model can still be empirically inaccurate. These checks establish internal consistency, not that a deployed detector or real session population follows the stipulated probabilities.

22Recognize the common substitutions that answer another question

“The detector finds 90% of intrusions, so an alert means 90% intrusion.” This replaces P(A|H) by P(H|A), changing the denominator. Reconstruct true and false alerts, then normalize.

“Specificity 95% means only 5% of alerts are false.” Specificity conditions on Hᶜ, whereas the false share among alerts conditions on A. In our model the latter is 49/67 ≈ 73.13%.

“Normalize the two likelihoods; the prior is optional.” The ratio 0.90/(0.90 + 0.05) = 18/19 implicitly uses equal class priors. It is not the 2%-prevalence answer 18/67.

“The posterior is below 50%, so the evidence was uninformative.” It moved the probability from 2% to 26.87% and multiplied odds by 18. Evidence strength and final probability are different quantities.

“A second dashboard confirms the first, so square the likelihood.” An exact duplicated alert adds no information. The correct updated posterior stays 18/67; a conditional-independence model for two new alerts gives a different 324/373.

“A plausible percentage validates the detector.” A value between zero and one can come from mismatched populations, biased reference labels or incomplete hypotheses. State the unit, population, priors, likelihoods and evidence definition before trusting the arithmetic.

23Practice with changed priors, evidence and model assumptions

State the evidence and hypothesis partition before computing. Try each question before opening the complete solution. Use exact values in intermediate calculations.

A · A new alert model

A separate detector has prior intrusion probability 0.04, sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.90. Find the positive posterior by formula and a constructed 1,000-session cohort.

Solution. False-positive rate is 0.10. Joint alert masses are 0.04 × 0.80 = 0.032 and 0.96 × 0.10 = 0.096; evidence = 0.128. Posterior = 0.032/0.128 = 1/4 = 25%. In 1,000 illustrative sessions there are 40 intrusions, with 32 alerts and 8 misses; 960 nonintrusions, with 96 alerts and 864 correct negatives. Among 128 alerts, 32 are intrusions: 32/128 = 25%. Sensitivity 80% is not this posterior.

B · The same detector in a different population

Keep the preceding sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.90, but change the prior to 0.20. Compute the posterior and explain its change.

Solution. Joint contributions are 0.20 × 0.80 = 0.16 and 0.80 × 0.10 = 0.08, totaling 0.24. Posterior = 0.16/0.24 = 2/3 ≈ 66.67%. It rises from 25% because intrusion now has greater prior mass, not because detector rates improved. A 1,000-session representation has 160 true alerts and 80 false alerts. Those counts also give 160/240 = 2/3.

C · Negative evidence is not the complement of positive PPV

Use the model in A, with prior 0.04. Calculate intrusion probability after no alert and NPV. Compare with one minus the positive posterior.

Solution. No-alert masses are 0.04 × 0.20 = 0.008 and 0.96 × 0.90 = 0.864. Total = 0.872. P(H|Aᶜ) = 0.008/0.872 = 1/109 ≈ 0.9174%; NPV = 108/109 ≈ 99.0826%. One minus PPV is 1 − 1/4 = 3/4 = 75%, which is P(Hᶜ|A), a different condition. The no-alert counts 8 and 864 reproduce the correct denominator 872.

D · A three-source model

Three disjoint exhaustive sources have priors 0.50,0.30,0.20 and observation likelihoods 0.10,0.20,0.40. Find evidence and all posteriors.

Solution. Contributions are 0.05,0.06,0.08. Evidence = 0.19. Posteriors are 5/19 ≈ 26.32%, 6/19 ≈ 31.58%, 8/19 ≈ 42.11%. Exact sum = (5 + 6 + 8)/19 = 1. Rounded percentages can sum to 100.01% without a model inconsistency. The last source has the smallest prior but largest posterior because its contribution is largest. These source probabilities do not describe the original cybersecurity cohort.

E · Probability versus odds

A hypothesis has prior probability 0.10. Evidence has likelihood 0.60 under it and 0.20 under its complement. Calculate the likelihood ratio, posterior odds and posterior probability.

Solution. LR = 0.60/0.20 = 3. Prior odds = 0.10/0.90 = 1/9. Posterior odds = (1/9) × 3 = 1/3. Convert: (1/3)/(1 + 1/3) = 1/4 = 25%. Direct check: joint masses 0.06 and 0.18, evidence 0.24, ratio 0.06/0.24 = 0.25. Multiplying probability 0.10 by LR3 would incorrectly give 0.30.

F · A posterior probability above one

Someone combines prior 0.10, likelihood P(E|H) = 0.80 and evidence probability 0.05. Explain the problem. If P(E|Hᶜ) = 0.20, repair the denominator and update.

Solution. The supplied numbers imply joint 0.10 × 0.80 = 0.08, larger than total evidence 0.05. The resulting 0.08/0.05 = 1.6 is impossible, so clipping it is not a solution. With complement likelihood 0.20, total probability gives 0.08 + 0.90 × 0.20 = 0.08 + 0.18 = 0.26. Posterior = 0.08/0.26 = 4/13 ≈ 30.77%. The evidence denominator, not the probability bound, needed correction.

G · Sequential evidence and an exact copy

Start from the main posterior 18/67 after one alert. A genuinely new alert has likelihood ratio 18 conditional on the relevant previous information under the class model. Find the next posterior. What if the second report is an exact copy instead?

Solution. Current odds = (18/67)/(49/67) = 18/49. New odds = (18/49) × 18 = 324/49; probability = 324/(324 + 49) = 324/373 ≈ 86.86%. An exact copy has conditional likelihood 1 under both classes after the first alert, so conditional LR = 1. Odds remain 18/49 and posterior remains 18/67. Equal-looking reports do not establish independent evidence.

H · What prior produces a posterior above one half?

A binary detector has sensitivity 0.80 and false-positive rate 0.10. Derive the prior boundary for a positive posterior above 50%, then check p = 0.05 and p = 0.20.

Solution. Compare 0.80p with 0.10(1 − p): 0.90p>0.10, hence p>1/9 ≈ 11.11%. At p = 0.05, posterior = 0.04/(0.04 + 0.095) = 8/27 ≈ 29.63%. At p = 0.20, posterior = 0.16/(0.16 + 0.08) = 2/3 ≈ 66.67%. The inequalities agree. This mathematical 50% boundary does not specify an optimal response policy without costs and objectives.

24A posterior supports a decision without defining it

A defensible report for the main model says: “With prior intrusion probability 2%, sensitivity 90% and specificity 95%, the modeled probability of intrusion among alerts is 18/67 ≈ 26.87%. The model yields 670 alerts per constructed 10,000 sessions, including 180 intrusions and 490 nonintrusions.” It states the inputs and reference population rather than presenting the posterior as a permanent detector property.

An operations team might use the posterior to prioritize investigation, but its action also depends on response cost, missed-intrusion consequences, capacity and alternative evidence. A probability below 50% can still merit investigation; a probability above 50% does not automatically authorize a disruptive action. No such response is being executed or prescribed by this example.

The illustrative 670-alert workload is not a realized staffing forecast. Real workloads can depend on changing populations, correlated sessions, detector thresholds and reporting rules. Validation requires labeled data appropriate to those conditions, with uncertainty and possible label error assessed separately.

Report which numbers are specified, estimated or observed; which assumptions were transferred; and which posterior comparisons are sensitivity scenarios. Bayes makes the combination of assumptions transparent. It does not guarantee that the assumptions are accurate or that an operational decision will succeed.

25Build a reproducible finite update before continuous Bayes

A future lab can accept the main prior and detector rates, compute the four joint probabilities, and scale them to the constructed cohort. Verify row and column totals, evidence, positive and negative posteriors, and posterior-times-evidence reconstruction. It should reject inconsistent inputs and return an explicit undefined state for zero-probability evidence.

Next vary only p, then only s or f, keeping the scenario assumptions visible. Compare the formula with exact frequency ratios where integer scaling is possible. For the three-hypothesis model, return all unnormalized contributions, their sum and normalized posteriors. Checking only the largest posterior would miss omitted hypotheses or a normalization error.

Finally contrast two conditionally independent alerts with an exact duplicate. The activity should distinguish 324/373 from 18/67 and identify the likelihood assumption responsible. Exact arithmetic can verify these finite examples; a simulation illustrates frequencies under a specified model, not proof of Bayes or validation of an actual detector.

Guide 09 introduces Discrete Random Variables and PMFs; later distributions and inference supply additional models for observations. Guide 57 returns to Bayesian parameter inference, where priors and likelihoods may be functions over continuous parameters and evidence involves integration. The finite normalization developed here prepares that extension without requiring it prematurely.

26A working vocabulary tied to conditioning groups

Use the definitions with their denominators. Similar numerical values do not make two metrics or probability statements interchangeable.
