1. Why one predictor is not always enough

Imagine a labor economist asking whether years of formal education are associated with hourly wages. Employees also differ in work experience. A simple regression of wages on education alone mixes at least two sources of variation: education and whatever characteristics differ systematically among employees with different education levels. Multiple linear regression lets us describe the mean wage as a function of education and experience simultaneously. It does not, by itself, recreate a randomized experiment. This distinction between an adjusted statistical association and a causal effect is the central intellectual task of this guide.

A question about prediction asks how accurately a model estimates wages for someone with specified characteristics. A question about conditional association asks how the fitted mean changes when education differs by one year while experience stays at a fixed value. A causal question asks what wage would change for the same person if an intervention changed schooling. That last question needs additional design assumptions; holding a variable fixed inside an equation is not the same as changing it in the world.

Our work proceeds from data to a model, from a model to an algebraic estimate, and then from an estimate to uncertainty and limitations. At each step, we will ask what is observed, what is assumed, what the calculation actually identifies, and what a reader is entitled to conclude. We avoid treating a coefficient as self-explanatory merely because software prints a number.

2. The statistical model, its units, and its population meaning

Let \(Y_i\) denote hourly wage measured in dollars per hour for employee \(i\), \(E_i\) years of schooling, and \(X_i\) years of experience. A two-predictor linear mean model is

KEY EQUATION · STEP 2
\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_E E_i+\beta_X X_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n. \]

The index \(i\) labels a person; \(n\) is the number of people. The Greek \(\beta\)'s are population model coefficients, unknown quantities in a proposed data-generating model. The error \(\varepsilon_i\) collects deviations that this linear specification does not explain. If the conditional mean assumption \(\operatorname E(\varepsilon_i\mid E_i,X_i)=0\) holds, the mean wage among people with the stated predictors is \(\beta_0+\beta_EE_i+\beta_XX_i\).

Units are a useful error detector. The response and intercept have units dollars/hour; \(\beta_E\) has units (dollars/hour)/school-year and \(\beta_X\) has units (dollars/hour)/experience-year. Multiplying each slope by the corresponding number of years returns dollars/hour. A purported slope with units dollars rather than dollars/hour per year would not match this model. Experience and schooling are quantitative inputs here; the model assumes an additive linear conditional mean across the studied domain. We will not yet model interactions or curvature: those belong primarily to Guide 53.

3. What the word “holding constant” really claims

Suppose the fitted equation is \(\widehat Y=10+2E+1.5X\). Compare a worker with 14 years of schooling and 4 years of experience with an otherwise identical predictor profile having 15 years of schooling and still 4 years of experience. The first fitted wage is \(10+2(14)+1.5(4)=44\) dollars/hour. The second is \(10+2(15)+1.5(4)=46\). Their fitted conditional difference is 2 dollars/hour. It is not an assertion that someone could increase schooling instantly without altering anything else.

Formally, subtract two model predictions:

KEY EQUATION · STEP 3
\[ \widehat Y(E+1,X)-\widehat Y(E,X)=b_E. \]

The intercept and experience terms cancel. This algebra is why the coefficient is called a partial or adjusted slope: it compares two predictor configurations differing in one coordinate. It does not assert that actual employees with those configurations are exchangeable in ability, occupation, location, health, or selection into schooling. If relevant variables are omitted or the functional form is wrong, the conditional regression coefficient need not equal the effect of an intervention.

Keep three statements separate in reports: (1) the fitted difference given the model, (2) uncertainty about that difference in a population, and (3) causal interpretation. Only the first follows immediately from substituting numbers into the fitted equation. The second requires sampling and error assumptions. The third needs an identification strategy beyond least squares.

4. The complete synthetic dataset and its design

To make every operation inspectable, we constructed eight hypothetical workers. Each combination of schooling \(E\in\{12,16\}\) and experience \(X\in\{2,6\}\) is represented twice. The wage values were generated by the teaching rule \(Y=10+2E+1.5X+e\), where the residual choices within each cell are \(-1,+1\). That provenance matters: these are deliberately convenient arithmetic values, not a random sample from real employment records, and statistical intervals applied to them are illustrations of model calculations rather than empirical claims about actual wages.

There are \(n=8\) rows, \(k=2\) explanatory predictors, and \(p=k+1=3\) coefficients including the intercept. Distinguish \(k\) from \(p\), because formulas for degrees of freedom often use one or the other. The outcome is quantitative, each observation is one hypothetical employee, and all measurements share the same unit convention. Repeated predictor combinations are allowed: the rows of the design matrix must not all be identical, but individual rows may be repeated.

The factorial balance is a pedagogical choice. It makes schooling and experience vary independently in this tiny table, allowing us to see the geometry without difficult inverses. Real employment data typically do not have this balance. Later a second synthetic dataset shows how a simple slope differs when predictors are associated. A student should be able to reconstruct the table above by independently evaluating the generating rule in all eight rows.

5. A bridge from lists of numbers to vectors

A vector is an ordered list, written as a column for regression. Our response vector has eight entries, \(\mathbf y=(36,38,42,44,44,46,50,52)^\mathsf T\). A superscript \(\mathsf T\) means transpose: converting a row arrangement into a column or vice versa. For example, \((2,5)^\mathsf T\) is a column containing 2 and 5, while \((2,5)\) can denote the corresponding row. An \(m\times q\) matrix is an arrangement with \(m\) rows and \(q\) columns; the order matters.

Addition of vectors requires matching dimensions and occurs component by component. Multiplying a matrix \(A\) of dimension \(m\times q\) by a column vector \(v\) of dimension \(q\times1\) produces a vector of dimension \(m\times1\). Each output is the dot product of one row of \(A\) with \(v\). For example, \((1,12,2)\cdot(10,2,1.5)=1(10)+12(2)+2(1.5)=37\). The operation calculates the model's wage prediction for the predictor row \((E,X)=(12,2)\).

There is no meaningful matrix product \((8\times3)(8\times1)\), because the touching dimensions 3 and 8 do not match. Conversely, \((3\times8)(8\times1)\) is valid and yields a \(3\times1\) vector. This elementary shape check will prevent some of the most common mistakes when interpreting normal equations. You do not need a prior course in linear algebra to use the next steps, but you must read dimensions as carefully as physical units.

6. Build the design matrix and explain every column

The design matrix, traditionally denoted \(\mathbf X\), places one observation in each row. Its first column contains ones to represent the intercept, its second contains education years, and its third experience years. The model in compact form is \(\mathbf y=\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon\), with dimensions \((8\times1)=(8\times3)(3\times1)+(8\times1)\).

KEY EQUATION · STEP 6
\[ \mathbf X=\begin{bmatrix}1&12&2\\1&12&2\\1&12&6\\1&12&6\\1&16&2\\1&16&2\\1&16&6\\1&16&6\end{bmatrix},\qquad \boldsymbol\beta=\begin{bmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_E\\\beta_X\end{bmatrix}. \]

The letters \(X\) and \(\mathbf X\) should not be confused: \(X_i\) is the person's experience; bold \(\mathbf X\) is the entire matrix including both predictors. Notations like \(\mathbf X_{i\cdot}\) mean the whole \(i\)-th row. Matrix multiplication produces eight fitted means at once. The first row gives \(\beta_0+12\beta_E+2\beta_X\), and the eighth \(\beta_0+16\beta_E+6\beta_X\).

An intercept column is not decorative. Without it, the fitted surface is forced through a zero-wage point when all predictors are zero. That constraint may be substantively implausible and changes the residual identities and common \(R^2\) conventions. The coefficients for a fixed data table depend on how columns are defined; the response never supplies its own explanatory column. In a spreadsheet, constructing the design matrix is simply arranging exactly those predictor columns plus the column of ones.

7. What OLS minimizes and why residuals matter

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) selects coefficients to make the sum of squared vertical residuals as small as possible. A fitted value is \(\widehat y_i=b_0+b_EE_i+b_XX_i\); the residual is \(e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i\). The objective function is \(S(b_0,b_E,b_X)=\sum_{i=1}^{8}(y_i-b_0-b_EE_i-b_XX_i)^2\), measured in squared dollars/hour. Squaring gives extra weight to a large discrepancy and avoids negative residuals canceling positive ones.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 7
\[ \operatorname{SSE}(\mathbf b)=(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\mathbf b)^\mathsf T(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\mathbf b). \]

Here \(\mathbf b\) is a proposed coefficient vector and \(\operatorname{SSE}\) is sum of squared errors or residual sum of squares, not a standard error. Expand the compact expression: it is the dot product of the residual vector with itself. This equivalence is a mathematical identity, not a modeling assumption. OLS is an optimization rule; even if error normality is false, the rule still defines an estimate whenever a unique minimizer exists.

OLS asks for the best-fitting coefficients among the proposed columns under this squared-error criterion, not whether the data-generating process is truly linear. There may be influential outliers, dependence between rows, heteroskedastic errors or omitted variables. Those issues affect the usefulness of the fit and especially the uncertainty calculations, but none changes the definition of the algebraic objective. Distinguish therefore existence of an estimate from validity of a confidence interval.

8. Derive the normal equations instead of memorizing an inverse

Take derivatives of the SSE with respect to the three coefficients. Differentiating one squared residual with respect to the intercept produces \(-2e_i\); differentiation with respect to a slope produces \(-2E_ie_i\) or \(-2X_ie_i\). Setting all three derivatives to zero gives \(\sum e_i=0\), \(\sum E_ie_i=0\), and \(\sum X_ie_i=0\). In plain language, at the OLS solution the residuals are orthogonal to the intercept column and to each included predictor column.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 8
\[ \mathbf X^\mathsf T(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta})=\mathbf 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf y. \]

These are the normal equations. Check shapes: \(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\) is \(3\times8\), so \(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X\) is \(3\times3\) and \(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf y\) is \(3\times1\). Thus three equations determine three unknown coefficients if the columns carry sufficient independent information. Writing \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf y\) is permissible only if \(\mathbf X\) has full column rank. We will inspect that condition, rather than assuming any matrix is invertible.

Why is the stationary point a minimum? SSE is a sum of squares and hence a convex quadratic function. If the design has full column rank, its curvature matrix \(2\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X\) is positive definite; there is exactly one global minimizer. This argument supplies the missing bridge between differentiating the objective and declaring that the answer is the least-squares solution.

9. Centering as an arithmetic and interpretive tool

Our predictor averages are \(\bar E=14\) years and \(\bar X=4\) years; mean wage is \(\bar Y=44\) dollars/hour. Define centered columns \(u_i=E_i-14\) and \(v_i=X_i-4\). The values of \(u\) are four -2's followed by four +2's; \(v\) cycles through pairs of -2 and +2. Their sums are zero. Their cross product sums to zero as well: \(\sum u_iv_i=0\). This is sample orthogonality of the two centered columns.

The centered design \(\mathbf Z=[\mathbf1,\mathbf u,\mathbf v]\) yields unusually transparent products:

KEY EQUATION · STEP 9
\[ \mathbf Z^\mathsf T\mathbf Z=\begin{bmatrix}8&0&0\\0&32&0\\0&0&32\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf Z^\mathsf T\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}352\\64\\48\end{bmatrix}. \]

To verify the first diagonal term, count eight ones. For the second, add eight squared values \((-2)^2=4\), giving \(8(4)=32\). The third follows identically. The off-diagonal intercept products vanish because centered columns sum to zero; the other off-diagonal vanishes by factorial balance. The right-hand values are \(\sum Y=352\), \(\sum u_iY_i=64\), and \(\sum v_iY_i=48\). One can reproduce 64, for example, by subtracting twice the sum of the first four wages from twice the sum of the last four wages: \(-2(160)+2(192)=64\).

Centering changes the intercept's meaning, not the fitted predictions or either slope. Under centered inputs the intercept is the mean wage at average schooling and experience, a point represented within our data domain. In raw inputs, the intercept describes schooling and experience both equal to zero, which is not represented in this table. This difference is crucial when deciding whether an intercept has any sensible substantive interpretation.

10. Solve the numerical system completely

Because the centered matrix is diagonal, its three normal equations can be read directly: \(8a=352\), \(32b_E=64\), and \(32b_X=48\). Division gives \(a=44\), \(b_E=2\), and \(b_X=1.5\). Thus the fitted equation in centered coordinates is \(\widehat Y=44+2(E-14)+1.5(X-4)\). Expand brackets: \(44+2E-28+1.5X-6=10+2E+1.5X\). We have recovered the uncentered intercept \(b_0=10\), with slopes unchanged.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 10
\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat Y&=44+2(E-14)+1.5(X-4)\\ &=10+2E+1.5X. \end{aligned} \]

Check person 1: \(\widehat Y=10+2(12)+1.5(2)=37\), whereas observed \(Y=36\), so the residual is -1 dollar/hour. Check person 8: \(10+2(16)+1.5(6)=51\), observed 52, residual +1. In every repeated predictor cell the two outcomes are located one unit below and above the fitted conditional mean. We therefore recover the eight residuals \((-1,+1,-1,+1,-1,+1,-1,+1)\).

Verification against the uncentered normal equations is an independent algebraic check. Direct products give

KEY EQUATION · STEP 10
\[ \mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X=\begin{bmatrix}8&112&32\\112&1600&448\\32&448&160\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}352\\4992\\1456\end{bmatrix}. \]

Multiply the matrix by \((10,2,1.5)^\mathsf T\): the first row yields \(80+224+48=352\); the second yields \(1120+3200+672=4992\); the third yields \(320+896+240=1456\). All three normal equations hold. That is stronger evidence than merely seeing a familiar coefficient in software.

11. Identifiability, rank, and singular designs

A coefficient is identifiable from the design when the available predictor columns provide enough independent information to distinguish it from the other coefficients. For three columns, full column rank means no nonzero combination of those columns equals the all-zero vector. Our centered matrix has nonzero diagonal cross products \(8,32,32\), so its columns are linearly independent and \(\mathbf Z^\mathsf T\mathbf Z\) is invertible.

Now imagine every person's experience always equaled schooling minus ten years. Then the experience column would be a linear combination of the education column and the intercept: \(X_i=E_i-10\). The model \(\beta_0+\beta_EE_i+\beta_XX_i\) becomes \((\beta_0-10\beta_X)+(\beta_E+\beta_X)E_i\). Many distinct coefficient triples produce the same predictions; there is no unique separate schooling and experience slope. The matrix loses rank, and blindly writing an inverse is mathematically wrong.

This is perfect multicollinearity, not merely strong correlation. Near collinearity, unique coefficients may exist but be numerically unstable and have large standard errors; Guide 54 develops the diagnostics. Another failure occurs when one includes the intercept and indicator columns for every category of a categorical predictor, creating a duplicate relation. A sensible remedy is to remove redundant columns or choose a reference category. The key lesson is not that predictors must be uncorrelated; rather, no column can be an exact linear combination of the others if we want unique OLS coefficients.

12. Predictions and residual geometry

Using the fitted equation, the four distinct predictor configurations have means: \((E,X)=(12,2)\to37\), \((12,6)\to43\), \((16,2)\to45\), and \((16,6)\to51\) dollars/hour. Each appears twice in the table. These numbers trace a plane in a three-dimensional space of education, experience, and fitted wage. Each observed wage is one unit above or below that plane.

The fitted vector is \(\widehat{\mathbf y}=\mathbf X\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\); the residual vector \(\mathbf e=\mathbf y-\widehat{\mathbf y}\). The normal equations imply \(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf e=\mathbf0\): residuals have zero dot product with the intercept, schooling, and experience columns. For this teaching table, every pair of repeated predictor rows has residuals -1 and +1. Pairwise cancellation makes all three orthogonality identities immediate.

The geometric idea is an orthogonal projection: OLS chooses a point within the space of possible fitted vectors and leaves a residual perpendicular to that space. This is not the same as a three-dimensional drawing being a literal perpendicular distance from a dot to the plane: OLS minimizes squared vertical outcome errors with the predictors treated as fixed. By keeping this distinction clear we avoid importing an inappropriate orthogonal-distance fitting method.

13. Partition variability into explained and unexplained pieces

The residual sum of squares is \(\operatorname{SSE}=8(1^2)=8\) in squared dollars/hour. The total sum of squares about mean wage 44 is \(\operatorname{SST}=\sum(Y_i-44)^2\). Using the data yields squared deviations \(64,36,4,0,0,4,36,64\), summing to 208. The regression sum of squares is \(\operatorname{SSR}=\sum(\widehat Y_i-44)^2=200\). You can compute it without eight substitutions using centered orthogonality: \(2^2(32)+1.5^2(32)=128+72=200\).

KEY EQUATION · STEP 13
\[ \underbrace{208}_{\mathrm{SST}}=\underbrace{200}_{\mathrm{SSR}}+\underbrace{8}_{\mathrm{SSE}},\qquad R^2=1-\frac8{208}=0.961538. \]

The identity \(\mathrm{SST}=\mathrm{SSR}+\mathrm{SSE}\) holds for OLS with an intercept on the same sample: cross terms disappear because fitted deviations from the mean are orthogonal to residuals. It means this fitted model accounts for about 96.15% of observed sample variation about its mean, not that education and experience causally determine 96.15% of wages. In real settings a high in-sample \(R^2\) can coexist with omitted-variable bias or poor transport to other populations.

Adding more columns to an OLS model with the same observations cannot increase training SSE: the larger model can always reproduce the smaller one by assigning new slopes zero. Consequently ordinary \(R^2\) cannot decrease when adding predictors. That is an algebraic property, not proof that the new predictor is useful. We need a penalty for model complexity and, for predictive work, evaluation on held-out observations.

14. Degrees of freedom, MSE, and residual scale

Three fitted coefficients use three independent directions of the response in an identifiable eight-row design. The residual degrees of freedom are therefore \(n-p=8-3=5\). If classical regression assumptions hold, \(\mathrm{SSE}/\sigma^2\) has a chi-square distribution with five degrees of freedom under normal errors. Without normality, the degrees-of-freedom adjustment remains part of the usual residual variance estimate under homoskedastic independent zero-mean errors.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 14
\[ \operatorname{MSE}=\frac{\operatorname{SSE}}{n-p}=\frac85=1.6\ (\$/\mathrm{hour})^2, \qquad s=\sqrt{\operatorname{MSE}}=1.26491\ \$/\mathrm{hour}. \]

Here MSE means mean squared error within the fitted training sample, not necessarily forecast error on new workers. Its square root \(s\) is a residual standard deviation estimate in the same units as the response. Do not confuse this \(s\) with the standard error of a coefficient: the latter also depends on spread and relationships among predictors. These are distinct uncertainties with different interpretations.

The design residuals were deliberately set to ±1. An unusual feature of the table is therefore that the raw RMS residual \(\sqrt{8/8}=1\) differs from the unbiased residual standard deviation \(\sqrt{8/5}=1.26491\). The latter is larger because the fitted model used information to estimate three coefficients. Reporting \(\sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/n}\) as the classical residual standard error would omit the degrees-of-freedom correction and distort later t statistics.

15. Coefficient uncertainty: where standard errors come from

For fixed design regression, the classical assumptions include errors with conditional mean zero, common variance \(\sigma^2\), and zero covariance between distinct observations. Matrix algebra then gives \(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid\mathbf X)=\sigma^2(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\). We estimate \(\sigma^2\) with MSE. The diagonal entries of the resulting covariance matrix are estimated variances of coefficients; their square roots are the standard errors (SE).

For our orthogonal centered design, \((\mathbf Z^\mathsf T\mathbf Z)^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(1/8,1/32,1/32)\). Hence the centered-intercept SE is \(\sqrt{1.6/8}=0.44721\) dollars/hour. Both slope SE values are \(\sqrt{1.6/32}=\sqrt{0.05}=0.22361\) dollars/hour per predictor year. The two slope standard errors match because the two centered columns have identical sums of squares and zero cross product; this equality is not expected generally.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 15
\[ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol\gamma})=1.6\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac18,\frac1{32},\frac1{32}\right), \quad\operatorname{SE}(b_E)=\operatorname{SE}(b_X)=0.22361. \]

An estimate can be precise in a sample with many widely spread, independent predictor values yet unstable when predictors move together. This explains why the coefficient magnitude alone is not a test statistic. It also explains the practical importance of inspecting ranges and correlations before drawing strong inferential conclusions. Note that our synthetic residuals are not independent random errors generated from a normal distribution; the formulas illustrate what we would calculate if their sampling model were justified in a real study.

16. The t test for a conditional coefficient

A researcher might test the null hypothesis \(H_0:\beta_E=0\) against the two-sided alternative \(H_1:\beta_E\ne0\). Under a classical normal-error model with fixed full-rank predictors and independent constant-variance errors, the statistic \(t=(b_E-0)/\operatorname{SE}(b_E)\) follows Student's t distribution with \(n-p\) degrees of freedom under the null. Here \(t_E=2/0.2236068=8.94427\), with five degrees of freedom. For experience, \(t_X=1.5/0.2236068=6.70820\). We compare each absolute statistic with the corresponding reference distribution, not with a universal threshold such as two regardless of sample size.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 16
\[ \begin{aligned} t_E&=\frac{2-0}{\sqrt{1.6/32}}=8.94427,\\ t_X&=\frac{1.5-0}{\sqrt{1.6/32}}=6.70820,\qquad\mathrm{df}=5. \end{aligned} \]

The education test asks whether the population conditional linear slope is zero after including experience in this model. It does not test whether education matters in every possible specification. Experience's t test similarly evaluates an adjusted coefficient, not whether actual career experience causes wages to change by exactly 1.5 dollars/hour. Rejecting a null is a statement about compatibility with the sampling model; it does not certify that the coefficient is practically important, free from confounding, or stable across sectors.

Because we deliberately selected a small clean table, both t statistics are large. That is a teaching outcome, not evidence from a random sample. In real labor research, results depend on sample selection, wage definition, industry, region, work hours, measurement error, dependence within organizations, and covariates. The assumptions behind the classical reference distribution must be evaluated independently of whether the printed p-value is small.

17. A confidence interval for a partial slope

A two-sided 95% classical confidence interval for the schooling coefficient is \(b_E\pm t_{0.975,5}\operatorname{SE}(b_E)\). The critical value for t with five degrees of freedom is about 2.57058. The margin is \(2.57058(0.2236068)=0.57480\) dollars/hour per school-year, giving the interval \([1.42520,2.57480]\). Experience has the same margin, so its interval is \([0.92520,2.07480]\) dollars/hour per experience-year.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 17
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\beta_E)&=2\pm2.57058(0.2236068)\\ &=2\pm0.57480\\ &=[1.42520,\;2.57480]. \end{aligned} \]

The confidence procedure has approximately 95% coverage under the stated normal linear-model assumptions; after the data are observed, the fixed true parameter is either inside or outside the interval. Avoid saying there is a 95% probability that a fixed classical parameter moves within the computed bounds. In ordinary exposition, “a 95% confidence interval for the adjusted education slope” is concise and correct. Always include units and the model used for adjustment.

Why does holding experience fixed appear in the interval interpretation? Because the parameter being estimated is the education column's population coefficient in the multiple regression model. No special manipulation of the interval creates this conditioning; the conditioning is encoded in which columns were included during estimation. Likewise, adding omitted confounders may alter the target coefficient and its uncertainty. A confidence interval only quantifies a certain kind of sampling error around a specified estimand; it does not absorb every possible systematic error.

18. Prediction versus inference about the conditional mean

At \(E=14\), \(X=4\), our fitted mean is 44 dollars/hour. This does not say every worker with those predictor values earns 44. A confidence interval for the conditional mean quantifies uncertainty in the regression plane at a given input vector \(\mathbf x_0=(1,14,4)^\mathsf T\). A prediction interval for one new wage additionally accounts for the individual's unexplained error. With classical assumptions the variance estimates are \(\operatorname{MSE}\mathbf x_0^\mathsf T(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf x_0\) for the mean and \(\operatorname{MSE}[1+\mathbf x_0^\mathsf T(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf x_0]\) for a new observation.

At the centered point \((u,v)=(0,0)\), the leverage factor is \(1/8\). Therefore the mean SE is \(\sqrt{1.6/8}=0.44721\), and the individual prediction SE is \(\sqrt{1.6(1+1/8)}=\sqrt{1.8}=1.34164\). Applying the same critical t value gives a 95% mean interval about \([42.8504,45.1496]\) and an individual prediction interval about \([40.5512,47.4488]\), both in dollars/hour. The second must be wider because individual outcomes vary around the population conditional mean.

These calculations concern an idealized independent new observation drawn under the same error model. They are not reliable forecasts of actual wages without external validation. Also observe the distinction between an interval for a single coefficient and an interval for a mean wage: the former is in dollars/hour per year, the latter in dollars/hour. The predictor profile determines how precisely the mean is estimated; extrapolating far beyond observed schooling or experience values can sharply increase this uncertainty and invalidate linearity assumptions.

19. The overall F test and the model's ANOVA table

The overall null for a model with two slopes is \(H_0:\beta_E=\beta_X=0\). Against it, the unrestricted alternative says at least one slope differs from zero. The reduced model under the null has only an intercept and predicts the sample mean for everyone. Its SSE is SST = 208. The full model SSE is 8. This yields a regression ANOVA table with 2 regression degrees of freedom, 5 residual degrees of freedom, and 7 total degrees of freedom.

\[ F_{\mathrm{overall}}=\frac{\mathrm{SSR}/2}{\mathrm{SSE}/5}=\frac{200/2}{8/5}=\frac{100}{1.6}=62.5. \]

Under the classical null, the statistic follows an F distribution with numerator df 2 and denominator df 5. The test checks whether the two predictors collectively improve fit over an intercept-only model in a way too large to attribute to the specified random-error model. It does not imply both slopes are nonzero; one could drive rejection. It also does not measure predictive calibration, ensure linearity, or produce a causal effect. Because the sample is engineered, the F value is chiefly a reproducible arithmetic lesson.

The overall F and individual t tests answer different questions. For one predictor in addition to the intercept, the t and corresponding one-degree-of-freedom F statistics satisfy \(F=t^2\). In a two-slope overall F test, the numerator tests two restrictions jointly, so the statistic is not equal to either individual slope's t squared. We next isolate a single restriction to see the identity arise correctly.

20. The partial F test: compare nested models

Suppose the scientific question is whether experience adds an adjusted linear association after education is already included. The full model has intercept, education and experience. The reduced model omits experience but retains intercept and education. With our orthogonal design, removing experience leaves its entire fitted contribution \(1.5v_i\) in the reduced residual. The eight residuals of the full fit are orthogonal to \(v\), so the reduced SSE is \(8+1.5^2(32)=8+72=80\). This is also directly checkable: the reduced fit is \(44+2(E-14)\), predicting 40 for everyone at E=12 and 48 for E=16; squared observed discrepancies total 80.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 20
\[ F_{\mathrm{partial}}=\frac{(\mathrm{SSE}_{R}-\mathrm{SSE}_{F})/(p_F-p_R)}{\mathrm{SSE}_{F}/(n-p_F)}=\frac{(80-8)/1}{8/5}=45. \]

The numerator df is one because exactly one parameter is removed. Denominator df is five from the full model. Compare this statistic with \(F_{1,5}\), not \(F_{2,5}\). And check the identity: the experience t statistic was \(6.70820\); its square is 45. To check education adjusted for experience, omit education instead. Its removed contribution is \(2u_i\), adding \(2^2(32)=128\) to SSE, so reduced SSE is 136 and partial F is \((136-8)/1.6=80=8.94427^2\).

A nested reduced model is one obtained by imposing restrictions on the full model; it must be fitted on the same observations and outcome. Comparing SSE values from different samples invalidates this calculation. The partial F provides an especially useful framework when testing a group of categorical indicator coefficients or other several-parameter restrictions jointly. This guide introduces the principle without moving into the interaction models reserved for Guide 53.

21. Adjusted R² and a carefully limited comparison

Ordinary \(R^2=1-8/208=0.96154\). Adjusted R² modifies the unexplained-variation fraction to account for the number of estimated coefficients: \(R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=1-(\mathrm{SSE}/(n-p))/(\mathrm{SST}/(n-1))\). Substitute \(n=8\), \(p=3\), SSE=8 and SST=208: \(1-(8/5)/(208/7)=1-11.2/208=0.94615\).

KEY EQUATION · STEP 21
\[ \begin{aligned} R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}&=1-\frac{8/5}{208/7}\\ &=1-\frac{11.2}{208}\\ &=0.946154. \end{aligned} \]

The adjusted measure penalizes using degrees of freedom to add predictors, and unlike unadjusted R² it can decrease after adding a column. It is not a universally unbiased measure of future prediction quality, does not select a causal model, and should be compared only for models fitted to the same response observations with compatible intercept conventions. High adjusted R² does not imply that the model's distributional assumptions hold. For predictive goals, cross-validation and a held-out test set are conceptually distinct safeguards.

Notice adjusted R² is numerically less than ordinary R² here. That is expected with more than just an intercept and nonperfect fits. Neither number is a percentage of wage caused by schooling and experience. We can state that the model accounts for about 96.2% of the observed variability and that the degrees-of-freedom-adjusted fit measure is approximately 0.946. Keep the language anchored to model fit, not causal attribution.

22. Categorical predictors, indicators and a reference level

Not all explanatory predictors are numbers with a meaningful one-unit increment. Suppose occupation is classified as analyst, technician, or coordinator. Assigning codes 1, 2 and 3 and treating the change from 1 to 2 like the change from 2 to 3 imposes an arbitrary numerical scale. Instead create two indicator variables, such as \(D_T=1\) for technician (else 0) and \(D_C=1\) for coordinator (else 0), with analyst as the reference category when both indicators equal zero.

A model \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_EE+\beta_XX+\gamma_TD_T+\gamma_CD_C+\varepsilon\) compares each nonreference occupation with analysts at the same modeled education and experience, assuming additive parallel differences. A technician's fitted wage is \(\beta_0+\beta_EE+\beta_XX+\gamma_T\); a coordinator's is the analogous expression with \(\gamma_C\). The technician-minus-coordinator fitted difference is \(\gamma_T-\gamma_C\), not either coefficient separately.

With an intercept, adding all three occupation indicators creates the dummy-variable trap: \(D_A+D_T+D_C=1\) exactly, which duplicates the intercept column. The normal equations no longer identify a unique set of occupation coefficients. Changing the reference category changes the individual indicator coefficients and their labels but leaves all fitted values unchanged when the new coding spans the same model space. A categorical predictor with three levels generally contributes two degrees of freedom to an omnibus nested-model test, not one. Real data must contain sufficient categories and overlap to estimate meaningful adjusted comparisons.

23. A second dataset that reveals confounding of a marginal slope

Our balanced eight-row table was constructed so that education and experience are orthogonal. Real covariates often move together, so the marginal schooling slope can differ from its adjusted slope. To see the distinction without hidden data, consider a separate six-row synthetic algebra example, with E coded in years above a common baseline, X years of experience, and Y an illustrative hourly wage in dollars/hour.

Person E (years above baseline) X experience years Wage Y ($/hour)
A 0 0 10
B 0 1 13
C 1 1 15
D 1 2 18
E 2 2 20
F 2 3 23

The table follows \(Y=10+2E+3X\) exactly, which makes coefficients transparent but provides no residual variance for standard-error inference. Mean education is 1, mean experience 1.5, and mean wage 16.5. Simple regression of Y on E uses \(\sum(E_i-1)^2=4\) and \(\sum(E_i-1)(Y_i-16.5)=20\), giving marginal slope \(20/4=5\). Multiple regression on E and X yields the conditional education slope 2 and the conditional experience slope 3. Why the change? The level of experience increases on average by one for each increase of one in E. The marginal slope therefore includes the experience contribution: \(2+3(1)=5\).

Here the difference between 5 and 2 is omitted-variable association in an engineered example. It illustrates the algebra of confounding-like adjustment but cannot prove real labor-market causal confounding. If experience were caused by schooling, or if selection or measurement error were operating, causal language would require careful directed reasoning. We do not assume that statistical “control” automatically identifies an intervention effect. Keeping this second table separate from our eight-person inferential case prevents inappropriate mixing of residual degrees of freedom or p-values.

24. The omitted-variable-bias identity, derived accessibly

In a population with suitable finite moments, suppose the true conditional mean is linear in education and experience, with coefficients \(\beta_E\) and \(\beta_X\). Regress experience linearly on education in the population and call that slope \(\delta=\operatorname{Cov}(E,X)/\operatorname{Var}(E)\), assuming \(\operatorname{Var}(E)>0\). If the errors have zero covariance with education, then the population simple-regression slope from omitting experience is

KEY EQUATION · STEP 24
\[ \widetilde\beta_E=\beta_E+\beta_X\delta. \]

The derivation is one line of covariance algebra: \(\operatorname{Cov}(E,Y)=\beta_E\operatorname{Var}(E)+\beta_X\operatorname{Cov}(E,X)+\operatorname{Cov}(E,\varepsilon)\). Set the last term to zero and divide by \(\operatorname{Var}(E)\). In the constructed six-row table, the finite-sample identity also holds with OLS arithmetic: conditional slope 2, experience slope 3, and slope of X on E equal to 1, hence \(2+3=5\). This exact equality is not an approximation for that fitted additive construction.

The identity identifies three ingredients needed for a changed coefficient: (1) the omitted predictor has an association with the response conditional on included predictors, (2) the omitted predictor is associated with the included predictor, and (3) the relevant model relations are interpreted at the appropriate linear-projection level. If either coefficient \(\beta_X\) or association \(\delta\) is zero, this particular omitted-variable term disappears. But a coefficient remaining unchanged after adjustment does not automatically prove no confounding; nonlinear effects, measurement error, poor overlap or offsetting biases can occur.

In scientific reporting say “the schooling coefficient fell from 5 in an unadjusted model to 2 in a model including experience.” Do not state “experience explains exactly 60% of education's causal effect.” The arithmetic is a difference between model associations, not a decomposition of independently identified causal mechanisms.

25. When conditioning can make an interpretation worse

Adding a variable is not always a beneficial causal adjustment. A confounder is a common cause of exposure and outcome under an appropriate causal model; controlling for measured confounders can sometimes help isolate an effect. A mediator lies on a causal pathway from exposure to outcome; conditioning on it changes a total-effect question into something closer to a direct-effect question, under additional assumptions. A collider receives causal arrows from two variables; conditioning on it can introduce a noncausal association that was absent before selection.

For example, suppose both education and unmeasured motivation influence admission to a selective employer. An analysis restricted to hires already conditions on admission, which can be a collider. Even with perfectly measured education, the within-hire comparison might reflect selection-induced relations between education and motivation. It would be careless to say that adding more controls or analyzing a more homogeneous company always removes bias. The order and role of variables matter, not merely their correlations.

There is also a support problem. If every highly educated employee has long experience and every less educated employee has short experience, comparing education levels at the same experience may require extrapolation rather than genuine observations. The model can print a conditional coefficient despite minimal overlap. Report where actual predictor combinations exist, check leverage and scatterplots, and acknowledge when conditional comparisons are extrapolations. This is part of responsible interpretation, even when the software converges and the normal equations are solvable.

26. Classical assumptions: organize them by what they justify

The linear mean relation states \(\operatorname E(Y\mid\mathbf X)=\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\); it defines the target conditional expectation. Full column rank ensures unique OLS coefficients. A random sample or credible independence structure supports standard sampling arguments. Conditional zero-mean errors support the usual unbiasedness calculations for coefficients under the specified design. Equal error variance and no cross-observation covariance justify the simple covariance formula \(\sigma^2(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\). Normal errors permit exact finite-sample t and F distributions; sufficiently strong regularity conditions can instead support large-sample approximations, but n=8 is not a large sample.

A residual plot of errors against fitted values or each predictor can reveal curvature, changing scatter or unusual observations. A Q–Q plot compares residual quantiles with those expected under a specified reference distribution, but eight residuals provide very limited evidence either for or against normality. A leverage diagnostic concerns unusual predictor configurations; an influential observation combines its predictor position with a consequential outcome. Residual checks cannot prove unobserved confounders absent. Nor can they distinguish a mediator from a genuine pre-exposure confounder.

If errors have nonconstant variance, ordinary least-squares coefficient estimates may still target a valid linear projection under suitable mean assumptions, but their classical standard errors need correction. Heteroskedasticity-consistent (robust) covariance estimation is one option, often requiring caution with tiny samples. If people are sampled within the same companies, employee outcomes may be correlated within company; a different dependence-aware design or cluster-adjusted analysis is needed. This guide explains the assumptions but does not certify inferential validity for actual data we do not have.

27. Matrix inversion in practice: why good software avoids the naive route

The closed-form \((\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf y\) is valuable for understanding what the coefficients solve. But explicitly computing a matrix inverse is usually not the recommended numerical approach for large or poorly conditioned datasets. Practical implementations use QR factorization or singular-value decomposition to solve least squares with better numerical stability. This matters because \(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X\) can amplify conditioning problems already present in the columns.

In our centered table, the matrix is diagonal, so division is both transparent and stable. For a less structured design, one can still derive and check \(\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf X\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\mathbf X^\mathsf T\mathbf y\), but calculating a full inverse by hand is not necessary to grasp conditional interpretation. A numeric solver should return residuals orthogonal to the design up to floating-point tolerance. If it silently drops a perfectly redundant column, one must inspect which coefficient definition remains estimable rather than attributing independent scientific meaning to arbitrary output.

A responsible analysis reports units, categories, sample restrictions, variable construction, and uncertainty alongside coefficient tables. It distinguishes numerical convergence, estimability, sampling validity and causal identification. These four conditions answer different questions; they cannot replace one another. Code can rapidly reproduce an answer, but the student must still explain why a one-unit conditional change has the stated units and why a confidence interval needs assumptions beyond matrix multiplication.

28. A visual map of adjusted versus marginal relationships

Think of the fitted wage plane as rising by 2 dollars/hour for each additional year of education in the E direction, and by 1.5 dollars/hour for each additional year of experience in the X direction. In a fixed-experience slice, the schooling slope is 2. In a fixed-education slice, the experience slope is 1.5. These are parallel slices because the model is additive. Whether a real wage surface is actually planar is a scientific question to investigate, not a theorem proved by OLS.

A further way to think about the distinction is to sketch two comparisons side by side. First compare people with different schooling while ignoring the distribution of experience. That is a marginal comparison, and it may include experience differences because those differences happen to occur alongside schooling. Next draw lines at a fixed experience level and compare schooling along those lines. This is a conditional comparison. A multiple regression coefficient describes the slope of the second kind of fitted comparison, provided the additive linear model is an adequate representation of the conditional mean. The word “conditional” therefore specifies the comparison, not a successful removal of every unwanted influence. If the data contain few workers at the chosen fixed experience level, the comparison may rely on extrapolation even though its algebraic coefficient is well defined. This is a reason to visualize data coverage before interpreting a coefficient: a mathematically identified projection can answer a scientifically weak question. In a serious study, plotting the relationship between schooling and experience and identifying sparse predictor combinations belongs alongside coefficient interpretation rather than being left as an optional cosmetic step.

The comparison between the eight-row balanced example and six-row confounded example should be read as two controlled conceptual experiments. In the balanced example, the centered predictors have zero sample covariance, so simple and multiple slopes coincide. In the confounded example, experience rises with education, so simple schooling regression picks up more than the schooling term. The conditioning operation changes which contrast the coefficient describes; it does not subtract unwanted influences through a magical universal filter.

Use a design matrix diagram as an audit: each row represents one individual, each column a predictor meaning, and the output vector a single measured response. Students can reconstruct every wage from the fitted equation and reproduce every coefficient from cross products. A diagram helps memory only when each element corresponds to real algebra and units. The tables and figures provided here serve precisely that purpose; their numbers are not decorative illustrations unrelated to the worked arithmetic.

39. Exercise 11 — the adjusted R² trap (fully worked)

Task. A colleague says adding any predictor improves a regression because training R² cannot fall. Explain the mathematical truth and the conclusion that does not follow. Hint: compare the spaces of possible fitted values before and after including a column.

Solution. When a predictor is added, the original smaller model is still available by setting its new coefficient to zero. The minimum SSE over the larger model is therefore at most the minimum SSE over the smaller model. Since SST is fixed for the same response sample and an intercept is used, \(R^2=1-\mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}\) cannot decrease. However, the bigger model estimated another coefficient using the same observations, making spurious in-sample improvement possible. Adjusted R² replaces SSE by SSE/(n−p), penalizing complexity, and may decrease. In our full example it is \(1-(8/5)/(208/7)=0.946154\). Interpretation: a marginal improvement in R² establishes no out-of-sample predictive gain or causal relevance. External validation, purpose-driven variables, residual diagnostics, and scientific knowledge remain necessary.

41. A guided reporting template, not a causal shortcut

A useful regression report should begin with population, sampling method, sample size, outcome definition and unit, predictors and their measurement windows. Then present the fitted model and the intended contrast. In this teaching case: “In eight synthetic illustrative observations, the fitted education coefficient is +2 dollars/hour per year, conditional on experience in an additive linear model.” If this were a legitimate sample under classical assumptions, one could add the 95% interval \([1.425,2.575]\) and describe the model-based inference conditions explicitly.

A responsible report should also include residual df, residual scale, adjusted R², relevant partial F comparisons, measurement missingness and any researcher decisions about included variables. If occupation indicators are used, state their reference group. Identify meaningful unsupported predictor combinations and likely dependencies between observations. Do not use the word effect as a causal claim unless the study design and identification assumptions justify it. A fit that describes conditional association is still useful when reported accurately.

For reproducibility, retain a complete small teaching table in the guide and a transparent sequence of cross-products, estimated coefficients, residuals and test formulas. For large studies, preserve code, data provenance, software versions and a documented variable dictionary. This guide intentionally does not include an unverified simulation or suggest that the eight rows came from a labor survey. The learning product is the ability to reproduce and question a conditional regression—not to believe that a coefficient proves what it was never designed to prove.

Why is the slope called conditional? How does the denominator df become five? Why do a coefficient SE and residual standard error differ? When does a one-parameter partial F equal t squared? Why can adjusted R² decrease? Why is a 95% model interval not evidence of a causal wage effect?

A complete answer should reconstruct one numeric path: from the eight-row table, center both predictors, compute the diagonal cross-product matrix, solve for 44, 2 and 1.5, verify predictions and normal equations, obtain SSE=8, compute MSE=1.6, derive a slope SE of 0.22361, and interpret a conditional coefficient with proper units. Another complete answer should explain how the second table changes the schooling slope from 5 unadjusted to 2 adjusted without claiming causal identification. If any step cannot be explained aloud, revisit that exact bridge rather than memorizing another formula.

The deepest connection is that a multiple regression coefficient is the answer to a specified comparison within a specified model and design. Matrix algebra tells us whether that answer can be estimated. Statistical assumptions tell us when its reported uncertainty is credible. Domain knowledge and research design determine whether an association has a causal interpretation. These are complementary forms of reasoning, not interchangeable guarantees. Guide 53 will build interactions and nonlinear terms that allow conditional slopes to vary; Guide 54 will examine multicollinearity in greater depth.
