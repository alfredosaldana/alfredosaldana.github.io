1. The scientific question comes before the multiplication

A regression coefficient is sometimes described as the effect of X on Y. That phrasing can be useful when the relationship is a straight line and its slope is held constant, but it becomes misleading as soon as the slope varies across settings. Consider electricity consumption and outdoor temperature. A poorly insulated building may respond more strongly to a temperature change than a highly insulated one. If we impose one common slope, we erase the very difference an engineer hopes to study. An interaction permits the temperature–consumption association to differ by building type. A nonlinear term permits the slope to vary along the temperature scale itself.

Our central question is therefore not merely whether temperature predicts electricity demand. It is: How much does predicted consumption change when temperature rises by one degree, for a specified building type and within a specified range? We will calculate that conditional change from actual rows of an explicitly synthetic teaching dataset. The numbers are created to reveal algebra clearly; they are not observations from a utility company, a causal experiment, or an energy-efficiency evaluation. Treating them as empirical evidence would be an error.

A useful mental picture is a collection of fitted lines. A common-slope model gives parallel lines. An interaction model permits those lines to fan apart, move together, or cross. A quadratic model bends a single line into a curve. The terms are not competing decorations: each makes a different assertion about how the conditional mean changes. Before adding either, state the physical question, the unit of response, the predictors held fixed, and the range where predictions are intended.

The reader should know simple and multiple regression from Guides 51 and 52, including fitted values, coefficients and residuals. We will introduce the one extra mathematical tool required for curvature—the derivative—and also verify its meaning with finite differences, so prior multivariable calculus is not assumed. Our goal is to use an equation to answer a meaningful question, not merely to read software output.

2. Variables, units, and the meaning of a conditional mean

Let Y be energy consumed during one hour, measured in kWh. The outcome is an amount of energy, not an instantaneous power reading in kW; every fitted value refers to the same one-hour interval. Let T be outdoor temperature in degrees Celsius and G be a building indicator: G=0 for type A and G=1 for type B. Type labels do not certify any insulation quality; they simply identify two hypothetical building classes. Define t=T−20, the number of Celsius degrees above or below the reference of 20 °C.

A regression function is a statement about E[Y|T,G], the expected Y at specified predictor values, rather than a promise that every observation equals a deterministic formula. Individual hours also differ because of occupancy, equipment, behavior, and random variation. We write Y_i=μ_i+ε_i, where μ_i is the conditional mean and ε_i the unexplained deviation. The subscript i labels an observation; the vertical bar in E[Y|T,G] means conditional on, not division.

Centering by 20 does not change Celsius into a new physical unit. Both T and t vary in degrees Celsius; the number zero for t means 20 °C rather than an absence of temperature. Likewise, multiplying t by G produces an interaction column with temperature units when G is dimensionless. A coefficient multiplying t thus has units kWh per °C, while a coefficient multiplying tG has units kWh per °C as well. Dimensional consistency makes apparently abstract regression equations easier to audit.

We will work mostly with the fitted conditional mean, denoted μ̂(t,g), and distinguish it from a recorded Y. Later, when we discuss uncertainty, we will identify the extra assumptions required for standard errors. If our available temperatures are only 17, 19, 21 and 23 °C, a prediction at 40 °C is extrapolation even when a line looks tidy.

To answer that question, we must use the cross-products of temperature and consumption within each group.

4. Start with the additive model and identify its restriction

The simpler multiple-regression specification is E[Y|t,G]=β₀+β₁t+β₂G. For group A, substituting G=0 gives μ_A(t)=β₀+β₁t. For group B, G=1 gives μ_B(t)=(β₀+β₂)+β₁t. The intercepts can differ by β₂ kWh, but both slopes are β₁ kWh/°C. Consequently the predicted difference μ_B(t)−μ_A(t)=β₂ is constant at every temperature. This parallel-lines restriction is a scientific assumption, not a property of ordinary least squares.

At a glance, an additive model may seem flexible because it already contains two predictors. Yet the number of coefficients alone tells us little about the permitted shape. Two differently labeled building classes need not respond identically to temperature; if the temperature association changes across groups, the additive model will systematically overpredict some group–temperature combinations and underpredict others. The corrective step is to formulate a specific departure rather than add arbitrary polynomial degrees.

The additive model also clarifies what should be compared. If the question concerns the group gap at 20 °C, β₂ can answer it. If the question concerns whether the group gap grows by 2 kWh whenever temperature rises one degree, β₂ cannot answer it because the model forces the gap to remain fixed. Model selection should reflect the research question and available data, not only which specification produces the smallest in-sample error.

A line plotted with G=0 and G=1 therefore provides a diagnostic sketch: parallelism is the mathematical consequence of omitting tG. When we introduce tG, we should be able to state precisely which equality is being relaxed and verify the new slopes using the table rather than simply saying that an interaction is 'significant.'

5. Derive the continuous-by-category interaction model

Add the product of centered temperature and building indicator. The model has four coefficients: β₀, β₁, β₂ and β₃. The last coefficient captures how the temperature slope differs for type B relative to type A. Do not interpret β₃ as a constant difference in consumption; its unit is kWh/°C, not kWh.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 5
\[ \boxed{\displaystyle E[Y\mid t,G]=\beta_0+\beta_1 t+\beta_2 G+\beta_3(tG)} \]

Substitute G=0 to obtain μ_A(t)=β₀+β₁t. Substitute G=1 to obtain μ_B(t)=(β₀+β₂)+(β₁+β₃)t. Therefore the A slope is β₁, the B slope is β₁+β₃, and their difference is β₃. The intercept gap at t=0 is β₂. For an arbitrary temperature, the group contrast is not β₂ alone; it is β₂+β₃t. The product term makes this gap dependent on temperature.

This decomposition answers two symmetrical questions. Holding building type fixed, a one-degree temperature increase changes the fitted mean by β₁+β₃G. Holding temperature fixed, switching from category A to B changes the fitted mean by β₂+β₃t. Neither comparison has a causal meaning solely because it can be calculated. Buildings may differ in many unrecorded ways. Regression estimates associations conditional on the included predictors; causal interpretation would demand a defensible assignment mechanism and assumptions about confounding.

The symbols describe conditional mean differences under the model. If actual individual-hour changes are the goal, uncertainty and time dependence matter. A sharp-looking pair of straight lines can still miss demand peaks, weekends, repeated-building correlations, or nonlinear heating behavior. Those issues do not invalidate the algebra; they limit what inferences the algebra supports.

6. Fit the two slopes directly from the visible rows

For four centered temperature values t=(−3,−1,1,3), the sum of squares is Σt²=9+1+1+9=20. Within group A, the mean outcome is 100. Calculate the temperature–response cross-product about that mean: Σt(Y−100)=(−3)(−11)+(−1)(−5)+(1)(3)+(3)(13)=33+5+3+39=80. The least-squares A slope is 80/20=4 kWh/°C. Because mean t=0, its intercept is precisely group mean Y=100 kWh.

For group B, mean Y=130, and the cross-product is Σt(Y−130)=(−3)(−17)+(−1)(−7)+(1)(5)+(3)(19)=51+7+5+57=120. The B slope is 120/20=6 kWh/°C, and the B intercept is 130 kWh at 20 °C. These are ordinary least-squares formulas applied within each group. In an interaction regression with a binary indicator and complete separate group lines, fitting the combined four-parameter model is algebraically equivalent to fitting two separate least-squares lines.

Thus β̂₀=100, β̂₁=4, β̂₂=130−100=30, β̂₃=6−4=2. We obtained all four coefficients without a black-box regression command. A numerical verification is to substitute the results into the model and inspect residuals. Equal group sizes are convenient here but are not conceptually required for a binary-by-continuous interaction.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 6
\[ \boxed{\displaystyle \widehat\mu(t,G)=100+4t+30G+2tG} \]

Units verify the algebra: 100 and 30 are kWh; 4 and 2 are kWh/°C; G is dimensionless; the predicted total is kWh. The prediction at t=0,G=1 equals 100+30=130; at t=3,G=1 it is 100+12+30+6=148 kWh. The observed value is 149, so that row's residual is +1 kWh, not zero. Keeping fitted and observed values distinct prevents a common interpretive mistake.

7. Verify every prediction, residual and squared error

A defensible worked example does not stop after computing a coefficient vector. Reproduce all eight predictions using the fitted formula. For group A at t=−3, the prediction is 100+4(−3)=88; at t=−1 it is 96; at 1 it is 104; at 3 it is 112. For group B the intercept is 130 and slope is 6, producing 112, 124, 136 and 148. The residual sum of squares is SSE=8 kWh², because eight squared residuals equal one. Within each group, the residuals sum to 1−1−1+1=0. Their temperature-weighted sum is (−3)(1)+(−1)(−1)+(1)(−1)+(3)(1)=−3+1−1+3=0. These two equalities show that residuals are orthogonal to a constant and temperature within each group, the normal-equation conditions for the two fitted lines. They are checks, not universal guarantees for an arbitrary proposed line.

We fitted p=4 coefficients to n=8 observations, so the classical residual degrees of freedom are n−p=4. The mean squared residual estimate is MSE=SSE/4=2 kWh² and the residual standard error is s=√2≈1.414 kWh. This quantifies residual scatter around the fitted lines, but it does not by itself establish that errors are independent, homoskedastic, or normally distributed.

An independent calculation with OLS software should recover (100,4,30,2), SSE=8 and the same row predictions. If a program gives a different intercept, inspect whether it used raw T rather than centered t, or coded group B differently, before accusing the software of an error.

8. Conditional predictions for two temperatures and both types

Take T=19 °C, so t=−1. For type A, μ̂_A=100+4(−1)=96 kWh. For type B, μ̂_B=130+6(−1)=124 kWh. The predicted B−A gap is 124−96=28 kWh. Check against the contrast formula: 30+2(−1)=28. Both paths agree.

At T=23 °C, t=3. For A, μ̂_A=100+4(3)=112 kWh; for B, μ̂_B=130+6(3)=148 kWh. The gap is 36 kWh, also verified by 30+2(3)=36. Thus the gap increases from 28 to 36 over a four-degree rise: 8/4=2 kWh per °C. That is exactly the interaction coefficient. This consistency among levels, differences, and slopes is a powerful check on an interpretation.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 8
\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat\mu_B(t)-\widehat\mu_A(t)&=30+2t,\\ \widehat\mu_B(-1)-\widehat\mu_A(-1)&=28\ \mathrm{kWh},\\ \widehat\mu_B(3)-\widehat\mu_A(3)&=36\ \mathrm{kWh}. \end{aligned} \]

The predicted temperature change of four degrees increases mean demand by 4×4=16 kWh for A and 4×6=24 kWh for B. Their difference is 8 kWh. Notice the distinctions: 30 is the group contrast at 20 °C; 4 and 6 are slopes; 2 is the difference of slopes; and 28 and 36 are group contrasts at specified temperatures. These quantities answer different questions, despite appearing in the same equation.

Report results using conditional language: Within the synthetic 17–23 °C range, the fitted association is 4 kWh per °C for A and 6 kWh per °C for B. Avoid 'type B causes consumption to rise by 6 kWh per degree' because type labels are not interventions and confounding remains possible.

9. What the two-line graph explains—and what it hides

A figure of these observations and fitted lines should show temperature on the horizontal axis, hourly energy in kWh on the vertical axis, and distinct lines for A and B. The vertical gap increases from lower to higher temperatures. This is not a decorative chart: it visually verifies both the different slopes and why the group difference is temperature-dependent. Every plotted observation corresponds to a row in the table; no hidden empirical data are being implied.

Read the graph with three deliberate questions. First, at a fixed horizontal coordinate, what is the difference between the two fitted heights? That reads the conditional group contrast. Second, moving one degree to the right along a single line, how many kWh does its fitted height change? That reads the group-specific slope. Third, do the two lines converge or diverge as temperature changes? That gives the sign of the interaction coefficient, but not its sampling uncertainty.

A graph is also a check on scale. If the y-axis started at zero instead of displaying the 80–155 kWh region, the slopes could appear flatter without changing any coefficient. A visual impression is therefore not a statistical significance test. The graph establishes what the fitted numbers mean; a formal interval or test requires assumptions and a standard error.

With only four temperatures per group, a linear relationship within 17–23 °C is a simple model choice. Even if the points are near lines, heating or cooling equipment can activate abruptly outside this interval. The graph should never be extended mechanically to extreme winter or summer conditions and presented as established physical law.

10. Interaction coefficient inference from residual variance

Suppose for a moment that observations are independent and that model errors have mean zero, constant conditional variance σ², and approximately normal sampling behavior. For an exact classical t test we additionally require normal errors or the relevant distributional justification. The two slope estimators each use centered Σt²=20 in their separate groups. Their estimated slope variance is MSE/20=2/20=0.1, with standard error √0.1≈0.3162 kWh/°C.

The interaction estimate is the difference between independent group slope estimators: β̂₃=b_B−b_A=6−4=2. Its estimated variance is the sum of their variances, 0.1+0.1=0.2; its standard error is √0.2≈0.4472 kWh/°C. Test H₀:β₃=0 against H₁:β₃≠0 by t=2/0.4472≈4.4721 with 4 residual degrees of freedom. The two-sided classical p-value is about 0.0111. A 95% interval uses t_{0.975,4}≈2.776, giving 2±2.776(0.4472)=2±1.2415, or approximately [0.7585,3.2415] kWh/°C.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 10
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{SE}(\widehat\beta_3)&=\sqrt{2\left(\frac{2}{20}\right)}=\sqrt{0.2},\\ t&=\frac{2}{\sqrt{0.2}}\approx4.4721,\\ CI_{95\%}(\beta_3)&=2\pm 2.776\sqrt{0.2}. \end{aligned} \]

The interval refers to a model coefficient under assumptions; it is not a 95% probability that the fixed coefficient changes from day to day. Moreover, repeated hours from a building would commonly be correlated. The synthetic rows deliberately avoid that complication, but real energy data might need building-level clustering, temporal structure, or robust standard errors. A statistically detectable slope difference is not automatically a causal insulation advantage.

11. Centering changes coefficient meanings, not predictions

Our regression uses t=T−20. Write it instead in raw Celsius T. Replace t with T−20 throughout: μ̂=100+4(T−20)+30G+2(T−20)G. Collect like terms: μ̂=20+4T−10G+2TG. The raw-temperature intercept for A is now 20 kWh at T=0 °C, which lies outside our sample; the raw-temperature group coefficient becomes −10 kWh at 0 °C. The interaction coefficient remains +2 kWh/°C because shifting the temperature origin does not alter the difference between group slopes.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 11
\[ \begin{aligned} 100+4(T-20)+30G+2(T-20)G &=20+4T-10G+2TG. \end{aligned} \]

This is an algebraic identity. To check with real values, substitute T=19,G=1 into the uncentered equation: 20+76−10+38=124 kWh, exactly the centered prediction. Thus centering has not improved the in-sample fit, reduced SSE, or changed a predicted group gap. It has changed what the intercept and group coefficient describe: centering makes them refer to 20 °C, a relevant observed temperature, instead of zero.

A common misconception is that the changed coefficient −10 represents a reversal in the group effect. It does not. Under the raw equation the group contrast at T=19 is −10+2(19)=28, and at T=23 it is −10+46=36. Only the reference value for the lower-order group coefficient changed. If a report compares coefficients across centered and uncentered models without checking the reference point, it may announce a scientifically nonexistent contradiction.

Centering can also reduce numerical correlation among polynomial columns in particular designs, making fitted coefficients easier to compute and interpret. It does not eliminate genuine confounding, create randomization, or guarantee that standard errors shrink. Always distinguish algebraic reparameterization from a change in information.

12. The hierarchy principle prevents misleading specifications

An interaction tG is built from lower-order predictors t and G. The hierarchy principle normally retains both lower-order terms whenever their product is included, even when the individual lower-order p-values are large. Otherwise a model such as μ=β₀+β₃tG forces the A slope to zero, the B intercept gap at t=0 to zero, and several other restrictions that typically have no scientific justification.

The rationale is not simply tradition. If we recenter temperature, the product (T−20)G equals TG−20G. Dropping G from a model containing TG makes the specification depend on the arbitrary choice of whether zero means 0 °C or 20 °C. With the lower-order terms retained, shifting origins is a change of coordinates that preserves fitted values. A model that excludes them cannot generally maintain that invariance.

The full interaction model tests whether slopes differ while permitting intercepts to differ. If data are sparse, the model may be imprecise; that concern calls for uncertainty accounting, design improvement, regularization where justified, or a simpler model supported by reasoning. It does not make it valid to discard component terms merely because their individual p-values exceed 0.05.

The same principle will apply to a squared term t²: ordinarily include t as well. A pure-square model β₀+β₂t² forces the curve to have zero derivative at the chosen origin. That might be a carefully justified physical restriction, but it should not sneak in as an automatic consequence of a software variable-selection rule.

13. Change of reference category: the slopes must agree

Suppose we recode the group indicator as H=1−G, so H=1 identifies A rather than B. The predictions can be represented by μ̂=130+6t−30H−2tH. Verify H=0 (B): μ̂=130+6t; H=1 (A): μ̂=100+4t. The formerly positive interaction +2 becomes −2 only because we reversed the order of the slope difference. There is no scientific reversal.

The reference group is the category whose indicator equals zero. In treatment coding, the intercept and basic temperature slope refer to that group; the indicator and product coefficients express deviations from it. Reversing the reference changes their numerical signs and meanings while leaving fitted group lines identical. If the model has three or more categories, include appropriate indicators and interactions relative to a stated reference; avoid the dummy-variable trap of including a full set of mutually exclusive indicators together with an intercept.

A reliable reporting habit is to state the two group-specific slopes and both conditional predictions before discussing the sign of an interaction coefficient. The sentence 'the interaction is negative' is incomplete unless the reader knows which group and which contrast direction were coded. A negative interaction can coexist with positive slopes in both groups, as here under H coding.

Group labels are not naturally ordered numbers. Coding A=0 and B=1 does not imply that B has 'one unit more building type.' The one-unit indicator change simply denotes a category contrast. This matters when translating formulas into physical or policy language.

14. Continuous-by-continuous interactions: two moving slopes

Now suppose a model includes centered temperature t and insulation index z, where z is a unitless numeric measure and greater values represent stronger insulation in a hypothetical rating. The fitted conditional mean is μ̂=100+4t−10z−1.5tz, in kWh. This illustrative coefficient model is separate from the eight-row estimated dataset; the coefficients are stipulated for teaching how to read a two-continuous-predictor interaction, not claimed as fitted from those eight rows.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 14
\[ \boxed{\displaystyle \widehat\mu(t,z)=100+4t-10z-1.5tz} \]

Holding z fixed, the one-degree change in predicted energy is 4−1.5z kWh/°C. At z=0 it is 4; at z=2 it is 1. Holding t fixed, the one-unit change in z is −10−1.5t kWh per index unit. At t=0 the z association is −10, while at t=3 it is −14.5. Thus the same interaction coefficient supports two equivalent directions of interpretation: higher z weakens the temperature slope, and higher temperature makes the z association more negative.

Unlike the binary case, every value of z can have its own temperature slope. That means a plot might show several lines corresponding to selected z values. The interaction term makes a surface that is not an additive plane; along one predictor direction with the other held fixed, however, the relationship remains linear. Students should not confuse a visually warped response surface with a squared single-variable curve.

Check one prediction: t=2,z=2 gives 100+8−20−6=82 kWh. At t=3,z=2 the prediction is 100+12−20−9=83, a one-degree change of 1. At t=2,z=0 the model predicts 108; at t=3,z=0 it predicts 112, a change of 4. We have verified the conditional slopes by direct subtraction rather than invoking calculus alone.

15. Derivatives and finite changes: two related tools

For a continuously varying predictor x, a derivative is the instantaneous rate at which a smooth fitted mean changes per very small change in x, with other predictors held fixed. We denote it ∂μ/∂x when the model depends on multiple inputs. In the bilinear model β₀+β₁t+β₂z+β₃tz, the partial derivative with respect to t is β₁+β₃z. Because this function is exactly linear in t at fixed z, the derivative also equals the change for a full one-degree increase. But this equality will not hold for every nonlinear model.

For finite changes, start from the definition Δμ=μ(t+h,z)−μ(t,z). Substitute the bilinear model, cancel terms common to both predictions, and obtain h(β₁+β₃z). This expression has units kWh, since h is in degrees Celsius and the slope is kWh/°C. At h=2 and z=2 in our example, Δμ=2(4−3)=2 kWh. If one wants the change from 19 to 23 °C, h=4, not 1.

For simultaneous changes in both t and z, the interaction creates a cross-product term: Δμ=(β₁+β₃z)h+(β₂+β₃t)k+β₃hk, where h is the temperature change and k the insulation-index change. The last term cannot be recovered by adding the two separate initial slopes alone. This shows concretely why 'holding everything else fixed' matters: one-predictor slopes are not a complete description of a simultaneous policy scenario.

We distinguish derivative, finite contrast, and causal intervention. A derivative is a mathematical property of the fitted function; a finite contrast compares two fitted predictions; a causal intervention would require real-world assumptions about what happens when a predictor is changed. An equation by itself establishes only the first two.

16. Quadratic regression: a linear model can trace a curve

The word linear in linear regression refers to linearity in its unknown coefficients, not necessarily straightness in the predictors. Consider μ̂(t)=a+bt+ct². Each coefficient appears to the first power and the design columns are 1,t,t². This is a linear-in-parameters regression even though the function is curved in t. Ordinary least squares can estimate a,b,c using the same machinery from Guide 52.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 16
\[ \boxed{\displaystyle \widehat\mu(t)=a+bt+ct^2,\qquad \frac{d\widehat\mu}{dt}=b+2ct} \]

The derivative varies with t unless c=0. A positive c makes the curve convex (U-shaped when the turning point lies in the domain); a negative c makes it concave. Neither curvature sign automatically implies that higher temperatures always increase consumption: the direction depends on t. The turning point solves b+2ct=0 and equals t*=−b/(2c) when c≠0. That location must be checked against the observed predictor range before interpreting it as a relevant minimum or maximum.

Quadratic regression is often a reasonable local approximation when a relationship bends smoothly. It is not a universal physical model. A third-degree term can bend again, a spline can change flexibility across intervals, and a genuinely nonlinear-in-parameters model may require different estimation. We introduce a quadratic because it allows the conditional temperature slope to change with temperature while remaining accessible from introductory OLS.

Do not confuse the temperature squared column with a new independent dataset. The t² value is calculated from the same temperature observation; it contains no additional measurement. Its coefficient adds model flexibility, not additional sample size. That matters when calculating degrees of freedom and assessing overfitting.

For a separate synthetic exercise, consider five one-hour demand observations at t=(−2,−1,0,1,2) °C relative to 20 °C. All energy values are in kWh. The constructed responses are 52.1, 48.6, 50.6, 54.6 and 64.1. The central values rise modestly while the right-hand tail rises sharply, suggesting a curve rather than one constant slope. With only five rows, however, the fitted shape is an illustration, not a basis for confident extrapolation.

SSE=0.01+0.16+0.36+0.16+0.01=0.70 kWh²; with n=5 and p=3, residual degrees of freedom are 2, MSE=0.35 kWh² and s≈0.592 kWh. The residual design was chosen intentionally to make the fitted coefficients exact; the numbers are not sampled evidence that actual energy follows precisely this quadratic.

18. Derive the quadratic coefficients instead of guessing them

The sample symmetry makes a particularly clear numerical proof. Across t=(−2,−1,0,1,2), Σt=0, Σt³=0, Σt²=10 and Σt⁴=34. Sum the five observed Y values: 52.1+48.6+50.6+54.6+64.1=270. Compute ΣtY=(−2)(52.1)−48.6+54.6+2(64.1)=−104.2−48.6+54.6+128.2=30. Compute Σt²Y=4(52.1)+48.6+54.6+4(64.1)=208.4+48.6+54.6+256.4=568.

The normal equations for a+bt+ct² are 5a+10c=270, 10b=30, and 10a+34c=568. The middle equation gives b=3. Multiply the first by two to obtain 10a+20c=540; subtract from the third to get 14c=28 and c=2. Back-substitute: 5a+20=270, so a=50. We have recovered the fitted quadratic by explicit linear algebra with no optimization library.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 18
\[ \begin{aligned} 5a+10c&=270,\\ 10b&=30,\\ 10a+34c&=568 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad(a,b,c)=(50,3,2). \end{aligned} \]

Unlike a loose 'curve fitting' instruction, this derivation identifies exactly which observed sums determine the three coefficients. It also shows why sample size matters: three free coefficients fit a shape flexible enough to pass through any three distinct x values, but with only three observations residual degrees of freedom would be zero and ordinary residual-based uncertainty could not be estimated. Our five observations leave two degrees of freedom, still a very small sample for serious inference.

19. Interpret the changing slope and the turning point

For μ̂(t)=50+3t+2t², the instantaneous slope is 3+4t kWh/°C. At 19 °C, t=−1, it is 3−4=−1 kWh/°C; at 20 °C, t=0, it is +3; at 22 °C, t=2, it is +11. The coefficient of t, namely 3, is only the slope at the reference point t=0. Calling it the overall temperature slope ignores the very purpose of including t².

The turning point is t=−3/(2×2)=−0.75, corresponding to T=19.25 °C. Evaluate the fitted mean: 50+3(−0.75)+2(0.5625)=50−2.25+1.125=48.875 kWh. Because c=2>0, this is a fitted local minimum. It lies between 18 and 22 °C, so it is an interpolation within the example's range. Whether a real energy system has that minimum requires real measurements, diagnostics and substantive knowledge.

The predicted change from 20 to 21 °C is μ̂(1)−μ̂(0)=55−50=5 kWh, not 3 kWh. Why? The instantaneous slope at 20 °C is 3, but it rises as temperature increases; across the full degree the average slope is 5. Algebraically, μ̂(t+h)−μ̂(t)=h(b+2ct)+ch². For h=1, this is b+2ct+c, while the derivative is b+2ct. Distinguishing these two calculations prevents incorrect predictions in nonlinear models.

At t=−2, the derivative is −5 even though the fitted value at t=−2 is 52. A negative local derivative says that a small move rightward initially lowers the fitted mean; it does not say predicted consumption is negative. Units are again essential: the derivative is kWh/°C, while the fitted level is kWh.

20. Centering a quadratic without changing its curve

Suppose we rewrite μ̂(t)=50+3t+2t² in terms of raw T=t+20. Substitute t=T−20 and expand: μ̂=50+3(T−20)+2(T−20)²=50+3T−60+2(T²−40T+400). Collect terms: μ̂=790−77T+2T². The raw linear coefficient is −77, which looks dramatically different from +3, but the entire curve and every prediction are unchanged.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 20
\[ \boxed{\displaystyle 50+3(T-20)+2(T-20)^2=790-77T+2T^2} \]

Verify T=21. The centered equation gives 50+3+2=55; the raw equation gives 790−77(21)+2(21²)=790−1617+882=55. Different lower-order coefficients are simply the consequence of changing the coordinate origin while retaining the full hierarchical polynomial. In the raw equation the derivative is −77+4T; at T=20 this equals 3, matching the centered coefficient.

This is why a slope coefficient in a polynomial must always be accompanied by the point at which it applies. Report the entire fitted equation, a graph over observed values, and derivative or finite contrasts at meaningful temperatures. Merely comparing the sign of −77 across studies with different temperature origins would be scientifically incoherent.

As with interactions, centering may improve interpretability and sometimes numerical stability; it does not create data, guarantee correct functional form, or solve an omitted-variable problem. Keep both the linear and squared columns unless a clearly defended restriction is intended.

21. Interaction versus curvature: two distinct questions

An interaction tG says a slope differs between groups; a squared term t² says a slope changes with temperature. They are not interchangeable. In our group example, each type has a constant fitted slope over the observed range, but the slopes differ between types. In our quadratic example, one curve has a slope that changes continuously as t changes. Both models remain linear in coefficients, and both can be fitted by OLS with appropriately constructed columns.

A combined model could include both: μ=β₀+β₁t+β₂G+β₃tG+β₄t². Then group-specific lines become parallel-shape quadratic curves with the same curvature β₄ and different local slopes separated by β₃. Adding β₅t²G would permit curvature to differ by group too. Each additional term increases complexity and consumes degrees of freedom. Without adequate sample sizes and range overlap, the model can fit noise rather than stable structure.

Do not automatically add every possible term. If the scientific question is 'Are temperature slopes different by building class?' the tG interaction is directly relevant. If residual plots show a systematic U shape within both classes, shared curvature may be relevant. If there is a physical heating–cooling threshold, a piecewise model may be more plausible than a high-degree polynomial. Scientific interpretation should lead feature construction, not follow an indiscriminate search for low p-values.

In the original eight-row example, adding separate quadratic terms with only four temperatures per group is technically possible but leaves very few residual degrees of freedom and may overfit. That is a design limitation, not a demand to make the model increasingly complex.

22. Comparing models fairly: nested tests, prediction, and assumptions

The additive group model is nested within the interaction model because setting β₃=0 recovers parallel lines. Under classical homoskedastic, independent, normal-error conditions, the partial F test compares the reduction in SSE per added coefficient with the residual MSE of the larger model. In our balanced example the interaction model has SSE_full=8 and df_full=4. An additive fit has one common slope: since both groups have equal Σt², the pooled slope is (80+120)/(20+20)=5. Group intercepts remain 100 and 130. The slope mismatch contributes 20×(4−5)²+20×(6−5)²=40 extra squared kWh, so SSE_reduced=48.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 22
\[ \begin{aligned} F&=\frac{(SSE_R-SSE_F)/(p_F-p_R)}{SSE_F/(n-p_F)}\\ &=\frac{(48-8)/(4-3)}{8/(8-4)}=20. \end{aligned} \]

Thus F(1,4)=20, exactly t² for the one-parameter interaction test: (4.4721)²=20. This identity provides an independent numerical check. The test asks whether relaxing the common-slope restriction improves fit more than expected under the specified error model; it does not prove causality or validate straight-line behavior outside the sample.

Prediction comparisons require a different discipline. In-sample SSE cannot increase when adding a column, even if that column is useless. Therefore a lower training SSE alone does not justify the complexity. Evaluate out-of-sample performance using a test set or suitable cross-validation, with grouping or time separation where observations are dependent. Do not select a model using the test data repeatedly and then report that same test error as an unbiased assessment.

If variance differs by group, the classical pooled-MSE F can be unreliable. Robust methods or a more appropriate variance model may be needed. A plot of residuals versus fitted values and temperature, plus domain knowledge, is part of interpretation—not an optional decoration after a statistically significant p-value.

23. Hierarchical tests and honest uncertainty reporting

When a model contains t, G and tG, report the three substantive quantities that readers need: the A slope, the B slope, and their difference, each with units. A single p-value for β₃ gives a test of the difference of slopes, not tests of each group's slope or of each conditional group gap. If we want a confidence interval for the group contrast at t=3, the estimate is β̂₂+3β̂₃. Its standard error generally depends on Var(β̂₂), Var(β̂₃) and 2×3Cov(β̂₂,β̂₃). We cannot obtain it by adding coefficient standard errors.

For this balanced, centered dataset, group intercept estimates are independent of their within-group slope estimates under the classical fixed-design model, and the two groups are independent by assumption. Each group intercept variance estimate is MSE/4=0.5; the group gap intercept variance is 0.5+0.5=1. For the gap at t=3, add t² times the slope-difference variance: 1+9(0.2)=2.8; SE=√2.8≈1.673 kWh. At t=−1 the SE is √(1+0.2)=√1.2≈1.095 kWh. Hence contrasts at different temperatures have different precision even though they use the same regression.

The classical 95% interval for the gap at t=3 would be 36±2.776√2.8≈[31.35,40.65] kWh. These are model-based intervals, not physical prediction guarantees. For individual future hours, the error of a new observation must also be included, producing a wider prediction interval.

If many temperatures and many subgroups are screened after examining the data, multiplicity becomes relevant; Guide 46 explains false-discovery and familywise methods. A confidence band for the entire temperature range is not the same as a separate 95% interval at each selected temperature. Scope the inferential claim before publishing it.

24. Transformations of predictors: scales change interpretations

Sometimes the shape is easier to represent after transforming a predictor. A model μ=α+β ln X is linear in ln X, not in raw X. It requires X>0. The derivative with respect to X is β/X, so the raw-unit slope decreases in magnitude as X increases. For a doubling of X, Δμ=β[ln(2X)−ln X]=β ln 2. Thus a coefficient β=8 kWh per one natural-log unit implies a predicted 8ln2≈5.545 kWh difference between X and 2X, regardless of the starting positive X value.

A polynomial t² does not require positive t; a logarithm of t does. If t=T−20 can be negative, ln t is undefined for cold temperatures. Do not add a constant until it works and then forget that the chosen constant changes the model and its interpretation. A physically meaningful positive predictor, such as floor area in m², may be more natural for a logarithm than centered Celsius.

Rescaling also matters. If temperature changes from Celsius degrees to a ten-degree index u=t/10, then μ=100+4t+30G+2tG becomes μ=100+40u+30G+20uG. The 40 and 20 are kWh per ten-degree unit; the fitted consumption for a specified physical temperature is unchanged. Coefficient magnitude is never interpretable without its unit and scaling definition.

A predictor transformation changes which patterns are linear, and can help smooth curvature, but a plausible transformation does not cure confounding or independence violations. Use graphical diagnostics, scientific constraints, and prediction performance to judge its usefulness.

25. Transformations of the response and back-transformation

A model for log demand is different from a model for demand: E[ln Y|x]=α+βx, with Y>0. The additive effect β is on the log scale. A one-unit x increase shifts the fitted log outcome by β, and the corresponding multiplicative change in the exponentiated fitted value is exp(β). For β=0.04, exp(0.04)−1≈0.04081, or 4.081%; for five units, exp(0.20)−1≈0.22140, or 22.14%. Multiplying 4% by five gives 20%, not the exact compounded change.

KEY EQUATION · STEP 25
\[ \boxed{\displaystyle \frac{\exp(\alpha+\beta(x+h))}{\exp(\alpha+\beta x)}=\exp(\beta h)} \]

Crucial subtlety: exponentiating the predicted conditional mean of ln Y generally does not give E[Y|x]. Under lognormal errors with constant variance σ², E[Y|x]=exp(α+βx+σ²/2), while exp(α+βx) is the conditional median in that model. Other error structures require different retransformation methods, such as a justified smearing estimator. Therefore a spreadsheet that simply exponentiates its log regression output can systematically underpredict the arithmetic mean.

Changing response scale also changes the loss being minimized by OLS: squared residuals are now in squared log units rather than kWh². A better log-scale fit does not automatically guarantee a better kWh-scale forecast. Evaluate predictions on the scale used for decisions, report whether a level refers to median or mean, and never apply log to zero or negative consumption without a carefully justified alternative.

26. Diagnostic checks for interactions and curves

Start with plots of residuals against temperature separately by building group. If the additive model leaves one group's residuals trending up with temperature and the other's trending down, missing interaction is plausible. If the interaction model leaves a U-shaped residual pattern in both groups, shared curvature may be worth examining. If residual spread increases strongly with fitted demand, constant-variance standard errors may be questionable. Plotting the observations and fitted lines is therefore part of checking the model's restrictions.

Ask whether observations are independent. Multiple hours from the same building, consecutive hours in the same day, or shared outside weather may create serial or clustered dependence. Then eight 'rows' do not necessarily represent eight independent replications. Standard errors from the toy calculation should not be transplanted to real panel energy data without revisiting that assumption. Residual normality is relevant for small-sample classical t and F formulas, but a visually normal residual histogram alone cannot demonstrate independence or absence of omitted drivers.

High-leverage temperatures at the edge of a narrow range may determine the curvature and interaction coefficients. A single unusually hot hour may be influential; automatically dropping it because it changes a p-value is not sound practice. Examine measurement accuracy, context, fit sensitivity and prespecified rules. Guide 54 develops leverage, influence and collinearity in detail; here the warning is enough to keep the Guide 53 inference in bounds.

Finally, evaluate stability across relevant groups and ranges. If buildings of type B have data only in summer and A only in winter, a group-by-temperature product may be weakly identified even when software prints a number. Overlap is not merely a cosmetic feature of our chosen example; it is the design feature that makes conditional comparisons empirically anchored.

27. Design, interpretation, and causal caution

A fitted interaction measures effect modification on the regression's scale, but the word effect here can be statistical rather than causal. The comparison of B and A at 20 °C may reflect floor area, insulation, occupancy, window placement, pricing incentives, or selection into building types. Temperature itself may be correlated with time of day and season, making a regression coefficient an imperfect intervention effect. A randomized insulation intervention or a carefully justified causal design would answer a stronger question than a cross-sectional association.

Interactions are also scale-dependent. A constant additive difference on the kWh scale can produce a varying percentage difference, while a constant percentage relationship on a log scale can produce a varying absolute difference. Therefore 'there is no interaction' must always be qualified by the chosen response scale, predictors, contrast and functional form. Statistically significant terms are not invariant facts about the world.

When advising a decision-maker, show the predicted outcomes for realistic scenarios, the units, the plausible range, and the uncertainty. A statement such as 'B is always worse' can be contradicted by a temperature-dependent contrast, a recoded group, or omitted variables. Our example has B's fitted energy greater across the observed temperatures, but that is a statement about the constructed example only, not a universal finding about building classes.

Explain what would change your conclusion: more independent buildings, temperature coverage on both sides of any suspected threshold, a robust variance estimate, checks for time correlation, and out-of-sample validation. That moves regression from coefficient memorization toward scientific reasoning.

28. Exercise 1 — reconstruct a group slope from raw records

Task. Using only the four type-A observations, compute the temperature slope and reference-temperature intercept. Explicitly state why dividing a response difference by a temperature difference is not quite the same as calculating OLS when residual noise exists.

Solution. Set t=(−3,−1,1,3), Y=(89,95,103,113). Mean t=0 and mean Y=100. Compute Σt²=20 and Σt(Y−100)=80, so b_A=80/20=4 kWh/°C. The intercept is a_A=100−4(0)=100 kWh at 20 °C. Predict 96 kWh at 19 °C and compare observed 95, giving residual −1. An endpoint slope (113−89)/(3−(−3))=24/6=4 happens to match here because we deliberately chose symmetric residuals, but in general endpoint differences use only two rows whereas OLS uses all four to minimize squared residuals.

Check and interpretation. Predictions (88,96,104,112) yield residuals (+1,−1,−1,+1); the weighted residual sum is zero. The coefficient describes a fitted association within the measured temperature range and this synthetic group; it does not show a causal response to changing the thermostat. Explain in your own words why the slope unit is kWh/°C rather than kWh alone.

29. Exercise 2 — rebuild the interaction and compare groups

Task. Given the separate fitted lines μ̂_A=100+4t and μ̂_B=130+6t, write the combined indicator model, identify each coefficient, and calculate B−A at 17 and 23 °C.

Solution. The A intercept is 100, its slope 4. Relative to A, B's intercept is 130−100=30 and its slope increment 6−4=2. Therefore μ̂=100+4t+30G+2tG. At 17 °C, t=−3, A prediction=88 and B=112, gap=24; at 23 °C, t=3, A=112 and B=148, gap=36. Verify by direct contrast 30+2(−3)=24 and 30+2(3)=36.

Interpretation. The group gap changes by 12 kWh over a six-degree range, giving 2 kWh/°C. The interaction coefficient is a slope difference, not the gap at every temperature. If the exercise were answered only with 'β₃=2 significant,' it would omit the core question about predicted energy levels.

30. Exercise 3 — center temperature and expose the reference

Task. Rewrite μ̂=100+4(T−20)+30G+2(T−20)G using raw T. Explain why the group coefficient turns negative without a reversed fitted comparison.

Solution. Expand: 100+4T−80+30G+2TG−40G=20+4T−10G+2TG. At T=21,G=0, predicted demand is 20+84=104; at T=21,G=1 it is 20+84−10+42=136. Their gap is 32, also 30+2(1)=32. The lower-order coefficient −10 describes the model's B−A contrast at 0 °C, far outside the observed range; it is not the contrast at 21 °C.

Check and interpretation. Both parameterizations give the same fitted 104 and 136, and SSE remains 8. Centering changes the reference origin, not the underlying line for either group. In a scientific report use the centered version because 20 °C is interpretable within the data.

31. Exercise 4 — calculate slope uncertainty by hand

Task. Under classical independent equal-variance errors, obtain the standard error, t statistic and 95% interval for the interaction coefficient in the eight-row example.

Solution. SSE=8, n=8, p=4, so MSE=8/(8−4)=2. Each group has Σt²=20 and slope variance estimate 2/20=0.1. Their estimated difference is 2, variance 0.1+0.1=0.2 and SE≈0.447214. The test statistic is 2/0.447214=4.47214, df=4. With t_{.975,4}≈2.77645, the margin is 2.77645×0.447214≈1.24167. The interval is [0.75833,3.24167] kWh/°C, subject to rounding.

Interpretation and limitation. Under the stated sampling assumptions the data are inconsistent with zero slope difference at the 5% two-sided level. The statement is model-based, not a guarantee that a genuine randomized insulation treatment changes energy. If consecutive hours shared correlated shocks, the classical SE calculation would need revision.

32. Exercise 5 — distinguish a conditional difference from its SE

Task. Calculate the type-B minus type-A fitted contrast at 23 °C and its classical standard error, using the centered balanced design. Give a 95% interval.

Solution. At 23 °C, t=3. Difference=30+2(3)=36 kWh. For group intercept estimates at t=0, variance per group=MSE/4=0.5, so the difference has variance 1. The slope difference variance is 0.2. Due to centered within-group t and the block-orthogonal structure, the intercept-difference and slope-difference estimators have zero covariance. Thus Var[gap(3)]≈1+3²(0.2)=2.8, SE=√2.8≈1.67332 kWh. A 95% confidence interval is 36±2.77645(1.67332)≈[31.35,40.65] kWh.

Interpretation and verification. This interval concerns the conditional mean difference at 23 °C, not the difference of two single future hours. Its standard error is not SE(β₂)+3SE(β₃); variances and covariances must be handled correctly. Compare the estimated gap at 19 °C, 28 kWh, where SE is √1.2≈1.09545.

33. Exercise 6 — calculate a continuous interaction from two directions

Task. Given μ̂=100+4t−10z−1.5tz, compare temperatures 20 and 22 °C at z=0 and z=2, then compare z=0 and z=2 at 22 °C.

Solution. At t=0,z=0 the prediction is 100; at t=2,z=0 it is 108, a +8 kWh change. At t=0,z=2 it is 100−20=80; at t=2,z=2 it is 100+8−20−6=82, a +2 kWh change. The temperature slope is 4−1.5z, or 4 at z=0 and 1 at z=2, so two degrees yield 8 and 2. At t=2, moving z from 0 to 2 changes the prediction from 108 to 82, namely −26 kWh. The z derivative at t=2 is −10−3=−13 per index unit; over two units that produces −26.

Interpretation. The result is conditional in both directions. The cross-term changes each predictor's local association depending on the other. A two-unit z contrast is not a '2%' difference unless the z index was explicitly defined as a percentage, which it was not.

34. Exercise 7 — fit the quadratic with normal equations

Task. From the five-row quadratic dataset, calculate a,b,c, then show that the residuals satisfy the OLS orthogonality identities.

Solution. The sums are n=5, Σt=0, Σt²=10, Σt³=0, Σt⁴=34, ΣY=270, ΣtY=30 and Σt²Y=568. The normal equations reduce to 5a+10c=270, 10b=30 and 10a+34c=568. Hence b=3, c=(568−540)/(34−20)=28/14=2 and a=(270−10×2)/5=50. The fitted values are 52,49,50,55,64; residuals +0.1,−0.4,+0.6,−0.4,+0.1.

Check. Σe=0, Σte=0 and Σt²e=0, so the residual vector is orthogonal to all three columns. SSE=0.70 and df=2. We have fitted a curve with linear-in-parameters OLS, not a fundamentally different estimation algorithm.

35. Exercise 8 — derivative versus one-degree prediction

Task. For μ̂=50+3t+2t², find the derivative at 20 °C, the predicted change from 20 to 21 °C, and the turning-point temperature.

Solution. Since t=T−20, at 20 °C t=0; the derivative is 3+4(0)=3 kWh/°C. But μ̂(1)−μ̂(0)=(50+3+2)−50=5 kWh. The discrepancy of 2 comes from the quadratic term over a finite one-degree interval. The derivative equals zero when 3+4t=0, so t=−0.75, T=19.25 °C. At that point μ̂=48.875 kWh. The positive quadratic coefficient means the point is a fitted minimum.

Interpretation. A local derivative describes an infinitesimal rate; a finite prediction difference includes curvature over the whole temperature interval. The observed domain 18–22 °C contains the turning point, but a five-observation teaching example cannot establish a universal energy-demand optimum.

36. Exercise 9 — preserve a quadratic after recentering

Task. Reexpress μ̂=50+3(T−20)+2(T−20)² as a raw-temperature polynomial and confirm its prediction at 19 °C.

Solution. Expansion gives 50+3T−60+2(T²−40T+400)=790−77T+2T². At T=19, raw formula gives 790−1463+722=49 kWh; centered formula gives 50+3(−1)+2(1)=49. Both agree. The raw derivative is −77+4T; at T=20 it equals 3. The negative raw linear coefficient −77 is therefore not an overall negative fitted slope.

Interpretation. Polynomial coefficients are tied to their origin and units. The unchanged predicted curve is a more stable reporting object than an isolated lower-order coefficient, which can swing strongly under benign recentering.

37. Exercise 10 — choose between interaction and curvature

Task. A building analyst sees two near-straight but differently sloped group trends. Another analyst sees a U-shaped trend common to both groups. Which additional terms would directly address each pattern? State assumptions that must still be checked.

Solution. The first pattern suggests a tG term while retaining t and G. It allows group-specific slopes and a varying group gap. The second suggests t², retaining t, and perhaps G if both groups differ in level; a shared t² term gives common curvature. If the curves differ in curvature, t²G can be considered with its lower-order components retained. But additional terms require enough observations across temperature in every group.

Verification and limits. Inspect residuals by group, the physical range, leverage at endpoints, independent sampling, variance patterns, and out-of-sample prediction. Neither a visual pattern nor a low p-value proves a causal mechanism. A useful report presents fitted values at representative temperatures rather than naming a model class without consequences.

38. Exercise 11 — understand the log response exactly

Task. A model for ln(Y) reports β=0.04 per °C. Compute the multiplicative ratio for one degree and for five degrees. Explain why exponentiating a fitted log mean may not yield the mean consumption.

Solution. One degree multiplies the exponentiated fit by exp(0.04)≈1.04081, which is a 4.081% increase. Five degrees multiply it by exp(0.20)≈1.22140, a 22.140% increase. This is not exactly five times 4% because compounding is multiplicative. If ln Y=α+βx+ε and ε is normal with variance σ² independent of x, then E[Y|x]=exp(α+βx+σ²/2), whereas exp(α+βx) is the conditional median.

Interpretation. State which response-scale quantity is predicted, and evaluate accuracy on the required kWh scale. Repeatedly ignoring back-transformation bias can produce systematically low forecasts of mean demand; simply taking exp() is not always sufficient.

39. Exercise 12 — independent transfer case and responsible report

Task. A third synthetic building analysis proposes μ̂=70+3u+12G−0.5uG+0.4u², where u=T−20 °C and Y is one-hour kWh. Predict both types at 18 and 22 °C; calculate temperature derivatives there; state the group gaps and what evidence is missing before any causal claim.

Complete solution. At 18 °C, u=−2 and u²=4. A: 70+3(−2)+0.4(4)=70−6+1.6=65.6 kWh. B adds 12−0.5(−2)=13, giving 78.6. At 22 °C, u=2. A: 70+6+1.6=77.6; B adds 12−1=11, giving 88.6. The group gap declines from 13 to 11 as temperature rises four degrees, a slope of −0.5 kWh/°C for the gap.

The temperature derivative for A is 3+0.8u, namely 1.4 at 18 °C and 4.6 at 22 °C. For B it is 3−0.5+0.8u, namely 0.9 and 4.1. At each temperature, the B derivative is 0.5 lower than A's, while both derivatives rise with temperature due to common positive curvature. Check the finite two-point changes: A rises 77.6−65.6=12 over four degrees; B rises 88.6−78.6=10. Their average slopes, 3 and 2.5, lie between the relevant endpoint derivatives as expected for these quadratic functions.

Report. 'In this constructed model, predicted hourly demand rises over 18–22 °C for both types, with type B higher at both temperatures but a smaller B−A gap in warmer conditions. The associations are conditional on the model and represent no measured building behavior.' Before causal or operational deployment, obtain observed data with range overlap, measured confounders, uncertainty estimates, independent buildings or suitable dependence handling, physical checks and external prediction validation. This is the final transfer: predictions, conditional derivatives, interaction and curvature must all agree in units and interpretation.

40. A reusable reporting and student-understanding checklist

A rigorous analysis begins by specifying the outcome unit, the predictor scales and valid ranges, and whether an indicator comparison concerns a category or an intervention. It then identifies a baseline, makes the needed product or squared columns, fits a model appropriate to the scientific question, and verifies selected predictions directly from its equation. For each interaction, give both conditional slopes and a contrast at meaningful settings. For each quadratic, give the derivative at meaningful points, a finite comparison, and the location of any turning point with a warning about extrapolation.

Next, inspect residuals and sampling structure before assigning weight to t tests or confidence intervals. If uncertainty is reported, write the sampling assumptions and distinguish a mean interval from an individual prediction interval. If many models or cutoffs were considered, disclose the selection process. Translate coefficients back into kWh, temperature degrees, percentage changes or other units actually measured; do not let a standardized number obscure the physical decision.

Student Understanding Test: “If I were a student learning this topic for the first time, could I follow all the reasoning, reproduce the calculations, and explain in my own words why it works?” This is an editorial self-test, not a claim that students were experimentally tested. Reproduce the four model coefficients from the eight-row table; explain why recentering changes β₂ but not any prediction; derive one quadratic slope and one finite difference; and resolve the independent final transfer without looking at its solution. If any step remains opaque, return to the corresponding table and reconstruct the substitution rather than memorizing a slogan.

The lasting distinction is simple but demanding: a main-effect coefficient is conditional on reference values when interactions or curvature exist. A mathematically correct regression may still be scientifically inappropriate if the comparison lacks support in the data. The goal is not to maximize the number of model terms, but to use a transparent conditional model to make defensible, checkable predictions and interpretations.

For further conceptual reading, Penn State's STAT 501 materials on transformations and hierarchical polynomial fitting, Penn State's STAT 462 explanation of interactions between quantitative predictors, and UCLA OARC's interaction-visualization resources provide established terminology and examples. Sources: Penn State STAT 501: Transformations; Penn State STAT 501: Model pitfalls; Penn State STAT 462: Quantitative interactions; UCLA OARC: Interaction interpretation . The classroom numerical examples here are independent and intentionally simpler than observational energy research.

This guide develops what an interaction or curve changes in a fitted regression. A subsequent guide may study leverage, influential observations and multicollinearity with greater depth; we have signposted those concerns without replacing this guide's learning objective. Never promote an automatically selected nonlinear coefficient into a claim of causality, and never treat a curve beyond its observed support as physically verified merely because its algebra can be evaluated.
