01Turn a process benchmark into a complete inference

A quality team measures package fill mass and inspects units for defects. One question concerns the mean mass relative to a 500 g target; another concerns a defect probability relative to 5%. Both are one-sample problems, but they require different random variables, standard errors and reference distributions. A formula chosen only because the spreadsheet has one column can answer the wrong question.

The goal of a complete test is not merely to produce a p-value. You must identify the population parameter, sampling unit, benchmark, alternative and decision rule; calculate a meaningful effect and its uncertainty; verify the reference assumptions; and write a conclusion that preserves the limits of the evidence. A process can differ statistically from a target without that difference automatically deciding an operational action.

This guide develops two constructed quality cases from the observations through the final report. We calculate a mean test with unknown scale, contrast a genuinely known-scale Z scenario, and connect matched intervals and tests. Then we calculate an exact Binomial test, examine two-sided conventions, compare Normal approximations and show why zero observed defects do not imply zero population risk.

The intended reader has Calculus I and introductory statistics. Guides 14,30,37 and 40 provide Binomial, interval, p-value and planning foundations. We recall necessary notation and introduce local bridges for a t pivot, score inversion and effective independent information. Programming is optional; all central arithmetic and conclusions are developed in the text.

By the end you should be able to execute a test and explain why it represents the study. Independent observations and a credible benchmark matter more than an arbitrary sample-size rule. When results disagree, examine assumptions, tail definitions and interval construction before blaming software or selecting a preferred answer.

02State the parameter, benchmark and sampling unit

For fill mass, let X be one independently sampled package's mass in grams, μ = E(X) the process mean and σ its population SD. The benchmark μ₀ = 500 g is an engineering target treated as a fixed constant. Before sampling, X̄ is random; after observing values, x̄ is its numerical realization. The estimated effect is x̄ − μ₀, in grams.

For defects, define Yi = 1 when inspected unit i meets the prespecified defect definition and 0 otherwise. Let p = P(Yi = 1). A statistical success here means a coded event, not desirable quality. The benchmark p₀ = .05 is a fixed comparison probability. With n independent trials sharing p, the defect count K = ΣYi has Binomial(n, p) law and the estimate is p̂ = k/n.

The primary questions are two-sided equalities: H₀:μ = 500 versus μ≠500 and H₀: p = .05 versus p≠.05, with α = .05 fixed in advance. For the discrete primary test we declare the central doubled-smaller-inclusive-tail convention before calculating.

A benchmark estimated from an earlier small sample is not automatically a known constant. If the question compares two uncertain population means or probabilities, ignoring the reference estimate's uncertainty changes the problem. A one-sample procedure is appropriate here because the targets are stipulated standards; later two-sample guides treat estimated references.

A useful data audit includes measurement units, instrument calibration, sampling times, duplicates, missing readings and the operational definition of the population. Before continuing, distinguish the target 500 from the observed mean 502. We are not testing whether the sample average equals 500 as an arithmetic fact; it does not. We test whether this discrepancy is unusual under a probability model whose population mean is 500, accounting for variability of averages from repeated samples.

04Calculate sample SD and the SE of the mean

Around x̄502, the centered values are − 6, − 4, − 3, − 3, − 2, − 2, − 1, − 1,1,1,2,2,3,3,4,6. Their squares sum 160 g². The sample variance is s² = 160/(16 − 1) = 32/3 ≈ 10.666666667 g², and s = √(32/3) ≈ 3.265986324 g.

The denominator 15 reflects estimating the mean from these 16 observations: the centered deviations must sum 0, leaving 15 residual degrees of freedom. It is not a rule that every variance everywhere uses n − 1. A completely specified probability distribution has its own weighted variance calculation; here s² estimates an unknown process variance from a sample.

For independent observations sharing variance σ², Var(X̄) = σ²/n. Its estimated standard error is s/√n = 3.265986324/4 = .816496581 g. SD describes variation among individual packages; SE describes variation of sample averages. Dividing by√n changes the random object whose spread is being described.

05Choose Z or t from the scale assumptions

A known-scale mean test uses Z = (X̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n). Under independent Normal outcomes and a genuinely known population SD, Z is standard Normal under H₀ at any n. This finite-model result does not require a rule such as n ≥ 30. With non-Normal data, a Normal approximation needs its own justification.

More commonly σ is unknown and estimated by s from the tested sample. Then T = (X̄ − μ₀)/(S/√n) has a t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom under independent Normal observations. The heavier tails reflect uncertainty in the estimated scale. The distributional result uses independence of the Normal sample mean and sample variance; we state that result rather than proving the full theorem here.

A long historical record may support a separate scale model, but a historical SD estimate is not automatically known σ. Its stability, uncertainty and relevance to the current process matter. Selecting Z merely because a software default requests a number can understate uncertainty.

The t procedure is also not assumption free. Strong skew, influential outliers, dependence or a changing process can make a small-sample t reference inappropriate. A large number of rows alone does not prove a useful approximation. Inspect how much independent information the design actually supplies.

For our main case σ is unknown, so the primary analysis is t. We separately calculate an external-known-σ = 4 scenario to show how Z works. These are different information assumptions applied to the same observed data; comparing their numbers does not prove that one reference is universally narrower or more significant.

The t formula requires a positive estimated SE. If all recorded readings are identical, s = 0 makes this statistic undefined; do not replace it with an invented finite value or declare certainty. Examine measurement resolution, data handling and the model before selecting a procedure for that degenerate dataset.

06Complete the external-known-SD Z comparison

Suppose, as a separate stipulated scenario, that the current process population SD is known externally to be 4 g and the observations are iid Normal. The observed mean remains 502 and the target 500. The known-scale SE is 4/√16 = 1 g, so zobs = (502 − 500)/1 = 2.

For the planned two-sided α = .05 test, reject if |Z|>z.975 ≈ 1.959963985. The observed 2 lies in that region. Its p-value is 2[1 − Φ(2)] ≈ .045500264. The area is taken under the null law of the standardized mean, not under the distribution of individual fill masses.

The matching 95% interval for μ is 502±1.959963985 × 1 = [500.040036015,503.959963985]g. It excludes 500, consistent with the two-sided Z decision. The effect interval relative to the target subtracts 500 from both ends, giving[.040036015,3.959963985]g.

A p-value near .05 is not a discontinuity in practical evidence. Report the 2 g effect and interval, not merely significant. Also report how the external σ assumption was established. If 4 was only a noisy sample estimate, this calculation's known-scale interpretation would not follow.

This scenario does not justify replacing the main unknown-scale analysis. Its larger SE 1 differs from the estimated .816496581 in the main sample. As a result, even though t has heavier standardized tails, the unknown-scale interval here can be narrower than this external-σ interval. Reference shape and the scale in the denominator both matter.

07Complete the primary unknown-scale t test

The primary mass analysis treats σ as unknown. We already have x̄502 g, s 3.265986324 g and SE .816496581 g. Standardize the discrepancy:

tobs = (502 − 500)/.816496581 = 2.449489743, with ν = 16 − 1 = 15.

Under H₀ and the independent Normal model, compare with t₁₅. At α = .05 two-sided, the critical magnitude is t.975,15 ≈ 2.131449546. Since 2.449489743>2.131449546, reject the equal-mean null. The corresponding original-scale cutoff is 500±2.131449546 × .816496581, or 500±1.740321266 for sample means under this estimated-scale calculation.

The observed p-value is 2 P(T₁₅ ≥ 2.449489743) ≈ .027068490. It is not the Normal tail at 2.449, because the denominator was estimated. The null reference incorporates the random scale through its t law.

The conclusion is evidence of a population mean different from 500 under the stated model and sampling assumptions. The estimated direction is positive and the magnitude 2 g. We have not established that every package is overweight, that all production periods share this mean, or that a specific machine adjustment caused the difference.

A test can reject while important operational uncertainty remains. The interval in the next section supplies a scale of mean uncertainty. Engineering tolerances, costs and individual-package specifications are separate requirements. A statistical test of the mean does not certify compliance of every item.

Those original-scale bounds use the observed s. In another sample, the t procedure re-estimates s, so its original-scale bounds change. The fixed rule is |T|>t.975,15, not a universal fixed cutoff on all future sample means.

08Invert the same pivot to obtain a matching interval

A 95% t interval uses x̄±t.975,15s/√n. The margin is 2.131449546 × .816496581 = 1.740321266 g. Therefore μ lies in the reported confidence set[500.259678734,503.740321266]g, with an effect interval[.259678734,3.740321266]g relative to the 500 g target.

The connection to the test is algebraic. For any candidate μ₀, the condition |(x̄ − μ₀)/SE| ≤ c is equivalent to x̄ − cSE ≤ μ₀ ≤ x̄ + cSE when SE>0. The nonrejected candidates form this interval because the test and interval share a pivot, reference, direction and confidence/significance pairing.

The confidence level describes repeated sampling: under the independent Normal model, this procedure includes the fixed μ in 95% of repeated samples. It is not a posterior probability that μ lies within these particular bounds. The true mean does not become random merely because it is unknown.

A confidence interval for the mean is not a prediction interval for a new package or a tolerance interval for the population of package masses. The individual values in our sample already range from 496 to 508, much wider than the mean interval. They concern another random object and require another construction.

The figure also displays the external-known-σ Z interval and a labeled data-sensitivity interval developed below. Compare assumptions and scale, not just width. A narrow interval under an invalid independence assumption would provide misleading precision rather than stronger evidence.

09Prespecify a direction and keep its tail and interval aligned

A two-sided mass test asks about either upward or downward drift. If the operational question had instead been only excess mean fill, a prespecified alternative would be H₁:μ>500, with composite null μ ≤ 500. Under the same t model, the largest null rejection probability occurs at the boundary 500, which supplies the reference calculation.

For the observed positive t 2.449489743, the upper-tail p-value is .013534245, half the symmetric two-sided value. A lower-direction test has p ≈ .986465755. The data do not support both directional questions equally; the sign matters.

A one-sided 95% lower confidence bound pairs with a positive-direction level .05 test. It uses t.95,15, not t.975,15, and has no finite upper bound. Comparing a one-sided test to a two-sided 95% interval without accounting for their different cutoffs can create an apparent contradiction.

Choose direction because of the scientific question before seeing data. Looking at the sign and then reporting the favorable one-sided tail changes the selection procedure and does not retain the originally claimed error control. The two-sided primary analysis remains the one declared for our examples.

Nonrejection also needs careful wording. It means the particular procedure did not find sufficient evidence at its declared level. It does not prove exact equality, equivalence, acceptable production or absence of a practically meaningful change. Those conclusions require margins, precision and decision rules suited to their own questions.

10Investigate an influential measurement without silently deleting it

Consider a labeled sensitivity scenario: replace the last 508 g reading with 528 g, leaving the other 15 values unchanged. The sum becomes 8052 and mean 503.25 g. The estimated effect grows from 2 to 3.25 g, but the centered sum of squares increases from 160 to 775 g².

The new sample variance is 775/15 = 51.666666667 g², s ≈ 7.187952884 g and SE ≈ 1.796988221 g. The resulting t = (503.25 − 500)/1.796988221 ≈ 1.808581693 gives two-sided p ≈ .090602951. Its 95% interval is[499.419810273,507.080189727]g and includes 500.

The larger observed effect now has a larger p-value because uncertainty increased even more. This illustrates why p-values are not effect-size rankings. It does not prove that the new observation is wrong or that the original sample is more representative.

In a real audit, check instrument records, units, transcription, production timing and whether the value belongs to the stated process. A verified measurement error can be corrected with a documented rule. A genuine extreme value may reveal a heavy tail, mixture or process change that challenges the small-sample Normal model. Deleting it solely to restore significance is not a valid analysis plan.

Report sensitivity transparently and distinguish a change of data from a change of inferential method. A robust alternative may be useful, but changing from a mean to a median changes the target, and no resampling method automatically repairs a biased or dependent sample. Resolve the scientific explanation before presenting a cleaned p-value.

11Specify the defect event and the Binomial assumptions

The second constructed case inspects 50 independently sampled units from a stable process. Six meet a defect definition fixed before inspection;44 do not. The sufficient observed summary for the common-probability Binomial model is n = 50, k = 6. We do not invent detailed unit-level records or imply these are the 16 weighed packages.

The estimate is p̂ = 6/50 = .12, or 12%. Relative to p₀ = .05, the absolute estimated excess is .07, or 7 percentage points. The probability ratio is .12/.05 = 2.4 and the relative increase is 140%. A large relative increase from a small baseline still needs an uncertainty assessment; the observed ratio is not proof of the population ratio.

The Binomial model requires a fixed number of trials, binary outcomes, independence and a common event probability. Clear inspection criteria and consistent detection matter. A unit with several defects is still one defective unit if Yi indicates any defect; counting all defects would define another variable and potentially another model.

Under H₀: p = .05, K∼Binomial(50,.05), with mean np₀ = 2.5 and variance np₀(1 − p₀) = 2.375. These are null expectations, not requirements that the realized count equal 2.5. The count is integer; the expectation need not be.

Our primary two-sided test uses the central doubled-smaller-inclusive-tail rule at α = .05, chosen to align with a central exact interval. An alternative probability-ordering test is presented separately. Naming the convention prevents the common mistake of treating every exact two-sided Binomial calculation as the same method.

12Construct null masses and count inclusive events

For k = 0,…,50, the null mass is

b(k) = C(50, k)(.05)^k(.95)^(50 − k).

C(50, k) counts ways to place k defect indicators among 50 independent trials. The product gives the probability of any one such arrangement, and multiplication by the count sums their identical probabilities. The 51 masses total 1.

At the observed count 6, b(6) ≈ .025989725. That is the probability of exactly six defects, not a p-value. A p-value must add all outcomes defined as at least as extreme by the chosen rule. The inclusive upper tail P₀(K ≥ 6) ≈ .037776173 contains b(6) and all larger counts.

Using K>6 instead would omit the observed count and give P₀(K ≥ 7) ≈ .011786448. The discrepancy is substantial because the count 6 has positive probability. In a continuous reference, an isolated point has zero probability; in a discrete reference, changing an inequality can materially change the answer.

The table shows k = 0–10 and aggregates 11–50 into one final row for readability; computation retains all 51 masses. The figure later uses the same aggregation, so no probability is silently discarded. Aggregating a remote tail is different from dropping it or treating distinct count values as equally likely.

Checkpoint: why does b(0) not equal zero under a 5% defect probability? Independent sampling can produce no defects even when the population probability is positive. The sampling law describes possible outcomes under the hypothesis, not a deterministic mapping from p₀ to observed proportion.

13Execute the declared central exact Binomial test

The primary two-sided p-value is pcentral = min{1,2 min[P₀(K ≤ k), P₀(K ≥ k)]}. Both tails are inclusive of the observed count. This doubled-smaller-tail construction is a valid conservative discrete p-value under the Binomial model. Capping at 1 is necessary because the overlapping inclusive tails can otherwise produce a doubled value greater than 1.

For k = 6, the upper tail is .037776173 and the lower tail P₀(K ≤ 6) = .988213552. The smaller is the upper tail, so pcentral = 2 × .037776173 = .075552346. At α = .05 the primary test does not reject H₀: p = .05.

This result does not say the true defect probability is 5%, that the observed 12% is acceptable or that there is only a 7.56% chance of a process problem. It says this particular two-sided central procedure does not cross its prespecified evidence threshold under the stated model. The effect estimate remains 7 percentage points above the benchmark.

The exact confidence interval developed below is wide and includes probabilities that could matter operationally. A nonrejection with such uncertainty is not proof of equivalence to the benchmark. If the purpose is to demonstrate that p lies below a maximum acceptable rate, a directional assurance question and interval bound would be more relevant than this equality test.

The declared convention is part of the method, not an optional reporting detail. Software may default to another valid two-sided rule. The correct workflow is to specify the rule, reproduce it explicitly and explain any comparison, rather than change the definition after discovering that another value falls below .05.

14Compare probability ordering without selecting it after the result

Another exact two-sided rule sums null masses no larger than the observed mass: porder = Σj: b(j) ≤ b(k)b(j). This is probability ordering. It regards outcomes that are individually at least as unlikely under the null as the observation, rather than doubling one tail. Inclusive equal masses must be retained.

For our k = 6, b(6) ≈ .025989725. Every j ≥ 6 has mass at most that value. None of j 0–5 does: even b(0) ≈ .076944975 is larger. Consequently porder = P₀(K ≥ 6) = .037776173, despite the method being called two-sided. A discrete skewed null need not contribute qualifying outcomes on both sides for every observed count.

This alternative rule rejects at .05 while the primary central rule does not. Both are mathematically valid under the Binomial model, but they allocate rejection probability differently. The discrepancy is not a rounding error and will not disappear by increasing numerical precision.

SciPy's binomtest two-sided calculation uses probability ordering, as its official implementation shows. Calling it and assuming it computes our central doubled tail would silently change the specified procedure. The public code therefore computes the central rule explicitly and labels the library result as a separate convention.

Do not select the rule because it gives the preferred decision. A prior protocol could reasonably choose a different exact method, but its interval construction and reporting should be named accordingly. We retain the central rule as primary so that its relation to the central exact interval is explicit.

15Translate exact p-values into count rejection regions

A rejection region tells us which future counts would trigger the fixed rule. Evaluate the central p-value for every k = 0–50 under p₀ = .05. At level .05, its rejection region is K ≥ 7. No low count is sufficiently extreme for this central rule at the chosen level: even K = 0 has doubled lower-tail value about .15389.

For the probability-ordering rule, the region is K ≥ 6. Under a true p₀ = .05 the actual central rejection probability is P₀(K ≥ 7) = .011786448; probability ordering attains P₀(K ≥ 6) = .037776173. Both are below .05. Exact means the finite reference and probabilities are used, not that every nominal α is attained exactly.

The primary observed count 6 lies just outside its rejection region. That is another way to verify the nonrejection from p .075552346. The alternative rule includes 6, explaining its different decision without treating the two p-values as interchangeable.

Discreteness can make a test conservative. Its available count probabilities cannot generally be split into exactly .05 by an ordinary nonrandomized rule. A smaller attained null size does not automatically make a procedure better; sensitivity to alternatives and the inferential convention also matter.

For an upper-direction test of p ≤ .05 against p>.05, the largest null tail occurs at the boundary .05 because larger p makes high counts more likely. That is a different prespecified question, even if its observed upper p happens to equal the probability-ordering p here. A numerical coincidence does not make directional and two-sided procedures identical.

16Evaluate the Normal score approximation using the null SE

The one-proportion score statistic uses the variance under the null:

Zscore = (p̂ − p₀)/√[p₀(1 − p₀)/n].

Here the null SE is√(.05 × .95/50) ≈ .030822070, so Zscore = (.12 − .05)/.030822070 ≈ 2.271099896. A two-sided Normal reference gives p ≈ .023140931. This is an approximation to a discrete sampling problem, not the exact central or probability-ordering value.

The null expected defect count is only 2.5, while expected nondefects are 47.5. The count distribution is skewed and concentrated near its lower boundary. This is a concrete warning about the Normal tail approximation. Rules requiring a certain expected count can be useful diagnostics, but no universal total-n threshold guarantees an accurate tail for all p₀ and designs.

A continuity correction can compare the inclusive upper event K ≥ 6 with a Normal boundary 5.5 rather than 6. Doubling that corrected upper tail gives about .051575864 here. This particular approximation changes its tail convention and still does not equal either exact two-sided method. It does not repair dependence or a wrong defect definition.

For testing p = p₀, replacing the null SE by√[p̂(1 − p̂)/n] produces a different Wald statistic. Its denominator is random and it has different approximation behavior. Do not mix that statistic with a score reference while describing the result as the same calculation.

Because exact Binomial summation is easy in this case, we use the declared exact method for the primary result. The Normal comparison teaches how approximations arise and where they can disagree; it is not an invitation to select whichever value is smallest.

The displayed score formula assumes 0<p₀<1. At a null boundary 0 or 1 its SE is zero and the ordinary standardized Normal argument is unavailable. The exact Binomial model can describe a degenerate boundary null, but that is not repaired by substituting a convenient nonzero denominator.

17Obtain the Wilson interval by inverting the score test

For candidate p values, the nonrejected score inequality is n(p̂ − p)² ≤ c²p(1 − p), where c = z.975. Expand and collect terms to obtain (n + c²)p² − (2 np̂ + c²)p + np̂² ≤ 0. The two roots define the Wilson interval.

Writing it as a center and margin, the center is[p̂ + c²/(2 n)]/[1 + c²/n], and the margin is c√[p̂(1 − p̂)/n + c²/(4 n²)]/[1 + c²/n]. Substituting p̂ = .12 and n = 50 gives approximately[.056176002,.238048174]. The null .05 lies below its lower endpoint, agreeing with the uncorrected two-sided score decision.

The interval is not simply p̂±a standard-error multiple centered at p̂. Inversion accounts for the candidate probability inside the null variance and keeps the bounds within[0,1]. It is a score-based approximate confidence construction, not an exact Binomial interval with a universal finite-sample guarantee.

For comparison, the ordinary Wald interval uses p̂±c√[p̂(1 − p̂)/n] and gives[.029926913,.210073087]. It includes .05. That difference does not imply a software error: the construction and variance treatment differ. Wald intervals can behave badly near 0 or 1 and can even leave the probability domain.

A reader should be able to name the interval method, not just its confidence level. A score p-value and Wilson interval share an inversion relationship; a score p-value and Wald interval do not automatically form that matched pair. Next we construct the central exact interval used with our primary Binomial convention.

18Construct the central exact interval from Binomial tails

For 0<k<n, a central 95% Clopper–Pearson interval chooses pL so that PpL(K ≥ k) = .025 and pU so that PpU(K ≤ k) = .025. These are equations in the candidate probability p, while the observed k and sample size remain fixed. The lower and upper bounds are not obtained by adding a single SE margin to p̂.

For k = 6, n = 50, solving the tail equations gives pL ≈ .045335321 and pU ≈ .243101317. The interval is about 4.53% to 24.31%. Relative to the 5% benchmark, the effect interval is approximately[ − .004664679,.193101317], or[ − .4665,19.3101]percentage points.

Because .05 belongs to this central interval, it agrees with the primary central two-sided p .075552346 nonrejection. More generally its tail inversion aligns with the doubled-smaller-tail convention, subject to consistent equality treatment at interval boundaries. Confidence-set endpoint inclusion and a test's strict/non-strict rejection convention must also be stated when exact boundary equality matters.

The word exact refers to finite Binomial tail probabilities and coverage of at least the nominal level under that model. Discreteness often makes coverage conservative, not exactly 95% for every true p. It does not mean the observed bounds have a 95% posterior probability of containing p or that the sampling assumptions have been validated.

Software may compute these bounds through equivalent Beta quantiles. That is a numerical representation of the same tail inversion, not an additional Bayesian assumption here. The code uses the library bounds and the independent verifier solves the tail equations directly.

The completed proportion results have several labels. Primary central exact p .075552346 and central exact 95% interval[.045335321,.243101317] agree about .05: no rejection. 20Interpret zero observed defects with a nonzero upper bound

Consider a different constructed inspection with 0 defects in 50 independent units. The estimate is p̂ = 0, but the population probability need not be 0. Under any positive p<1, the event K = 0 still has probability(1 − p)^50. No observed events is evidence about risk, not proof of its impossibility.

For a one-sided 95% exact upper bound U, solve PU(K = 0) = (1 − U)^50 = .05. Taking the 50 th root gives U = 1 − .05^(1/50) ≈ .058155079. A two-sided central 95% interval instead allocates .025 to that upper endpoint and gives[0,1 − .025^(1/50)] = [0,.071121736]. The confidence construction must be named.

21Count independent information rather than spreadsheet rows

Suppose the 16 mass readings actually came from 4 batches of 4 packages, with dependence within a batch. For equal cluster size m and common nonnegative within-cluster correlation ρ, independent clusters and equal individual variance, the mean variance is σ²[1 + (m − 1)ρ]/n. The factor DE = 1 + (m − 1)ρ measures variance inflation relative to independent rows under that model.

To see the source, sum n individual variances and n(m − 1) ordered within-cluster covariances, then divide by n². The effective-size shorthand neff = n/DE matches this variance to an independent-row variance. With n = 16, m = 4 and ρ = .4, DE 2.2 and neff ≈ 7.2727.

That shorthand is not permission to replace 16 by 7.2727 in a t test and invent a new exact degrees-of-freedom value. There may be only 4 independent batches, with a sampling and analysis structure that requires batch-level or model-based inference. The correct method depends on the actual design and target.

Similarly,50 inspections from 5 shared production runs need not follow a common-probability independent Binomial law. Correlated defects can produce a count distribution with more variability than Binomial. An exact Binomial calculation remains exact for its assumed model while becoming inappropriate for the real data-generating process.

If sampling without replacement from a small finite lot, the count can instead have a Hypergeometric reference. If a process changes over time, a single stable p or μ may be inadequate. Identify the population period, independent unit, selection mechanism and dependence before using any row count. More records cannot by themselves certify the standard error.

22Use a reproducible sequence for method selection and reporting

Begin with the parameter and unit: mean mass of independent packages, defect probability for a defined inspection event, or another target. Decide whether the comparison value is a fixed standard or an uncertain estimate. Record outcome units, sampling period, missing values and how independence is supported.

Write the null, alternative and α before computing. For means, determine whether σ is genuinely known, estimated from this sample or subject to another uncertainty model. For proportions, check the fixed-n binary/common-probability assumptions and whether a finite-lot or dependent design changes the reference. Define the two-sided rule for a discrete test.

Calculate an effect in the original scale, then its SE or exact count law. Produce a statistic, reference distribution, p-value and critical-region check. Construct a named confidence interval and verify its relationship to the chosen test. A mismatch should trigger a review of methods, tails and boundary conventions rather than automatic dismissal of the software.

Finally interpret magnitude, uncertainty and practical limits. A mean test is not individual-item certification; a defect nonrejection is not acceptance of a maximum-risk claim. A p-value is not a posterior probability of the null, and a confidence interval is not a prediction interval unless constructed as one.

Keep an audit trail of data corrections, sensitivity analyses and deviations from the protocol. Avoid selecting direction, exclusions or an exact convention because it produces a desired threshold crossing. A transparent report should allow another analyst to reconstruct both the arithmetic and the probability experiment that makes it meaningful.

A. Reproduce the primary mean test

Using n = 16, sum 8032 and centered SS 160, compute mean, sample variance, SE, t and the interpretation of p .027068490.

Complete worked solution

The mean is 8032/16 = 502 g and effect 502 − 500 = 2 g. Sample variance is 160/15 = 32/3 g²; s = 3.265986324 g. Dividing by√16 gives SE .816496581 g.

The unknown-scale statistic is 2/.816496581 = 2.449489743 with 15 degrees of freedom. Its two-sided t₁₅ tail is .027068490, below the declared .05. The critical magnitude 2.131449546 gives the same decision.

Under independent Normal sampling, this is evidence that the population mean differs from 500 g. It is not a probability that the null is true or proof that each package is overweight. J. Repair a complete process-quality report

An audit treats 16 masses from 4 shared runs as iid, uses a historical mean estimated from 8 units as an exact target, and inspects 50 units from 5 batches for 6 defects. It switches to the smaller exact p, uses a Wald interval, and calls nonrejection acceptance. Give a complete correction plan.

Complete worked solution

Start by separating the questions and units. A historical mean estimated from 8 observations is uncertain; comparing it with a current process mean is not automatically the one-sample fixed-standard problem. Recover its data, SD, sampling design and period. If it is actually an engineering specification rather than an estimate, state that clearly. A two-sample or another design-specific comparison may be needed.

The 16 masses share 4 runs. Audit within-run dependence, process stability and selection. Do not assume 16 independent measurements or repair the analysis merely by substituting an estimated neff. Independent run summaries or a suitable model may be appropriate, with substantial limitations from only 4 runs. Instrument and data corrections need documented evidence rather than deletion to obtain significance.

The defect count also shares 5 batches, so the independent common-p Binomial model is not established. Recover batch sizes, defect counts, selection and timing; decide whether the target is an overall unit probability, a batch-average probability or another quantity. A cluster-aware analysis requires its own assumptions. Fifty rows alone do not justify an exact Binomial guarantee.

Prespecify the inferential rule. A central doubled-tail p and probability-ordering p are different procedures; selecting the smaller after inspection is not a valid reconciliation. A Wald interval is not automatically dual to either exact rule and can behave badly near boundaries. If the independent Binomial design were justified, the central p and central exact interval would provide the matched analysis developed here; otherwise repair the reference first.

Report estimates, uncertainty, methods and operational limits. Nonrejection does not establish equality, acceptability or a maximum-risk guarantee. A quality acceptance decision needs a defined loss/tolerance criterion and evidence appropriate to that question, possibly an upper risk bound under a valid plan.

The next step is a documented sampling and comparison protocol with enough independent runs or batches, not another decimal from the existing invalid model. Preserve the original report as an audit trail, explain corrections, and distinguish computational reproducibility from scientific validity.

Before continuing, explain why the mean uses t when σ is unknown, why a central Binomial p can differ from a software default, why a named interval matters, and why zero events retain a positive risk bound. The final transfer problem requires repairing dependence and benchmark uncertainty rather than producing another decimal from an invalid model.

28Keep the reference vocabulary precise

The glossary summarizes distinctions already developed. In particular, sample SD, mean SE, exact probability mass, p-value, interval coverage and individual-item specification refer to different objects. A clear analysis names the object before interpreting its number.
