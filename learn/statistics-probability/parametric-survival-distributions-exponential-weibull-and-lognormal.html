Parametric Survival Distributions: Exponential, Weibull and Lognormal

Understand and verify lifetime distributions, density, survival, hazard, censoring, maximum likelihood and responsible model comparison.

Prerequisites: 21, 22, 32, 73, 74Review: 19Explicitly synthetic data
MODULE 01 / 25

1. Why model a lifetime distribution?

A reliability engineer wants more than a statement that the average component lasted twelve hundred operating hours. The useful questions are operational: What fraction is expected to survive a warranty period? When might failures accelerate? What is the conditional risk of failure among units that have already survived? What assumptions would be required to predict beyond the time actually observed? Parametric survival analysis answers these questions by specifying a probability distribution for the time to an event and estimating its parameters from observed and censored durations.

This guide builds three common models from the ground up: Exponential, Weibull, and Lognormal. They are not interchangeable names for the same curve. The Exponential model has a constant hazard; Weibull can have increasing, decreasing, or constant hazard; Lognormal can produce a hazard that rises and eventually falls. Each model embodies a scientific assertion about the pattern of failure over time. Choosing the shape is therefore an interpretive decision, not merely selecting whichever software output has the smallest number.

Our recurring example concerns identical hypothetical electronic modules followed from the beginning of operation until first failure or administrative end of observation. All eight records are synthetic, constructed for instruction; they are not a manufacturer's field data. Time is measured in units of 100 operating hours, so a recorded 4 means 400 operating hours. A failure is observed with event indicator 1, and right censoring is encoded 0. Our limited exercise is not a reliability qualification, a warranty recommendation, or evidence that a specific supplier is safer.

By the end you should reconstruct the link among density, survival, and hazard; derive the three models' relevant formulas; form a likelihood that uses censored observations correctly; reproduce an Exponential maximum likelihood estimate; and explain why fit in observed data does not by itself justify remote extrapolation. Prerequisites are Guides 21, 22, 32, 73, and 74; Guide 19 is an optional review. We revisit their key ideas here so the calculations remain independently readable.

MODULE 02 / 25

2. A coherent clock, event, and data table

In survival analysis the most consequential definition may be the least mathematical one: time zero. For our modules it means the first hour of operation under the monitored test conditions, not the date when a unit arrived at a warehouse. The endpoint is first functional failure under the stated testing definition. A unit removed while functioning at the planned close of observation remains at risk of later failure; its observed record is incomplete, not a confirmed long life.

Let \(T\) be the true nonnegative lifetime, \(C\) the censoring time, \(Y=\min(T,C)\) the recorded duration, and \(\delta=I(T\le C)\) the event indicator. The event is identified when \(\delta=1\). Under \(\delta=0\), we only know \(T>Y\) (for continuous models the distinction between > and ≥ at an exact point is immaterial). If a machine stops its test at 700 hours without failure, saying that its lifetime was 700 hours would be wrong; its lifetime is at least that long.

These eight rows are the complete instructional dataset, not an excerpt. There are five observed failures and three right-censored records. The observed follow-up totals \(4+7+9+11+13+16+19+22=101\) units of 100 h, or 10,100 accumulated module-hours. Do not report 101 as the average lifetime: it is the total exposure actually observed. The mean observed follow-up of \(101/8=12.625\) hundred hours also is not a consistent estimator of true mean lifetime under general censoring.

The likelihood examples assume independent modules drawn from one common lifetime distribution and noninformative censoring. Administrative censoring is plausibly noninformative when scheduling is set independently of latent weakness, but removing hot or noisy units early could violate that assumption. These assumptions affect the validity of all three fits; they are not corrected automatically by choosing the most flexible curve.

MODULE 03 / 25

3. Density, survival, and hazard are three views of one model

Before naming a distribution, define the questions precisely. The cumulative distribution function \(F(t)=P(T\le t)\) is the probability that failure has occurred by time \(t\). The Survival Function \(S(t)=P(T>t)=1-F(t)\) is the probability of remaining operational beyond \(t\). Because \(T\ge0\), a proper continuous lifetime model generally has \(S(0)=1\), decreases or remains flat with time, and approaches zero if eventual failure occurs with probability one.

The Probability Density Function, denoted \(f(t)\), is the derivative of \(F(t)\). It is not the probability of failing at an exact instant, which is zero under a continuous distribution. Rather, \(f(t)\,\Delta t\) approximately describes the probability of failure in a short interval of width \(\Delta t\), and its units are inverse time. Density values may exceed one when measurement units or concentration permit, without violating probability: the integral, not each individual density height, must equal one.

The Hazard Function asks a conditional question: among units that have survived until just before \(t\), at what instantaneous rate are failures occurring? The distinction between population density and conditional hazard is essential because the fraction of units still at risk changes over time.

KEY EQUATION
\[f(t)=-\frac{dS(t)}{dt},\qquad h(t)=\frac{f(t)}{S(t)},\qquad S(t)=\exp\!\left[-\int_0^t h(u)\,du\right].\]

The integral \(H(t)=\int_0^t h(u)du\) is the cumulative hazard. It is dimensionless because the hazard has inverse-time units and \(du\) has time units. Thus \(H(t)=-\log S(t)\). We can derive it rather than memorize it: \(h(t)=-S'(t)/S(t)=-\frac{d}{dt}\log S(t)\); integrating from 0 to \(t\) and using \(S(0)=1\) gives \(\log S(t)=-H(t)\). The baseline calculus assumption is differentiability at the relevant points.

A compact sanity check: if \(S(10)=0.8\), then \(F(10)=0.2\); this does not imply that \(h(10)=0.2\). To calculate hazard we also need local density. With \(f(10)=0.04\) per hundred hours, \(h(10)=0.04/0.8=0.05\) per hundred hours. A 0.1-hundred-hour interval then has approximately \(0.05\times0.1=0.005\), or 0.5%, conditional failure probability, not a 5% probability at a single instant.

MODULE 04 / 25

4. Exponential: what constant hazard means

The Exponential distribution begins with one parameter: a rate \(\lambda>0\) whose unit is inverse time. Its hazard does not depend on how long a unit has operated. The model says that, conditional on surviving until hour 500, its instantaneous failure tendency is the same as it was at hour 100. This is a strong idealization suitable in some random-shock settings; it is not a universal law of aging equipment.

KEY EQUATION
\[T\sim\mathrm{Exponential}(\lambda),\quad S(t)=e^{-\lambda t},\quad f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t},\quad h(t)=\lambda,\quad t\ge0.\]

To verify the formulas, start with the proposed constant hazard \(h(t)=\lambda\). Integrating gives \(H(t)=\lambda t\); applying \(S=e^{-H}\) gives \(S(t)=e^{-\lambda t}\); differentiating \(-S'(t)\) gives \(f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\). The PDF integrates to \([ -e^{-\lambda t}]_0^\infty=1\). These operations make the formulas a coherent probability model, not independent rules to memorize.

A second common parameterization uses the scale \(\theta=1/\lambda>0\) in time units. Authors may write Exponential(rate=0.05) or Exponential(scale=20) for the same distribution when time is counted in hundreds of hours. Always read documentation: a parameter called \(\lambda\) in one book may be the scale in another. Under the rate parameterization, the mean and standard deviation are both \(1/\lambda\), and the median is \(\log(2)/\lambda\).

Suppose \(\lambda=0.05\) per hundred hours. At 1,000 hours, \(t=10\), so \(S(10)=e^{-0.05(10)}=e^{-0.5}\approx0.6065\). Therefore the probability of a failure by 1,000 hours is \(1-0.6065=0.3935\). The model predicts mean lifetime \(20\) hundred hours, or 2,000 hours, and median \(13.8629\) hundred hours, or approximately 1,386 hours. The mean exceeds the median because the distribution is right-skewed.

Interpretation check: the 0.05 hazard has units per hundred operating hours. It cannot be read as a guaranteed 5% failure probability for every 100-hour interval. The exact interval probability conditional on survival is \(1-e^{-0.05}\approx0.04877\); 0.05 is a small-interval approximation. Differences grow when hazards or intervals are larger.

MODULE 05 / 25

5. The memoryless property: a theorem with practical limitations

A defining consequence of Exponential lifetimes is memorylessness: conditional on survival to \(s\), the remaining waiting time has the same Exponential distribution as the original lifetime. This describes a mathematical distribution, not the actual absence of physical wear. Engineers should investigate whether their failure mechanism makes it plausible.

KEY EQUATION
\[P(T>s+t\mid T>s)=\frac{S(s+t)}{S(s)}=\frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}=e^{-\lambda t}=P(T>t).\]

Every equality has a reason. The first uses the conditional probability rule and the fact that \(\{T>s+t\}\) is a subset of \(\{T>s\}\). The second substitutes the Exponential survival function. The third cancels \(e^{-\lambda s}\). The final expression no longer involves the elapsed age \(s\). We have therefore proved the memoryless property rather than asserting it by analogy.

For \(\lambda=0.05\), a unit already functioning at 1,000 hours has probability \(e^{-0.05(5)}\approx0.7788\) of surviving another 500 hours. The same conditional probability applies to a newly installed unit surviving its first 500 hours. This is counterintuitive when bearings wear out; it may be more plausible when failures occur from age-independent shocks and the environment remains stable.

This theorem also offers a diagnostic intuition. If an empirical hazard appears to rise with elapsed operating hours, imposing a memoryless law will underrepresent late deterioration and may overrepresent early risk. If hazard falls because fragile modules fail early while robust modules persist, a constant hazard hides early selection. Note that unobserved heterogeneity can create decreasing population hazard even when individual hazard mechanisms differ; choosing a curve does not establish the underlying physical mechanism.

Transfer question: would memorylessness be reasonable for lightbulb burn-out, a fresh manufacturing defect, or random power surges? There is no universal answer. One must specify the mechanism, exposure, stress, maintenance, and definition of first failure. Statistical compatibility alone does not validate the physical claim.

MODULE 06 / 25

6. Weibull: one parameter changes the hazard shape

The Weibull distribution adds a shape parameter \(k>0\) to a positive scale parameter \(\eta>0\). Here \(\eta\) has the same time unit as \(T\); \(k\) is dimensionless. We use the common scale-and-shape convention, not an alternative convention in which an inverse scale or powered rate appears. The distribution is supported on nonnegative time.

KEY EQUATION
\[S(t)=\exp\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^k\right],\quad f(t)=\frac{k}{\eta}\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{k-1}\exp\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^k\right].\]

Divide the density by survival: the common exponential factor cancels. This yields the hazard, and integration yields the cumulative hazard. Presenting the cancellation is important: it explains why a Weibull hazard is a power of time rather than merely asking us to accept a formula.

KEY EQUATION
\[h(t)=\frac{k}{\eta}\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{k-1},\qquad H(t)=\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^k.\]

The power \(k-1\) controls the direction. If \(k=1\), the hazard is constant at \(1/\eta\); Weibull is then exactly Exponential with rate \(\lambda=1/\eta\). If \(k>1\), hazard increases with age; when \(0<k<1\), hazard decreases. The scale \(\eta\) is the time when \(H(\eta)=1\), and thus \(S(\eta)=e^{-1}\approx0.3679\); it is not generally the mean or median.

Take \(\eta=12\) hundred hours. With \(k=2\), the hazard at \(t=6\) is \((2/12)(6/12)=0.08333\) per hundred hours; at \(t=18\), it is \((2/12)(18/12)=0.25\). The second hazard is three times the first because the operating age tripled, not because the probability of failure at the exact instant became 25%. At \(t=12\), survival is \(e^{-1}\) regardless of \(k\); at \(t=6\), survival is \(e^{-(1/2)^2}=e^{-0.25}\approx0.7788\).

For \(k=0.7\), the exponent \(k-1=-0.3\) makes hazard decline with time. This can represent early failures that taper off, but it must not be casually taken as proof of 'burn-in': inspection practices, population mixtures, and censoring may produce similar-looking patterns. Weibull is related to—but is not generally identical to—a Gamma distribution; confusing these families leads to incorrect formulas.

MODULE 07 / 25

7. Weibull quantiles, expectation, and conditional reliability

A reliability prediction is often most useful when translated into a calendar threshold. Define the \(p\)-quantile \(t_p\) by \(P(T\le t_p)=p\), or equivalently \(S(t_p)=1-p\). For Weibull, take logarithms carefully rather than guessing: \(1-p=e^{-(t_p/\eta)^k}\); then \(-\log(1-p)=(t_p/\eta)^k\); finally take the positive \(k\)th root.

KEY EQUATION
\[t_p=\eta[-\log(1-p)]^{1/k},\qquad t_{0.5}=\eta(\log2)^{1/k}.\]

For \(k=2\), \(\eta=12\), the median is \(12\sqrt{\log2}=12(0.83255)=9.9906\) hundred hours, approximately 999 hours. The 90th percentile is \(12\sqrt{-\log0.10}=12(1.51743)=18.209\) hundred hours. Hence approximately 90% of modeled lifetimes end by 1,821 hours; phrasing it as '90% survive' would reverse the meaning of a failure-time percentile.

The mean Weibull lifetime requires the Gamma function (which is not the same thing as saying Weibull has a Gamma distribution): \(E[T]=\eta\Gamma(1+1/k)\). For \(k=2\), \(\Gamma(1.5)=\sqrt\pi/2\approx0.886227\), giving mean \(12(0.886227)=10.6347\) hundred hours. This mean is distinct from both the scale 12 and median 9.9906. The Gamma-function identity can be proved by the substitution \(u=(t/\eta)^k\) in the expectation integral; we use the standard named integral \(\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty u^{a-1}e^{-u}du\).

Suppose a unit has already survived to \(s=8\) hundred hours and management asks about survival until \(s+t=12\). The conditional probability is \(S(12)/S(8)=\exp[-(12/12)^2+(8/12)^2]=e^{-1+4/9}\approx0.5738\). It differs from the probability that a new unit survives its first 4 hundred hours, \(S(4)=e^{-1/9}\approx0.8948\). The growing Weibull hazard makes survival over the next 400 hours harder for an older unit. Exponential would give the same conditional probability regardless of age; this numerical contrast makes the modeling assumption tangible.

MODULE 08 / 25

8. Lognormal: normality belongs to log time

An Exponential lifetime begins at zero and decreases in density immediately; a Weibull model may emphasize early or later failures through its power-law hazard. A different story arises when many positive multiplicative factors—material tolerances, load, temperature history—combine to influence lifetime. A Lognormal model treats the logarithm of lifetime as normally distributed. This is a distributional assumption that must be checked, not a claim that those factors have been measured or proven causal.

KEY EQUATION
\[\log T\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),\qquad T=e^Z,\qquad Z\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),\quad \sigma>0.\]

The parameter \(\mu\) is the mean of \(\log T\) in a chosen time unit; \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of log time and is dimensionless once a reference unit is fixed. Logarithms of dimensional quantities require care: strictly we take the log of a numerical value relative to the chosen unit, such as \(\log(T/(100\,\mathrm{h}))\). Changing from hundreds of hours to hours shifts \(\mu\) by \(\log100\), while leaving \(\sigma\) unchanged. The parameters therefore need a declared measurement convention.

Let \(z(t)=(\log t-\mu)/\sigma\) for \(t>0\), and let \(\phi\) and \(\Phi\) denote the standard Normal PDF and CDF. Since \(P(T\le t)=P(\log T\le\log t)=\Phi(z(t))\), survival is \(1-\Phi(z(t))\). The PDF also needs the Jacobian \(d(\log t)/dt=1/t\); omitting it is a common error in transformations.

KEY EQUATION
\[f(t)=\frac{1}{t\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac{(\log t-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],\quad S(t)=1-\Phi\left(\frac{\log t-\mu}{\sigma}\right).\]

The mean is \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\), the median is \(e^\mu\), and the variance is \((e^{\sigma^2}-1)e^{2\mu+\sigma^2}\). These formulas describe time, not log time. Because \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}>e^\mu\) for \(\sigma>0\), the mean exceeds the median. A Normal approximation directly applied to \(T\) could predict negative lifetimes; Lognormal avoids that because exponentials are positive.

MODULE 09 / 25

9. Lognormal hazard and its non-monotone interpretation

Lognormal hazard is obtained by dividing its density by survival. Both parts matter: although the density eventually becomes small, the survivor group also becomes increasingly small. We must analyze their ratio, rather than assume that a right-skewed density automatically gives a rising hazard.

KEY EQUATION
\[h(t)=\frac{\phi(z(t))}{t\sigma[1-\Phi(z(t))]},\qquad z(t)=\frac{\log t-\mu}{\sigma}.\]

At very early positive times, hazard approaches zero; the density assigns almost no failures arbitrarily close to time zero. As time increases, hazard rises and reaches an internal maximum, then eventually declines toward zero in a standard Lognormal model. This is a non-monotone hazard. The maximum's location depends on \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\); do not confuse the density's mode, \(e^{\mu-\sigma^2}\), with the hazard's peak. They solve different optimization problems.

For an illustrative Lognormal distribution with \(\mu=\log12\approx2.48491\) and \(\sigma=0.5\), the median is 12 hundred hours, and the mean is \(12e^{0.125}\approx13.598\) hundred hours. At 12 hundred hours, \(z=0\); \(\phi(0)=1/\sqrt{2\pi}\approx0.39894\), and \(S(12)=0.5\). Therefore \(h(12)=0.39894/[12(0.5)(0.5)]=0.13298\) per hundred hours. This is a rate among survivors, not 13.3% of all original units failing precisely at that moment.

For a 90% failure-time quantile, use \(t_{0.9}=e^{\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(0.9)}\). Taking \(\Phi^{-1}(0.9)\approx1.28155\), obtain \(t_{0.9}=12e^{0.640775}\approx22.776\) hundred hours, about 2,278 hours. By construction \(S(t_{0.9})\approx0.1\). In general, Lognormal quantiles satisfy the formula below, which can be checked by substituting into the standardization step.

KEY EQUATION
\[t_p=\exp\left[\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)\right],\qquad 0<p<1.\]

The hazard eventually decreasing does not mean old components become physically rejuvenated. A mixture of different strengths can leave a selected cohort of robust survivors; any mechanistic interpretation requires independent evidence. Extrapolating this late decline beyond the observed maximum time is especially hazardous when few units remain under observation.

MODULE 10 / 25

10. Right censoring: what each observation contributes

A survival likelihood must encode what was actually observed, not what we wish we had seen. If a component fails at time \(t\), the model's density \(f(t)\) supplies the likelihood contribution, up to factors describing measurement resolution. If the component is still working when observation stops at \(c\), all we learn is \(T>c\), so its contribution is the survival probability \(S(c)\). Substituting \(f(c)\) would falsely treat an administrative stop as a breakdown. Coding it as 'no failure ever' would falsely supply infinite follow-up.

KEY EQUATION
\[L_i(\vartheta)=f(y_i;\vartheta)^{\delta_i}S(y_i;\vartheta)^{1-\delta_i}.\]

Here \(\vartheta\) denotes all model parameters—one for Exponential, two for Weibull, and two for Lognormal. The exponents are simply an economical indicator switch. When \(\delta_i=1\), the survival factor becomes one; when \(\delta_i=0\), the density factor becomes one. Independent observations contribute multiplicatively, giving the full likelihood \(L(\vartheta)=\prod_{i=1}^nL_i(\vartheta)\). We are using the conventional right-censoring likelihood conditional on censoring mechanisms whose relevant factors do not depend on the lifetime-model parameters.

Use our eight complete records. Unit A failed at 4, so contributes \(f(4)\); unit B stopped while working at 7, so contributes \(S(7)\). The sequence is

KEY EQUATION
\[L=f(4)S(7)f(9)f(11)S(13)f(16)S(19)f(22).\]

This formula applies to any of the three continuous families after using the corresponding expressions for \(f\) and \(S\). Observe that censored units are not discarded; they contribute information about how much survival probability should remain beyond their observation times. They often contribute strongly to scale estimation.

Two assumptions require examination. First, the status and timing must be recorded correctly: repairs, intermittent failures, delayed test starts, and interval-only failure detection alter the observation model. Second, noninformative censoring is not guaranteed simply because the dataset has a column called censored. If weaker units are removed systematically, the survival times of remaining units are selected. The formulas shown here do not solve competing risks, left truncation, or interval censoring; those require appropriately changed likelihoods.

MODULE 11 / 25

11. Exponential likelihood: every arithmetic step

For Exponential rate \(\lambda\), the event contribution is \(f(y)=\lambda e^{-\lambda y}\) and the censored contribution is \(S(y)=e^{-\lambda y}\). The combined contribution is \(\lambda^{\delta}e^{-\lambda y}\). Multiplying over the eight modules collects one factor of \(\lambda\) per observed failure, and an exponential factor for every unit of observed follow-up—including censored exposure.

KEY EQUATION
\[L(\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{8}\lambda^{\delta_i}e^{-\lambda y_i}=\lambda^{\sum_i\delta_i}\exp\left(-\lambda\sum_i y_i\right).\]

Compute the two sufficient statistics directly from the data table: \(d=1+0+1+1+0+1+0+1=5\) failures, and \(E=4+7+9+11+13+16+19+22=101\) hundreds of operating hours. No hidden records enter these totals. The likelihood becomes \(L(\lambda)=\lambda^5e^{-101\lambda}\), with \(\lambda>0\) measured per 100 operating hours. Its logarithm is easier to differentiate: \(\ell(\lambda)=5\log\lambda-101\lambda\).

KEY EQUATION
\[\frac{d\ell}{d\lambda}=\frac{5}{\lambda}-101=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\widehat\lambda=\frac{5}{101}=0.04950495\ \text{per 100 h}.\]

The second derivative is \(d^2\ell/d\lambda^2=-5/\lambda^2<0\), confirming that the stationary point is a maximum for \(\lambda>0\). The corresponding estimated scale or mean is \(\widehat\theta=1/\widehat\lambda=101/5=20.2\) hundred hours, or 2,020 operating hours. Notice the denominator: it is total observed exposure, not eight customers, five failures, or the sum of failure times only. Censored follow-up informs the estimate even though its true endpoint is unknown.

At 1,000 hours, substitute \(t=10\) in estimated survival: \(\widehat S(10)=e^{-(5/101)10}=e^{-50/101}\approx0.6095\). The estimated probability of failure by that time is \(1-0.6095\approx0.3905\). This calculation is a model-based estimate under homogeneous Exponential lifetimes and noninformative censoring; it is not the observed fraction 5/8. Confusing a probability at a horizon with the overall sample event proportion discards the information about follow-up duration.

The log-likelihood at the optimum is \(\ell(\widehat\lambda)=5\log(5/101)-5\approx-20.028\). The exact expression is preferable to an unnecessarily precise decimal because the dataset is tiny and synthetic. We can independently check the score numerically: \(5/(5/101)-101=101-101=0\). We can also test a nearby rate: differentiating shows the log likelihood is increasing below the optimum and decreasing above it. These are mathematical checks of optimization, not evidence that Exponential is the true distribution.

MODULE 12 / 25

12. Why discarding censored rows produces a wrong estimate

A tempting shortcut is to keep only the five failed modules, compute their mean failure time, and estimate the Exponential rate as its reciprocal. This procedure silently changes the sampling scheme: it treats the surviving units as if they never participated in the experiment. The remaining observed failure times are 4, 9, 11, 16, and 22 hundreds of hours, summing to 62. The resulting shortcut would be \(5/62=0.080645\) per hundred hours, substantially above the valid censored likelihood estimate \(5/101=0.049505\).

The discrepancy is not a computational quirk. The censored modules contribute 7+13+19=39 additional hundred-module-hours during which no recorded failure occurred. Failing to count that exposure inflates the estimated instantaneous failure rate. Conversely, replacing each censored lifetime with its censoring time as an observed failure would create eight fictitious failures and give \(8/101=0.079208\). Different mistakes can lead to similar-looking wrong estimates, so checking only the numerical magnitude is insufficient.

Reasoning checkpoint: Why not simply impute a very long lifetime for each censored unit? Because Right Censoring identifies only a lower bound, not a valid fixed lifetime. Imputation would require a specified model and uncertainty treatment, and arbitrary values would bias the distribution toward whichever tail was chosen. The likelihood \(S(c)\) handles the observed information without inventing a precise unobserved failure time.

If no failures were observed, \(d=0\) and the unconstrained Exponential MLE would move to the boundary \(\lambda=0\), indicating that the data alone do not identify a finite mean failure time by the usual interior estimate. This edge case is pedagogically important: a numerical optimizer returning a tiny positive rate does not convert absent failure events into strong evidence of infinite reliability. Additional design information, longer follow-up, or specialized interval inference would be needed.

The problem is also not solved by comparing only average observed durations across groups. With different censoring schedules, one group may exhibit longer recorded follow-up simply because it was monitored for longer. Survival analysis defines what each row tells us, rather than treating all record endings as identical events.

MODULE 13 / 25

13. General log-likelihood: stable arithmetic, same scientific question

Multiplying eight small densities and survival probabilities is already inconvenient; multiplying thousands may produce numerical underflow. Taking natural logarithms converts a product into a sum, while preserving the parameter value that maximizes likelihood, because logarithm is strictly increasing on positive values. The transformation is not an arbitrary statistical ritual: it makes optimization practical and allows each event and censoring contribution to be inspected.

KEY EQUATION
\[\ell(\vartheta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\left[\delta_i\log f(y_i;\vartheta)+(1-\delta_i)\log S(y_i;\vartheta)\right].\]

The unit of density requires care when interpreting numerical likelihood values. Changing time units multiplies each failure density by a Jacobian, shifting absolute log-likelihood by a constant if the change is applied consistently. Therefore compare log-likelihood or AIC only for models fitted to the same outcome measurements, observations, censoring definitions, and likelihood conventions. A lower negative log-likelihood is better in sample, but it cannot on its own establish that the model predicts new data or has a meaningful causal mechanism.

For reproducibility, a stable numerical implementation evaluates log survival directly when possible. For Lognormal at extremely large \(z\), the naive difference \(1-\Phi(z)\) may underflow to zero because \(\Phi(z)\) rounds to one; numerical libraries provide a survival function or log-survival function that avoids cancellation. Similarly, optimization should enforce positive shape and scale parameters. One practical strategy is to optimize \(\log k\), \(\log\eta\), or \(\log\sigma\), and exponentiate to recover valid positive parameters.

An MLE is the value that maximizes the specified likelihood, subject to its model and observation assumptions. It is not the same as estimating the true failure mechanism without assumptions. With eight records, estimates of a two-parameter family can be unstable and uncertainty wide. A reported optimum should be accompanied by information about model adequacy, plausible parameters, sample size, and what the observed time range can actually support.

MODULE 14 / 25

14. Weibull censored likelihood and profile of the shape

For Weibull with shape \(k\) and scale \(\eta\), every observation contributes \(S(y)=\exp[-(y/\eta)^k]\) and every failure adds the hazard factor \(h(y)=(k/\eta)(y/\eta)^{k-1}\). This factorization follows from \(f=hS\). It is valuable because it shows precisely how failure ages help determine shape, while all observed time contributes cumulative hazard.

KEY EQUATION
\[\ell(k,\eta)=d\log k-dk\log\eta+(k-1)\sum_{\delta_i=1}\log y_i-\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i/\eta)^k.\]

Check dimensions before reading this as a formula: logs of time values refer to time measured relative to the declared unit, and the ratio \(y_i/\eta\) is dimensionless. The first three terms arise from the five observed event densities; the last term includes all eight records. Replacing the last sum with failure records only would again throw away censoring information.

For a fixed \(k\), differentiate with respect to \(\eta\). The derivative is \(-dk/\eta+k\sum_i y_i^k/\eta^{k+1}\). Setting it to zero gives an exact conditional scale estimate. This is a useful reduction: instead of maximizing freely over two positive parameters, we can substitute the estimated scale for each candidate shape and maximize a one-dimensional profile likelihood.

KEY EQUATION
\[\widehat\eta(k)^k=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^k}{d},\qquad \widehat\eta(k)=\left(\frac{\sum_i y_i^k}{d}\right)^{1/k}.\]

As a verification, set \(k=1\). Then \(\widehat\eta(1)=\sum_i y_i/d=101/5=20.2\) hundreds of hours, exactly the Exponential scale estimate. This is not an accidental similarity; Exponential is the Weibull submodel with \(k=1\). At \(k=2\), the squared times sum to \(4^2+7^2+9^2+11^2+13^2+16^2+19^2+22^2=1537\). The conditional estimate is \(\widehat\eta(2)=\sqrt{1537/5}=\sqrt{307.4}\approx17.533\). That is a conditional estimate given shape 2, not necessarily the joint MLE.

To estimate both parameters, vary \(k>0\), recompute \(\widehat\eta(k)\), and find the value that maximizes the profile. An optimization routine must specify starting conditions and precision; we report numerical values as numerical results, not closed-form solutions. Because this dataset has only five observed failures, the uncertainty around \(k\) can be substantial. The estimate should not be translated into a confident assertion about wear-out mechanisms without model checks and engineering context.

MODULE 15 / 25

15. Lognormal censored likelihood and numerical evaluation

Lognormal uses \(\mu\) and \(\sigma>0\), with \(z_i=(\log y_i-\mu)/\sigma\). A failed module contributes the log-density \(-\log y_i-\log\sigma-\frac12\log(2\pi)-\frac12z_i^2\). A censored module contributes \(\log[1-\Phi(z_i)]\). Both groups therefore inform the fitted parameters, but in different ways. Unlike the Exponential MLE, the simultaneous estimates do not generally reduce to a simple ratio of two dataset totals.

KEY EQUATION
\[\ell(\mu,\sigma)=\sum_{\delta_i=1}\left[-\log(y_i\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})-\frac12z_i^2\right]+\sum_{\delta_i=0}\log[1-\Phi(z_i)].\]

An instructive cross-check is the case with no censoring. If every \(\delta_i=1\), maximizing this likelihood is equivalent to fitting a Normal model to \(x_i=\log y_i\), with \(\widehat\mu=n^{-1}\sum x_i\) and \(\widehat\sigma^2=n^{-1}\sum(x_i-\widehat\mu)^2\). The variance denominator \(n\), rather than \(n-1\), is appropriate for the Normal maximum likelihood estimator. As soon as censoring appears, directly taking the mean and variance of observed log durations is no longer the correct MLE, because censored records represent lower bounds.

For the eight instructional records, the numerical fit is obtained by minimizing negative log-likelihood using the complete table. One parameter can be unconstrained, \(\mu\), while positivity of \(\sigma\) can be enforced by estimating \(q=\log\sigma\) and recovering \(\sigma=e^q\). The computation uses the Normal log-survival function for censored observations, avoiding catastrophic cancellation for extreme right tails.

A one-row audit makes the distinction concrete. Unit A fails at \(y=4\), so its term is \(-\log[4\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}]-(\log4-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)\). Unit B is censored at \(y=7\), so its term is \(\log\{1-\Phi[(\log7-\mu)/\sigma]\}\). Notice that B contributes neither a Normal PDF nor a zero residual; it contributes a right-tail probability. Numerical solvers find a plausible optimum only if these terms match what was observed.

The resulting fitted \(\mu\) depends on whether time was coded as hours or hundreds of hours; corresponding survival predictions at physical times should not. Checking unit invariance is a powerful independent test of an implementation. An optimizer returning parameters without a convergence status, starting-point check, or likelihood value is insufficient documentation for a high-stakes analysis.

16. The three numerical fits: what they can and cannot tell us

We now compare fits to exactly the same eight observations, with the same event indicators and time units. Exponential has one free parameter; Weibull and Lognormal have two each. Numerical fits should be seen as conditional calculations under competing models, not as evidence that synthetic data reveal a real physical degradation law. The following values are computed from the displayed right-censored likelihoods; differences in last digits can occur across optimizers and tolerances.

The maximum log-likelihood \(\widehat\ell\) describes how well each distribution fits the recorded event times and censoring information under its own assumptions. With more parameters a model can often adapt more closely, which is why we also report AIC, the Akaike Information Criterion: \(\mathrm{AIC}=2p-2\widehat\ell\), where \(p\) is the number of estimated parameters. Lower AIC is preferred among models fitted on comparable likelihoods; it is not an absolute certificate of truth, good calibration, or successful extrapolation.

The model parameters have different interpretations: Exponential rate is an inverse-time hazard, Weibull shape describes hazard direction while scale controls time, and Lognormal \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) describe normality of the log-time variable. A smaller AIC difference in this eight-record dataset is not a decisive scientific finding. Any model selection claim should discuss how many failures were observed and whether the differences would be robust to new observations or alternative censoring schedules.

We should also inspect the modeled survival at a meaningful horizon, say 1,200 hours (\(t=12\)). Use the fitted parameters from the table to compute \(\widehat S_E(12)=e^{-12\widehat\lambda}\), \(\widehat S_W(12)=e^{-(12/\widehat\eta)^{\widehat k}}\), and \(\widehat S_L(12)=1-\Phi[(\log12-\widehat\mu)/\widehat\sigma]\). Even if these predictions are close at 1,200 hours, their implications at 5,000 hours may diverge because their tails differ. That divergence is precisely why fitted families should be considered scientific assumptions rather than interchangeable interpolation devices.

MODULE 17 / 25

17. Read the curves together, not one at a time

The three essential plots answer different questions and must be interpreted side by side. A density plot shows where failure times are concentrated among the original population. A survival plot shows the fraction modeled to persist past each time. A hazard plot shows the instantaneous failure rate conditional on having reached that time. One curve cannot substitute for the others. A density peak, for example, is not in general the same time as peak hazard, because the denominator \(S(t)\) changes.

Read the horizontal axis as operating time measured in hundreds of hours, not module IDs. Read the vertical axes with their own units: density and hazard have units per 100 hours; survival is a unitless probability between zero and one. The plots use illustrative fixed parameters selected to demonstrate shape differences, not the fitted parameter estimates from the tiny dataset. This separation matters: a pedagogical visual comparison of possible shapes is not evidence that any one shape has been empirically established.

At the same horizon, a survival curve higher on the plot represents greater predicted probability of still functioning. Curves can cross, so a model with greater survival at an early time may have lower survival at a later time. This fact discourages vague claims such as 'Weibull is always safer' or 'Lognormal is always more reliable.' A model family is not a product or a treatment; reliability depends on estimated parameters and the defined conditions.

The hazard comparison is particularly informative: the Exponential line is flat; Weibull with shape greater than one rises; Lognormal can climb and later decline. A rising hazard does not mean that survival increases, because \(S(t)\) never increases for a proper lifetime distribution. A declining hazard means that the instantaneous conditional failure rate is decreasing, not that failed units are coming back to life. The survival curve continues to decrease whenever hazard is positive.

For reading practice, choose a time on the vertical grid, describe the density, survival, and hazard of one model at that same time, and explain why the three numbers answer different questions. Then repeat at a later time and identify which claims are about an absolute failure probability and which are conditional rates. This practice converts visual design into reasoning, rather than merely decorating the text with charts.

MODULE 18 / 25

18. Empirical checkpoints and model adequacy

A fitted parametric curve should be evaluated against the observed pattern before it informs a decision. The Kaplan–Meier estimator supplies a nonparametric summary of survival under appropriate independent censoring assumptions. It does not assert a specific functional shape for the hazard. Comparing parametric survival predictions with the Kaplan–Meier steps over the range with adequate risk-set support can reveal systematic disagreements. In a sample of eight, apparent agreement or disagreement is noisy and should be treated as illustrative rather than conclusive.

For this dataset, event times are 4, 9, 11, 16, and 22. Immediately before these times, the risk sets contain 8, 6, 5, 3, and 1 observed-at-risk modules respectively. The Kaplan–Meier factors are therefore \(7/8\), \(5/6\), \(4/5\), \(2/3\), and \(0/1\). Multiplying through the fourth event gives \((7/8)(5/6)(4/5)(2/3)=7/18\approx0.3889\) after time 16; the fifth event drops the estimate to zero because the last observed module fails at 22. That final zero is a property of this tiny observed risk set, not proof that no real module can survive past 2,200 hours.

A model with a constant Exponential hazard might be challenged if observed failures appear clustered at high ages, whereas decreasing early hazard may motivate a Weibull shape below one. However, informal graphical impressions cannot identify hazard shape reliably with only five failures. Confidence intervals, parametric bootstrap checks, domain knowledge, and sensitivity analyses may be warranted. We should also inspect whether censoring times cluster around administrative deadlines and whether some subsets of modules differ systematically in operation or manufacturing batch.

Probability plots and transformed survival plots offer complementary checks. Under Weibull, \(\log[-\log S(t)]=k\log t-k\log\eta\) is linear in \(\log t\) wherever \(0<S(t)<1\). A roughly linear plot can support—but not prove—the Weibull assumption. Under Exponential, \(-\log S(t)\) is linear in \(t\) through the origin. Such plots become unstable where Kaplan–Meier survival reaches zero or one; they should not be evaluated at undefined log values.

A model failing a check does not tell us which alternative is true. It tells us which assumption deserves further study. The goal of diagnostics is to expose mismatches between data and model features, not to engineer an appearance of fit by repeatedly searching until a p-value becomes favorable.

MODULE 19 / 25

19. AIC, uncertainty, and honest model selection

AIC compares penalized in-sample log-likelihood values: each fitted parameter costs two AIC units, while improving the maximized log-likelihood improves the score. It can be useful when comparing Exponential, Weibull, and Lognormal fits on identical records and outcome units. It does not ask whether model assumptions are physically plausible, whether future production batches resemble the test sample, or whether the right tail is correctly predicted.

A small dataset makes the parameter-count issue especially visible. With eight units and only five failures, a two-parameter family can easily appear flexible relative to one-parameter Exponential, while the estimate may vary substantially with a single failure time. A corrected AIC sometimes helps for small samples, but its conventional formula rests on approximations and a meaningful effective sample size; it should not be treated as an automatic repair for five events and censored observations.

Likelihood-based uncertainty can be explored using profile likelihood: hold a parameter at candidate values, optimize nuisance parameters, and examine how far the log-likelihood falls from its maximum. This is often more informative for positive and skewed parameters than a symmetric Wald interval. A bootstrap procedure may resample independent modules, refit the model repeatedly, and summarize the variability in survival at a policy-relevant horizon. If modules share a batch or test fixture, naive row-level resampling can misrepresent dependence; the resampling unit must match the independent unit in the design.

Compare prediction at a horizon within substantial observed follow-up before investigating distant tails. At 1,200 hours the dataset still contains several observed records supporting the curve. Beyond 2,200 hours no unit is followed; any value of \(S(40)\) or \(S(50)\) is determined almost entirely by parametric tail assumptions. A narrow numerical confidence interval under a misspecified distribution would not rescue the extrapolation. Model uncertainty and parameter uncertainty are different components that should not be conflated.

If the purpose is to decide a warranty length, one must also specify a loss function: failure costs, preventive replacement costs, safety margins, and acceptable risk. Choosing the mathematically best distribution from eight invented records does not make a warranty policy defensible. This is the boundary between learning statistical machinery and making an evidence-based engineering decision.

MODULE 20 / 25

20. Unit conversions and parameterization traps

A formula is not fully specified until its parameters and units are specified. In the running dataset, one unit of \(t\) equals 100 operating hours. Exponential rate \(0.05\) per hundred hours becomes \(0.0005\) per hour. A survival calculation must remain invariant: \(e^{-0.05(10)}=e^{-0.0005(1000)}=e^{-0.5}\). If two software runs disagree after a unit conversion, they may be using inconsistent rate and scale conventions, not different science.

For Weibull, the shape \(k\) stays unchanged when time units change, while the scale \(\eta\) multiplies by 100. Thus \(\eta=12\) hundreds of hours is \(1{,}200\) hours; at \(t=6\) hundreds, \((6/12)^k\) equals \((600/1200)^k\). The hazard numeric value must transform as an inverse time unit: a rate of 0.0833 per hundred hours equals 0.000833 per hour. This cross-check often catches the mistaken treatment of Weibull \(\eta\) as an inverse rate.

For Lognormal, the log-scale parameter shifts while dispersion stays the same: if \(T_h=100T_{100h}\), then \(\log T_h=\log100+\log T_{100h}\). Thus \(\mu_h=\mu_{100h}+\log100\) and \(\sigma_h=\sigma_{100h}\). Directly multiplying \(\mu\) by 100 would be wrong because \(\mu\) is a location parameter for log time. By contrast, the Lognormal median time \(e^\mu\) does multiply by 100.

Distribution Parameter Convention here Units
Exponential \(\lambda\) Constant rate Per 100 h
Weibull \(\eta\) Scale 100 h
Weibull \(k\) Shape Dimensionless
Lognormal \(\mu\) Mean of log(time in 100-h units) Log of numerical time
Lognormal \(\sigma\) SD of log(time) Dimensionless

A good habits checklist is to write the formula, the parameter convention, the measurement unit, and the meaning of the reported probability together. Without these four items, a numerical answer can look correct while answering the wrong physical question.

MODULE 21 / 25

21. A model choice decision workflow

Start with the event-generating process, not the package menu. Ask whether time zero and failure definition are consistent; whether first failure is an appropriate endpoint; whether independent, conditionally noninformative censoring is plausible; and whether the observed modules can reasonably share a lifetime distribution. A mix of distinct operating temperatures or incompatible manufacturing batches should be investigated before compressing everything into one pooled curve.

Next identify the time horizon relevant to the decision. A comparison at 1,200 hours may have some support from the observations, while a comparison at 10,000 hours is mainly extrapolation. Then inspect nonparametric survival behavior, the numbers at risk, and approximate evidence of early versus late failures. Finally fit several scientifically plausible families using a common censored likelihood, compare numerical fit and diagnostics, and analyze sensitivity to influential observations and censoring assumptions.

A defensible conclusion might say: 'On this dataset, a Weibull model describes the observed timing of failures with a certain fitted shape; the exact late-life hazard remains uncertain, because only five failures were seen.' It would not say 'The system has proven Weibull aging' or 'No module will last beyond the final observed time.' This distinction separates the fitted conditional model from scientific certainty.

Decision matrix for interpreting family assumptions: Exponential is parsimonious but rules out age-dependent hazard; Weibull allows monotone age dependence but not a genuine interior peak followed by a decline; Lognormal permits a non-monotone hazard but imposes Normality of log time. None of these families automatically handles a mixture of failure causes, preventive maintenance, competing event definitions, changing operating conditions, or dependent component failures. Such features belong in the experimental design and possibly richer models.

We stop before Accelerated Failure Time regression with covariates. AFT models explain how predictors shift or stretch lifetime distributions, and that is the central subject of Guide 79. Here the goal is to understand each baseline parametric duration family, its censored likelihood, and its interpretation sufficiently well that adding predictors later will not feel like a collection of unexplained formulas.

22. Independent practice: seven problems with explicit data

Work on paper before looking at the solutions. Every exercise uses the conventions of this guide: time is in hundreds of operating hours unless otherwise stated, event means first failure, and the specified synthetic data are not real product measurements. Show the formula, substitution, intermediate arithmetic, units, and interpretation. A calculator or Python may check arithmetic, but a correct decimal without reasoning is not a complete answer.

Problem 1 — Connect three functions. A hypothetical model has \(S(6)=0.75\) and \(f(6)=0.03\) per hundred hours. Compute \(F(6)\) and \(h(6)\); explain why neither the density nor hazard is a probability at the exact time 6. For a very small interval of 0.2 hundred hours, approximate the conditional failure probability.

Problem 2 — Exponential prediction. With rate \(\lambda=0.08\) per hundred hours, find the probability of surviving 500 hours, the probability of failure by 500 hours, the median, and the mean. Then calculate the exact conditional probability of surviving from 500 to 800 hours; compare it with survival from time zero to 300 hours.

Problem 3 — Weibull forms. Consider two modeled component groups with common scale \(\eta=10\) hundred hours and shapes \(k=0.8\) and \(k=2\). Compute each hazard at \(t=5\) and \(t=15\). State which hazard increases and which decreases, and explain why a hazard comparison at a single time does not establish the probability of surviving an entire future interval.

Problem 4 — Weibull percentile. With \(\eta=12\), \(k=2\), calculate the median and the 90th percentile of time to failure. Verify the median by substituting back into the survival function. Clearly distinguish the percentile of failures from a survival probability.

Problem 5 — Lognormal. Let \(\mu=\log 10\), \(\sigma=0.4\), and time be measured in hundreds of hours. Find the median, the mean, the probability of surviving 1,000 hours, and the 90th percentile. Explain what quantity, if any, is normally distributed.

Problem 6 — Right-censored likelihood and MLE. A separate synthetic test follows four units: (2, event), (5, censored), (8, event), (10, censored). Write the complete generic likelihood, specialize it to Exponential rate \(\lambda\), derive the log-likelihood, calculate the MLE and its mean lifetime, and explain why a censored time is not an observed failure time.

Problem 7 — Model selection and transfer. A researcher reports AIC values 44 for Exponential, 43 for Weibull and 42 for Lognormal on the same test, with only six failures and maximum follow-up of 2,000 hours. Explain what can and cannot be concluded; identify the danger of predicting reliability at 10,000 hours. Then discuss how conclusions would change if the units censored at 1,500 hours were actually removed for signs of deterioration rather than administrative closure.

Before continuing, ask: Can I articulate what each event indicator means? Can I derive the effect of a censoring row on likelihood? Can I tell whether a result is a rate, a probability, or a time? If not, revisit the conceptual modules and reproduce one example without looking at the printed formula.

23. Fully worked solutions, problems 1–3

Solution 1. Survival is the complement of the cumulative probability of failure, so \(F(6)=1-S(6)=1-0.75=0.25\). The hazard uses the density divided by survival: \(h(6)=f(6)/S(6)=0.03/0.75=0.04\) per hundred hours. Although 0.03 and 0.04 are both decimal numbers, their units differ from probability: they describe density or instantaneous conditional rate, not the mass of an exact point. Over \(\Delta t=0.2\) hundred hours, approximate conditional failure probability is \(h(6)\Delta t=0.04(0.2)=0.008\), or 0.8%. This approximation assumes hazard is nearly constant over the short interval; an exact calculation would need \(S(6.2)/S(6)\).

Verification 1. \(F(6)+S(6)=0.25+0.75=1\), so the probability complement is consistent. Units also cancel in \(h\Delta t\), leaving a unitless probability. An incorrect answer of 4% for the interval would mistake the numeric rate per 100 h for the probability over only 20 hours. A further transfer check: halve the interval to 10 hours, and the first-order approximation halves to 0.4%.

Solution 2. Convert 500 hours to \(t=5\). Exponential survival is \(S(t)=e^{-\lambda t}\), so \(S(5)=e^{-0.08(5)}=e^{-0.4}=0.670320\). Failure by 500 hours has probability \(1-0.670320=0.329680\). The mean is \(1/0.08=12.5\) hundred hours, or 1,250 hours. The median solves \(e^{-0.08t}=0.5\): take logarithms to get \(t=\log2/0.08=8.66434\) hundred hours, approximately 866.4 hours.

Solution 2 continued. From 500 to 800 hours the additional waiting time is 300 hours, \(t=3\) hundred hours. By conditional probability, \(P(T>8\mid T>5)=S(8)/S(5)=e^{-0.08(8-5)}=e^{-0.24}=0.786628\). Survival from a new start to 300 hours is also \(S(3)=e^{-0.24}=0.786628\). This equality is the Exponential memoryless theorem in action. Do not replace it with \(S(8)\), which is the unconditional probability of reaching 800 hours.

Solution 3, first group. With \(k=0.8\), \(\eta=10\), the hazard is \(h(t)=(0.8/10)(t/10)^{-0.2}=0.08(t/10)^{-0.2}\). At \(t=5\), \((0.5)^{-0.2}=e^{-0.2\log0.5}=e^{0.138629}\approx1.14870\); hence \(h(5)\approx0.09190\) per hundred hours. At \(t=15\), \((1.5)^{-0.2}=e^{-0.081093}\approx0.92211\), hence \(h(15)\approx0.07377\). The hazard decreases because the power exponent is negative.

Solution 3, second group. With \(k=2\), \(\eta=10\), \(h(t)=0.2(t/10)\). Thus \(h(5)=0.1\) and \(h(15)=0.3\) per hundred hours, an increasing hazard. Conditional survival from \(s\) to \(s+u\) depends on an integral of hazard, \(\exp[-\int_s^{s+u}h(v)dv]\), not solely on \(h(s)\). For Weibull this integral equals \(((s+u)/\eta)^k-(s/\eta)^k\). Therefore a single instantaneous rate cannot stand in for an entire time interval, particularly when hazard changes rapidly.

24. Fully worked solutions, problems 4–7

Solution 4. Start with \(t_p=\eta[-\log(1-p)]^{1/k}\). For the median with \(p=0.5\), \(t_{0.5}=12\sqrt{-\log0.5}=12\sqrt{\log2}=12(0.832555)=9.99066\) hundred hours. In operating hours, multiply by 100: approximately 999.1. Substitution checks the result: \(S(9.99066)=\exp[-(9.99066/12)^2]=\exp[-0.693147]=0.5\). For \(p=0.9\), \(t_{0.9}=12\sqrt{-\log0.1}=12\sqrt{2.302585}=18.2094\) hundred hours, about 1,821 hours. The 90th percentile is the time by which 90% have failed under the model; only 10% remain afterward.

Solution 5. For Lognormal with \(\mu=\log10\) and \(\sigma=0.4\), median time is \(e^\mu=10\) hundred hours, or 1,000 hours. Mean is \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}=10e^{0.08}=10.83287\) hundreds, about 1,083.3 hours. At \(t=10\), \(z=(\log10-\log10)/0.4=0\), hence \(S(10)=1-\Phi(0)=0.5\). For the 90th percentile, \(t_{0.9}=10\exp(0.4\times1.28155)=10e^{0.51262}\approx16.696\) hundred hours, or about 1,670 hours. The normally distributed variable is \(\log T\), not \(T\). The physical time is positive and right-skewed.

Solution 6, likelihood. The full data are (2,1), (5,0), (8,1), (10,0). Consequently \(L=f(2)S(5)f(8)S(10)\). Exponential gives \(f(y)=\lambda e^{-\lambda y}\), \(S(y)=e^{-\lambda y}\), so \(L(\lambda)=\lambda^2e^{-\lambda(2+5+8+10)}=\lambda^2e^{-25\lambda}\). Taking logarithms yields \(\ell(\lambda)=2\log\lambda-25\lambda\). Differentiate: \(\ell'(\lambda)=2/\lambda-25\), so \(\widehat\lambda=2/25=0.08\) per hundred hours. The second derivative \(-2/\lambda^2\) is negative for positive rates. Estimated mean is \(1/\widehat\lambda=12.5\) hundred hours, or 1,250 hours. The two censored records contribute 15 of the 25 hundred hours of observed exposure, but no observed failures. Treating them as events would turn \(\lambda^2\) into \(\lambda^4\), contradicting the data.

Solution 6, independent check. The score evaluated at \(0.08\) is \(2/0.08-25=25-25=0\). The MLE is not the reciprocal of the average among the failed records alone: their sum is 2+8=10, which would yield \(2/10=0.2\), more than twice the correct rate. This contrast shows why the censoring contributions matter.

Solution 7. The smallest of the three reported AIC values is 42, so Lognormal ranks best by this particular in-sample criterion among the compared families. Differences of one or two units are small and do not justify a categorical claim that the true model is Lognormal, especially with only six failures. The fit tells us little about the distribution after 2,000 hours; predicting 10,000-hour reliability requires large extrapolation into a tail entirely determined by the assumed family. Weibull and Lognormal may extrapolate very differently even if they fit the observed range similarly.

If units were withdrawn at 1,500 hours because they showed deterioration, censoring could depend on impending failure. The usual \(S(c)\) terms then fail to describe the observation process without additional assumptions or information; the fitted distribution and AIC comparison may be biased or inapplicable. A responsible response is to investigate reasons for withdrawal, test sensitivity to plausible mechanisms, recover additional information if possible, and avoid making strong long-horizon claims. The correct lesson is not merely 'choose the smallest AIC', but 'interpret the statistic conditional on a defensible design and likelihood.'

MODULE 25 / 25

25. Synthesis and a final understanding test

A complete analysis links the question, the observable data, a probability model, a likelihood, mathematical verification, diagnostics, and limits. The question concerns time until first failure. The data record a duration and an event indicator for each unit. The probability model supplies density, survival, and hazard, which represent different views of the same distribution. The right-censored likelihood uses \(f(y)\) for observed failures and \(S(y)\) for administrative observation endings. Maximum likelihood then provides estimates conditional on that full specification.

The three families make distinct claims. Exponential assumes constant hazard and has memorylessness. Weibull adds a shape parameter that makes hazard monotone, with Exponential as the special case \(k=1\). Lognormal models Normality of log-time and permits a hazard that rises then falls. No family is universally superior. A comparison should be tied to a scientifically meaningful horizon, enough events, appropriate censoring, honest uncertainty, and credible physical assumptions.

Use this final self-test without looking back: (1) Explain why \(S(c)\), not \(f(c)\), represents a right-censored unit. (2) Derive \(h=f/S\) and connect hazard to cumulative hazard. (3) State which Weibull shape values imply increasing, constant, or decreasing hazard. (4) Explain why a Lognormal parameter \(\mu\) changes when hours become hundreds of hours. (5) Reproduce the Exponential MLE \(5/101\) from the eight displayed rows. (6) Identify one prediction in our case that would be mostly extrapolation, and justify your concern. Your answers should include meaning and units, not only symbols.

A reader who can reproduce those connections is prepared for the next conceptual step: modeling how observable characteristics influence duration. Guide 79 develops Accelerated Failure Time regression and Time Ratios. The present guide intentionally stops before that regression extension. Its purpose is to ensure the underlying distributions and censored likelihood make sense before additional model complexity is introduced. Statistical fluency is the ability to explain both what a fitted number means and which questions it cannot answer.
