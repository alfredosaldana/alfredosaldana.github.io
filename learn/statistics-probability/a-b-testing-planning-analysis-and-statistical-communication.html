01. A decision—not a p-value—is the beginning of an A/B test

A subscription-based digital service operates a registration page. Its product team proposes a shorter form, variant B, instead of the existing version A. The practical question is not merely whether a difference exists: should the company expose future visitors to B, considering the size of the gain, engineering cost, possible user harm, and uncertainty? An A/B test is a randomized experiment in which eligible units are assigned to two alternatives. Randomization makes treatment assignment independent of potential outcomes in the assignment design; it does not magically guarantee correct measurement, perfect compliance, transportability to future customers, or the absence of interference.

We shall use a fully synthetic teaching case, not observations from an actual company. Assignment is at the account level, 1:1, before showing a registration experience. Here an account means a stable provisional identity created before final registration; it is not an already-completed paid subscription. The primary outcome is whether an eligible assigned account completes registration within seven days of its first exposure; one account contributes one binary observation. The pre-agreed decision threshold is a minimum worthwhile absolute increase of three percentage points, because implementation and maintenance have real costs. The test was designed for 2,100 accounts per arm, with a fixed calendar end and seven additional days for outcome maturity. At the analysis snapshot, for exposition, there are 2,000 eligible randomized accounts per arm with mature outcomes: 240 complete registration under A, and 290 under B. This example is a fixed synthetic snapshot, not proof that the original target enrollment was reached. We will explicitly distinguish the planned sample from the analyzed snapshot and discuss how that affects claims of power and protocol adherence.

That distinction teaches a useful habit: a promising dashboard number is not automatically a launch recommendation. A defensible recommendation requires an estimand, data integrity, a predeclared decision rule, an uncertainty interval, guardrails, and an honest account of deviations from the design. Every numerical statement in this guide can be reconstructed from the counts just given. No external dataset, company performance claim, or causal finding is implied by these fabricated values.

02. Prerequisites, learning objectives, and a compact glossary

Prerequisites are elementary probability, conditional probability, a sample proportion, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, the idea of multiple hypotheses, and the logic of random assignment from Guides 40, 42, 44, 46, and 59. We recall each tool locally rather than assuming specialist knowledge. Familiarity with regression is helpful but unnecessary. By the end, a learner should be able to produce an experiment charter; calculate a two-proportion comparison by hand; diagnose assignment imbalance; explain Intention-to-Treat; distinguish statistical from practical significance; and write a recommendation that makes uncertainty and harm visible.

An experimental unit is what is assigned, here an account. An observation is a recorded measurement, here an account's seven-day completion indicator. A treatment is the assigned experience. The estimand is the precisely defined population quantity to be estimated; here the difference in seven-day completion probabilities under the two assignment policies. Guardrails are monitored outcomes on which deterioration is unacceptable even when the primary metric improves. A minimum detectable effect (MDE) is an effect size used to plan power, not the smallest effect that a subsequent significant test can establish. A minimum practically important effect is a business threshold; the two may be numerically set equal but represent different decisions.

The significance level α describes the long-run Type I error under an appropriate null, not the probability the discovered result is false. Power is the probability of rejection under a specified true alternative. A confidence interval is generated by a procedure with repeated-sampling coverage; it is not, by itself, a posterior probability statement about a fixed parameter. Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM) describes an assignment-count imbalance inconsistent with the intended traffic allocation, sometimes signaling an implementation problem. Optional stopping means using observed interim evidence to decide when to terminate; conventional fixed-horizon p-values generally do not preserve their advertised error guarantees under unplanned repeated looks.

03. Define the causal question before opening an analytics dashboard

For each eligible account i, imagine two potential completion indicators: Y_i(A) had it been assigned version A, and Y_i(B) had it been assigned B, each observed within the same seven-day window. Only one is observed for each account. The causal estimand is the average contrast between assignment policies: Δ = E[Y(B)] − E[Y(A)], for the eligible population at the times and conditions studied. This is a risk difference measured in probability units, conventionally reported as percentage points (pp). A separate useful descriptive measure, the relative lift, divides the absolute difference by the A rate. Neither is the same as an odds ratio.

Potential outcomes focus the design on the missing counterfactual. Random assignment, when implemented correctly and when units do not meaningfully affect one another, makes the observed groups exchangeable on average across hypothetical assignments. It does not force their demographic counts to match exactly in one realized sample. Confounding by pre-existing user characteristics is addressed at the assignment stage; measurement errors, attrition, concurrent campaigns, and post-assignment filtering require additional controls. A sharp null says Y_i(B)=Y_i(A) for every unit; a weaker null about averages says only E[Y(B)−Y(A)]=0. The null targeted by an ordinary two-proportion z-test is an equality of marginal probabilities under its sampling assumptions, not a universal statement that no person responds.

Specify the target population: eligible newly exposed accounts, not all historical website users. Specify the exposure window, duplicate handling, eligibility timing, geography, and whether one customer can create multiple accounts. Suppose a family shares a computer and sees both variants: assignment by account may permit spillovers. The appropriate unit could then be customer, household, or geographic cluster, depending on the causal interference mechanism. Increasing the raw number of page views cannot fix a design where the same person contributes dependent events. A p-value calculated as though those events were independent is unjustifiably optimistic.

Core formula · symbols and assumptions explained in prose
\[\Delta=\mathbb{E}[Y(B)]-\mathbb{E}[Y(A)]\]

04. Build a precise experiment charter

A concise preregistration document can be more valuable than elaborate statistics performed after seeing results. Write: “Among eligible newly exposed accounts, randomize 1:1 between A and B using a persistent assignment key; measure registration completed within seven days; estimate B minus A in percentage points using all assigned eligible units; run until the prespecified enrollment/calendar endpoint, then wait seven days for outcomes to mature; check SRM and data loss before interpretation; report a two-sided 95% confidence interval; treat three percentage points as the minimum practically worthwhile lift; monitor complaints and technical failures as guardrails; and do not stop early based on a nominal p-value.”

The wording matters. If the team changes “within seven days” to “within 24 hours” after discovering a more favorable result, it has silently selected among outcomes. If it excludes people who never loaded the new interface, it changes the population from assigned accounts to a post-treatment subset. If it measures a click without distinguishing clicks from completed registration, it may optimize the wrong behavior. The charter should identify the single primary metric, a small motivated set of guardrails, and exploratory diagnostics labeled as exploratory. Precommitment cannot repair flawed assignment, but it reduces hindsight-driven choices disguised as confirmatory evidence.

For our example, the charter distinguishes the design target of 2,100 accounts per group from a mature-outcome snapshot of 2,000 accounts per group. An analyst should not quietly rename the latter “the completed trial.” If the missing 100 per arm were still awaiting the outcome window, the correct action is to wait, not to treat immature accounts as failures; if they were excluded because of an unplanned operational issue, investigate and document how exclusion changes the estimand. This guide analyzes the snapshot to teach the arithmetic, and reserves the final launch decision until protocol integrity is established.

05. Assignment unit, consistency, and exposure logging

The service should generate a stable assignment at the account level. A deterministic hash of a persistent account identifier and an experiment seed is one possible implementation; a trustworthy randomization service is another. In either case, the group recorded for analysis must represent the original assignment, not whichever page the account happened to view most recently. All eligible accounts assigned during the enrollment window belong to the analysis denominator even if they abandon the page, subject to the originally specified eligibility rules.

A clean event record has at least an anonymized account key, randomization timestamp, assigned arm, eligibility status established before assignment, exposure or delivery diagnostics, registration timestamp if any, and a maturity timestamp seven days after assignment. Important checks include unique keys, immutable assignments, server-clock consistency, missing events, and logging parity. Logging parity is not cosmetic: if the B form fails to send events for some mobile devices, a lower observed completion rate may reflect instrumentation rather than user behavior. Conversely, counting only successful interactions creates a self-selected population.

An experiment unit is not the same as an event. If one assigned account loads the form five times, those five loads do not become five independent experimental units. If users can switch devices, the same identity must retain assignment or the design must explicitly handle cross-device collisions. If coworkers can share an invitation code, interference may dilute or contaminate the contrast. These are causal-design concerns, not merely engineering details. Record how many units were allocated, how many were exposed, how many generated mature outcome data, and why those counts differ.

06. Primary outcomes, denominators, and metric traps

Define Y_i=1 when eligible randomized account i completes registration no later than seven days after assignment, and Y_i=0 otherwise, once the full window is observable. Then a group conversion rate is the arithmetic average of the binary indicators, not the count of page loads divided by ad clicks. Every denominator choice implicitly defines which population the finding describes. “Completed among accounts that clicked start” answers a different, post-assignment question from “completed among all randomized eligible accounts.” The second often better matches the policy decision to expose incoming eligible users to B.

A primary metric must be sensitive to the treatment while measuring something important. A shorter form might increase submission taps but also produce erroneous records or complaints. Those are candidates for quality guardrails. Guardrails should have definitions, owners, directions of concern, and decision thresholds written in advance. For example, “technical failure rate must not increase by more than 0.5 percentage points” requires its own precision discussion; observing no failures in a handful of users cannot establish safety. A revenue measure may require a longer outcome horizon than seven-day completion and must not be relabeled as measured if it is merely anticipated.

Distinguish absolute lift from relative lift. A movement from 12% to 14.5% is 2.5 percentage points or 20.83% relative lift because 0.025/0.12=0.20833. Saying “20.83 percentage points” would be wrong. Saying “about one-fifth more completions per eligible user” communicates the relative change, but does not establish extra revenue, long-run retention, or a robust effect in all subgroups. Units and denominators deserve the same care as formula algebra.

\[\widehat p_g=\frac{x_g}{n_g},\quad \widehat\Delta=\widehat p_B-\widehat p_A\]

07. Power analysis: which effect should the experiment detect?

Before running the experiment, specify an assumed control conversion probability p_A=0.12, a planning alternative p_B=0.15, two-sided α=0.05, desired power 0.80, and equal allocation. The hypothetical alternative is three percentage points, chosen because that is the minimum improvement worth launching. With p-bar=(0.12+0.15)/2=0.135, the approximate two-group sample size is derived by matching the standardized alternative shift to a rejection threshold plus a power margin. For moderate sample sizes, a normal approximation gives:

Core formula · symbols and assumptions explained in prose
\[n\approx\frac{\left[z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)}+z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_A(1-p_A)+p_B(1-p_B)}\right]^2}{(p_B-p_A)^2}.\]

Insert z_0.975=1.95996 and z_0.8=0.84162; the first term is 1.95996 sqrt(2×0.135×0.865), the second is 0.84162 sqrt(0.12×0.88+0.15×0.85), and their squared sum divided by 0.03² is approximately 2,036 accounts per arm. A target of 2,100 per arm allows modest rounding but is not a comprehensive loss allowance. The formula is an approximation, not an exact test guarantee; continuity corrections, clustering, other outcomes, allocation ratios, or attrition can change the requirement.

Notice the inverse-square effect of the target difference. Asking to detect a two-point lift instead of three points, with baseline 12%, requires roughly 4,438 per arm rather than 2,036. Smaller effects are harder to distinguish from sampling noise. The MDE is not an estimate of the eventual true effect and is not an after-the-fact excuse to dismiss an unexpected result. If a planning assumption changes, recompute before inspecting outcomes; do not reverse-engineer the assumptions from observed data to make the study appear adequately powered.

08. Duration, enrollment, and time-varying populations

Sample size and duration are not synonyms. If the service randomizes about 600 eligible accounts per day across both arms, achieving 4,200 assignments takes about seven days, followed by a full seven-day maturation period for the last assigned account. Yet seven calendar days may underrepresent weekday/weekend cycles, marketing changes, or onboarding seasonality. A plan might therefore specify at least two full weeks of enrollment, alongside an enrollment target and a fixed rule for what happens if traffic is lower than expected.

Do not move the finish line based on favorable results. If enrollment ends on a predetermined date, the trial needs the outcome lag before final analysis. If a fixed target count is the design, state whether recruitment can continue past the calendar horizon, how the randomization schedule behaves, and which condition takes precedence. Repeatedly opening the dashboard while data accumulate is not itself a violation; using the p-value to decide when to stop is the issue for conventional fixed-horizon inference.

An account joining on Monday and another joining Saturday may have different external experiences. Randomization balances such timing effects in expectation when both groups enroll concurrently; launching A in week one and B in week two would confound treatment with calendar time. Predeclare intended strata if necessary, such as device or acquisition channel. Do not promise balance within tiny strata without sufficient counts. If an interruption occurs, preserve an audit trail of timestamps, affected traffic and any blinded decision to pause for platform safety.

09. Sample Ratio Mismatch: diagnose before estimating lift

Suppose the intended 1:1 allocation generates counts N_A=2,000 and N_B=2,000. Under the hypothesis that each allocation is independent with probability 1/2, expected counts are 2,000 and 2,000. The chi-square goodness-of-fit diagnostic is Σ(O−E)²/E=0, with one degree of freedom and p=1. In real experiments, exact equality is not required and can be coincidental. This test assesses the assignment proportions, not treatment efficacy and not the causal outcome difference.

Consider instead 2,200 assigned to A and 1,800 to B, while the plan expected 2,000 each. The SRM statistic is (200²/2,000)+(−200)²/2,000=40. For one degree of freedom the tail probability is about 2.54×10⁻¹⁰. Such an extreme deviation should trigger investigation: unequal exposure logging, bucketing bugs, redirect failures, eligibility filters applied after assignment, geographic routing, or a deliberate but undocumented traffic split are candidates. It is not a license to massage counts until the p-value looks comfortable.

A non-significant SRM check does not prove randomization was correctly implemented. Count balance cannot reveal a mislabeled treatment, a bug affecting both arms symmetrically, or outcome loss that differs without shifting total assignments. Conversely, an SRM flag is a diagnostic rather than mathematical proof of a specific bug. Follow up with assignment-system logs and data-lineage checks. Exclude units only for rules written before assignment or for documented integrity failures, not because they make an estimate inconvenient.

\[\chi^2_{\mathrm{SRM}}=\sum_{g\in\{A,B\}}\frac{(O_g-E_g)^2}{E_g}\]

10. Intention-to-Treat protects the assignment question

Intention-to-Treat (ITT) means analyze eligible randomized units in their originally assigned groups, even when some do not receive, notice, or follow the intended experience. This estimates the effect of a policy of assignment. It is especially valuable because assignment happens before later behavior. Conditioning on “saw the entire form” or “reached checkout” may select people affected by the treatment and break randomized comparability.

Imagine 2,000 assigned B accounts, but only 1,800 successfully load the page, among whom 290 complete. Reporting 290/1,800=16.11% as the randomized B conversion rate would be misleading. The ITT rate, under the stated complete-outcome definition, is 290/2,000=14.5%. The loading failure is part of the overall assigned experience and belongs in a reliability guardrail. An as-treated comparison can be informative diagnostically, but because treatment received may depend on device, user behavior, or other post-assignment factors, it does not generally inherit randomization's causal guarantee.

ITT does not mean ignoring all missing data. If outcomes for some assigned accounts truly cannot be observed, the analyst must report missingness and justify handling; treating unknown outcomes as zero is a substantive assumption, not a universal convention. Our main example assumes mature outcomes are available for every one of the 4,000 accounts in the snapshot. It does not establish why 200 additional planned assignments are absent. The distinction between adherence, missing measurement, and incomplete enrollment is part of an honest results report.

Core formula · symbols and assumptions explained in prose
\[\widehat p_B^{\mathrm{ITT}}=\frac{290}{2000}=0.145\]

11. Analyze the complete synthetic 2×2 table

Here is the complete aggregated synthetic teaching dataset: A has 240 completers and 1,760 noncompleters among 2,000; B has 290 completers and 1,710 noncompleters among 2,000. These four counts determine the two sample proportions. The A estimate is 240/2,000=0.1200; B is 290/2,000=0.1450. Consequently Δ-hat=0.145−0.120=0.025, or 2.5 pp. The risk ratio is 0.145/0.12≈1.2083, giving 20.83% relative lift. A naive extrapolation to 100,000 similar accounts predicts 2,500 additional completions, but that is a scenario calculation, not a validated forecast of future behavior.

For a two-sided test of H0:p_A=p_B, the pooled estimate under the null is p-pool=(240+290)/(2,000+2,000)=530/4,000=0.1325. The pooled standard error is sqrt(0.1325×0.8675×(1/2,000+1/2,000))≈0.01072. Dividing the observed 0.025 by this value gives z≈2.332 and a two-sided normal p-value ≈0.0197. At the prespecified α=0.05, the equality null is rejected by this approximate fixed-horizon test if its assumptions and the planned analysis are valid. This sentence alone does not determine whether B should launch.

The ordinary unpooled standard error for the difference is sqrt(0.12×0.88/2,000+0.145×0.855/2,000)≈0.010714. The illustrative Wald 95% confidence interval is 0.025 ±1.95996(0.010714), approximately [0.0040,0.0460], or [0.40,4.60] pp. The interval excludes zero but includes improvements smaller than the predeclared 3 pp minimum. Thus the evidence favors a positive effect under the statistical model, yet does not establish that the intervention clears the business threshold. The elementary Wald interval is adequate for this demonstration with moderate rates and large n, but alternative score or Newcombe intervals can behave better in small or extreme samples.

12. Statistical evidence versus a launch-worthy effect

A p-value answers a question about incompatibility with a point null under a test model. A launch decision answers whether the possible gains outweigh costs and risks. These are not interchangeable. The estimated 2.5 pp gain is below the predeclared 3 pp minimum practically worthwhile effect. More importantly, the 95% interval covers values from about 0.4 to 4.6 pp. It contains effects that would not justify launch and effects that might. Calling B “proven valuable” because p<0.05 silently swaps the statistical threshold for the economic one.

Suppose implementation costs $25,000 per quarter and each additional valid registration generates $2 of contribution after operating costs. At 100,000 eligible quarterly accounts, 2.5 pp means an estimated 2,500 additional completions, generating only $5,000 before the implementation cost. The central estimate therefore fails the stated simplified cost comparison. Even the upper endpoint 4.6 pp would generate about $9,200 at that traffic and per-registration value. In this illustrative economic scenario the treatment would not justify a $25,000 quarterly cost regardless of nominal significance. Change the volume or value per conversion and the decision may change: the analysis needs the actual decision economics, not an invented universal business rule.

These costs and values are hypothetical and deliberately distinct from the trial measurements; they are not in the outcome table. Also consider a quality guardrail: a shorter form might reduce correctness of registration data. A primary-metric lift can coexist with damage elsewhere. When value is uncertain, report a range of scenarios and identify which assumptions would reverse the recommendation. A responsible analyst does not hide favorable statistical evidence, but neither claims a business success unsupported by costs, safety, and uncertainty.

13. Guardrails: what counts as acceptable collateral change?

A guardrail can be technical error rate, support-contact rate, identity-verification failure, fraud signal, complaint rate, or longer-term retention. The appropriate choice depends on a plausible mechanism of harm from the changed form. A guardrail should have a precisely defined event, unit, horizon, direction of concern, and action threshold before launch. Not all guardrails need a significance-hunting test: some are monitored with safety limits, upper confidence bounds, or operational alerts under a deliberately separate policy.

Imagine the primary completion lift is positive while the technical-failure rate appears 0.3 pp higher under B. Without denominator counts and an uncertainty interval, the word “appears” is essential. It would be wrong to declare no harm merely because a small guardrail test has p>0.05; absence of evidence is not a demonstration of equivalence or noninferiority. To assert that an increase in failures is below a safety margin, one needs an appropriate one-sided interval or noninferiority analysis with a declared margin and adequate sample size.

Guardrail data may mature more slowly than the primary endpoint. A seven-day completion measure and a 30-day retention guardrail cannot both be complete seven days after the final assignment. If the policy requires mature 30-day safety data, either wait or identify the launch as provisional under a controlled rollout. An advanced operational design might include an emergency safety stop, specified independently of efficacy p-values; that does not justify opportunistic efficacy stopping. Separate safety governance from confirmatory inference, and log the rules that authorize any exception.

14. Multiplicity and the false-discovery problem

Suppose the team examines 20 independent metrics, each null and each tested at nominal 5%. The probability of at least one false rejection would be 1−0.95^20≈0.6415. The exact result relies on independence; correlated metrics change the number, but the general problem remains. A team that picks the metric with the smallest p-value after analysis and calls it its primary metric does not have a 5% confirmatory claim. This is why the experiment charter declares one primary test and labels other comparisons with their intended role.

For a family of confirmatory hypotheses, procedures such as Bonferroni or Holm can control the familywise probability of one or more false positives, under their stated conditions. Benjamini–Hochberg targets a different quantity, the expected fraction of false discoveries among rejected hypotheses, under appropriate assumptions. It does not promise that each individual rejected metric has a particular probability of being false. A plan may designate one primary efficacy hypothesis and treat guardrails through separate safety criteria rather than stuffing all metrics into an unprincipled adjustment. Families should be defined based on the scientific decision, not after seeing the results.

Subgroup inspection multiplies opportunities too. Looking separately at mobile, desktop, three geographies, five acquisition channels, and new versus returning accounts creates many noisy comparisons. A visually large subgroup effect may reflect small denominators and regression to the mean. To claim a difference of treatment effects, test an interaction or another preplanned contrast; “significant in subgroup 1 but not subgroup 2” is not itself proof that effects differ. Exploratory slicing can generate useful hypotheses for another study, as long as the report calls it exploratory.

\[\Pr(\text{at least one false rejection})=1-(1-\alpha)^{20}\]

15. Why optional stopping changes a familiar p-value

With conventional fixed-horizon testing, the decision rule is calibrated for a particular analysis timing and specified sample or sampling rule. If an analyst tests after every 100 accounts and stops whenever p<0.05, the event “ever cross 0.05 during the sequence” can be substantially more likely under the null than the nominal level. The individual p-value at the selected stopping time is not the same as one obtained at an independently fixed sample size. Continual monitoring without a valid sequential rule undermines the advertised Type I error control.

This is not a claim that analysts must never inspect operational dashboards. They can monitor assignment counts, page failures, mature data, or safety alerts without deciding the efficacy result from unplanned interim p-values. Formal solutions exist—group-sequential boundaries, alpha-spending functions, and always-valid testing or confidence sequences—but each requires its own assumptions, design and implementation. None is implemented or certified by the fixed-horizon calculations in this guide. Retrofitting a sequential explanation after a favorable early stop is not an adequate substitute for prespecification.

Suppose at the first look the data give p=0.048, at the next p=0.061, and at the final planned look p=0.020. Reporting the first look as proof because it was below 0.05 would select the stopping moment. Reporting the final planned result is appropriate only if the plan was actually followed and data integrity holds. The numbers here are a pedagogical illustration, not a trajectory simulated from our 4,000 accounts. The good habit is to write the looks and stop rules before the first outcome is available, and to disclose any deviations.

16. Uncertainty, clustering, and covariate adjustment

The elementary formulas above assume independent account-level outcomes within and across groups, suitable sample sizes and reliable randomization. If accounts are clustered inside households, schools, sales teams or stores, the independence assumption can fail. Correlated outcomes carry less effective information than the same count of independent accounts. In a cluster randomized experiment, the number and distribution of clusters matter; treating all members as independently randomized is pseudoreplication. The design effect can be approximated by 1+(m−1)ρ for equal cluster size m and intraclass correlation ρ, subject to its assumptions.

Pre-treatment covariates such as device category or past engagement can sometimes improve precision through prespecified adjustment; randomization does not forbid adjustment. But conditioning on variables measured after assignment may introduce selection bias. A model-based adjusted estimate should be reported alongside a clear definition of its target, adjustment set, robust or design-based uncertainty, and the unadjusted primary estimate where appropriate. More complicated analysis is not automatically more valid. The same principle applies to missing data: explain the missingness mechanism and sensitivity instead of assuming a statistical package solves it.

Uncertainty also includes non-sampling limitations: event loss, untracked duplicate accounts, interference, shifting traffic, and users responding differently after permanent rollout. A narrow interval around a precisely measured short-term behavior does not cover those sources by itself. One can report confidence intervals and still explicitly discuss external validity. Avoid presenting a single number as if it were the entire uncertainty of a product decision.

17. Balance checks and why randomization is not a demographic matching algorithm

It is sensible to summarize pre-treatment characteristics across A and B: device type, acquisition channel, historic activity and geography. Random assignment implies balance in expectation across repeated assignments; the observed groups may differ by chance. Baseline tables can reveal impossible values, missing data or strong operational inconsistencies. They should not become a fishing expedition where the team repeatedly randomizes until every covariate p-value exceeds 0.05—such behavior modifies the actual assignment mechanism and must be part of the original design.

A covariate can be imbalanced without invalidating the randomized estimator, provided the assignment really occurred as intended and no other design failure exists. Conversely, closely matched summary statistics do not prove successful randomization. Audit the randomization mechanism and exposure logs rather than relying only on significance tests for baseline variables. Large sample sizes can make trivial baseline differences look statistically significant; small sample sizes can hide meaningful imbalances. Report standardized differences when helpful and interpret them as diagnostics, not as a certificate of causal validity.

If allocation is stratified in advance—for example, randomize 1:1 within mobile and desktop—the estimation and variance strategy should respect those strata. Stratification may stabilize allocation and improve precision when strata predict outcomes. It is not equivalent to comparing only the subgroup with the most favorable observed effect. Write the randomization and analysis rules together. The statistical model must follow the design, not the reverse.

18. Communicate the complete result without overstating it

A transparent summary can read: “In an illustrative sample of 4,000 randomized eligible accounts with mature seven-day outcomes, completion was 12.0% in A (240/2,000) and 14.5% in B (290/2,000). Estimated absolute lift was 2.5 pp; the approximate two-sided 95% confidence interval was 0.40 to 4.60 pp. Under a conventional fixed-horizon two-proportion z-test, p≈0.0197. Although the statistical evidence is compatible with a positive lift, the point estimate is below the predeclared 3 pp practical threshold, and the interval does not establish an effect above that threshold. This snapshot falls short of the planned 2,100 accounts per arm, so final protocol review and outcome maturity are required before any confirmatory claim or launch.”

This paragraph is deliberately more useful than “B wins, p<0.05.” It includes units, outcome horizon, denominators, uncertainty, test conditions, decision benchmark and operational caveats. A complete report should add guardrail results once observed, SRM diagnostics, missing data and exclusions, planned-versus-actual dates, and any analysis deviations. The synthetic case has no measured safety outcomes; therefore this guide does not invent reassuring guardrail findings. “Not available” is a real and important reporting status.

For an executive audience, begin with the decision, then the magnitude and evidence, then limitations and next action. For a technical reviewer, include the charter, unit-of-analysis checks, assumptions, exact counts and calculation steps. Both audiences deserve the same factual honesty. A report that displays only positive subgroup effects or replaces absolute lift with dramatic-looking relative percentages is misleading even if every displayed calculation is arithmetically correct.

19. Decision memo and action under uncertainty

Our provisional recommendation is do not approve an unrestricted rollout based on this snapshot. The observed effect is below the pre-agreed practical threshold; the economic illustration does not justify the assumed quarterly implementation cost; the planned recruitment target has not been documented as met; and guardrail data are unspecified. This is not a conclusion that B is harmful or that its true effect is zero. It is a conclusion that the presently documented evidence is insufficient for the stated launch rule. The distinction between “not proven enough” and “proven ineffective” matters in responsible experimentation.

The next action should follow the protocol rather than the attractiveness of the observed p-value. Reconcile 2,100 planned versus 2,000 mature accounts per arm; check whether the planned period ended, all final outcomes matured, and whether all pre-assignment eligible units are represented. Inspect SRM and data lineage. Obtain guardrail measurements and their uncertainty. If the test was stopped early for an unplanned reason, label the analysis exploratory and determine whether a new, prospectively planned experiment is needed. If the cost model changes, document a new business decision calculation without rewriting the historical experimental plan.

A decision can legitimately incorporate nonstatistical information, such as compliance requirements or deployment risk, but should not be disguised as a consequence of p<0.05. The most durable experiment result is often a clearly bounded finding about the population, metric, and time studied, together with an explicit statement about what remains unknown. Such a report supports learning even when the launch is deferred.

Snapshot decision, not a certified rollout: defer until protocol completion and guardrails are verified.

20. Further reasoning: false positives are not the only error

Type I error occurs when the test rejects a true null; Type II error occurs when it fails to reject a false null. Power is one minus the Type II error probability for a specified alternative. At a fixed α and variability, a larger sample can reveal smaller nonzero effects; this can increase statistical significance without increasing practical value. The operational danger is not merely false discovery; it is choosing actions based on an effect that is too small, too short-lived, or measured with the wrong denominator. Conversely, insisting on a significant effect in every tiny subgroup can discard a beneficial treatment because subgroup analyses have poor power.

A confidence interval links the two concerns. Its position relative to zero addresses the point null; its position relative to the practical threshold addresses decision relevance. For a positive-effect launch requirement Δ>0.03, the ordinary two-sided interval [0.004,0.046] is ambiguous because part lies below and part above 0.03. To show Δ>0.03 at a one-sided 5% standard, the lower one-sided bound must exceed 0.03; here it clearly does not. A one-sided procedure must be chosen prospectively, not selected after a two-sided result looks unhelpful. Statistical and business thresholds can both be prespecified without conflating them.

\[H_0:\Delta=0\qquad\text{versus decision requirement }\Delta>0.03\]

21. Further reasoning: measurement changes can change the estimand

Imagine that the treatment B simplifies form validation, so users who formerly failed to register now complete, but many of those completions contain invalid email addresses. If the primary metric counts raw completion events, B could genuinely increase its measured value and simultaneously reduce the quantity the organization actually values: valid, contactable accounts. The solution is not to claim the experimental result was mathematically wrong. The solution is to ask whether the chosen outcome represented the intended decision quantity. Measurement validity is a separate step from the correctness of the variance formula.

A complementary metric might be “valid registration within seven days,” defined using a validation rule applied identically to both arms. If invented after looking at the outcome and used as the only success criterion, it would not be confirmatory under the original charter. It may nevertheless motivate a follow-up experiment with a better endpoint. This is a general lesson from experimentation: randomization can identify causal effects on what was measured, not necessarily on what the product team ought to care about.

22. Further reasoning: the counterfactual launch population

The estimated ITT effect pertains to the eligible accounts randomized in the experimental period. Future users may differ in geography, acquisition source, season, device mix or learning behavior. If B will be launched only to existing customers, but the experiment involved newly exposed accounts, the experiment answers a different population question. Likewise, a novelty effect during the first week may differ from sustained behavior after three months. None of these problems is fixed by making the original confidence interval narrower.

An analyst can predefine a target population and use weighting or stratified transportability analyses under stated assumptions. But extrapolation requires information about how treatment effects vary with relevant characteristics and how those characteristics are distributed in the target setting. The presence of random assignment in the experiment does not randomize the choice of who appears in the study. A clear report states the sampled population, the intended deployment population, and the reasons they may differ. Where external validity is uncertain, a staged rollout with prospective monitoring can be more responsible than an unconditional launch claim.

23. Further reasoning: exposure, noncompliance, and treatment dilution

If some users assigned B see A due to caching, the ITT contrast estimates the effect of assignment under that imperfect delivery system. It may be smaller than the causal effect of actually receiving B, but it remains relevant to a decision about implementing the actual assignment policy. One should not simply remove users with failed delivery to “recover” the true effect. Delivery failure may depend on device, geography or behavior, turning a receipt-based subset into a nonrandom group. The observed assignment contrast can still be valid for a policy effect even if imperfect adherence reduces the impact.

More advanced methods can estimate effects among compliers under additional assumptions such as exclusion restrictions, monotonicity and a valid instrument, but those methods fall outside the central scope of this guide. The key lesson is to define which effect the business needs. “If we assign B in production” is different from “if every person fully experiences B.” The first aligns naturally with ITT. Document noncompliance rates as implementation quality and interpret the point estimate against the policy that produced it.

24. Further reasoning: sample size is a design choice, not an observed result

The formula for n depends on a guessed baseline, alternative, desired power and testing convention. Suppose the true baseline is 20% rather than 12%; variance p(1−p) rises in this range and the same three-point absolute effect may require a different sample. Conversely, a stronger effect may be detected earlier in a fixed design, but that does not authorize abandoning the stop rule for a favorable interim p-value. Sample-size planning is an exercise in explicit assumptions, not a guarantee of any particular eventual inference.

The planning number of 2,036 per arm is approximate. A choice of 2,100 per arm should be justified against forecast dropout, scheduling needs and whether a safety metric is rarer than completion. If the primary target is met but the important guardrail has only ten events, the primary experiment may be precise while the risk assessment remains weak. Planning a trustworthy decision may therefore require a larger sample or longer follow-up than planning a significant efficacy test. Making this trade-off visible is part of competent experimental design.

\[n\propto\frac{1}{\Delta^2}\quad\text{when other design quantities are comparable}\]

25. Further reasoning: sequential methods require their own mathematics

It is tempting to say “we will peek every day and just use a more conservative p-value.” But a valid sequential rule must account for when and how many looks occur and what decision boundaries are applied. A group-sequential design might spend Type I error over a fixed set of interim analyses, allowing early stopping for compelling evidence with a corrected final decision boundary. An always-valid method might control errors under a broader class of stopping times by different mathematical constructions. These are not interchangeable labels, and their interpretation depends on the exact procedure.

This guide does not derive those boundaries, implement an e-process, or claim that a conventional z-test is safe under optional stopping. The practical bridge for a beginner is to preserve the fixed horizon, define the sample and finish date before observing outcomes, and avoid converting exploratory intermediate results into the primary claim. When sequential experimentation is an actual product requirement, involve a qualified statistician in designing the method before launch and document its stopping guarantees. Honesty about scope is better than a sophisticated word used without its conditions.

26. Exercises and complete solutions: reconstruct, decide, transfer

The next twelve exercises are not an answer key detached from the lesson. Each solution restates the relevant denominator, explains the method, carries out the essential arithmetic, and names a limitation. Work through the prompt before reading its solution; a correct number without a reason for the denominator or threshold is not sufficient understanding.

Exercise 1 — Compute the absolute and relative effects

Task. Use 240 completions from 2,000 A accounts and 290 from 2,000 B accounts. Find both rates, absolute effect in pp and relative lift. Solution. Divide 240/2,000=0.120 and 290/2,000=0.145. Subtract 0.145−0.120=0.025=2.5 pp. Divide the difference by the A rate, 0.025/0.120=0.20833=20.83%. The absolute change describes extra completers per eligible account; the relative change describes proportional improvement over baseline. They are not alternative units for the same displayed number.

Exercise 2 — Rebuild the null test

Task. Test equal completion probabilities using the fixed-horizon pooled z statistic. Solution. Under equality, pool the successes: (240+290)/(2,000+2,000)=0.1325. Compute SE₀=sqrt(0.1325×0.8675×(1/2,000+1/2,000))≈0.01072. Then z=(0.145−0.120)/0.01072≈2.332. The two-tailed standard-normal area beyond |z| is ≈0.0197. Because 0.0197<0.05, the conventional test rejects equality if the design and fixed analysis are valid. It does not imply the probability the null is true equals 1.97%.

Exercise 3 — Construct and interpret a confidence interval

Task. Produce an approximate 95% interval for B−A. Solution. For the estimated difference, calculate unpooled SE=sqrt(0.12×0.88/2,000+0.145×0.855/2,000)≈0.010714. The margin is 1.95996×0.010714≈0.0210. Subtract and add to 0.025: [0.0040,0.0460], or [0.40,4.60] pp. Repeated use of this procedure has approximate 95% coverage under suitable sampling assumptions. The range includes effects too small to reach the three-pp practical target.

Exercise 4 — Detect sample ratio mismatch

Task. Planned counts are 2,000 and 2,000, but observed assignment counts are 2,200 and 1,800. Compute the chi-square diagnostic. Solution. Deviations are +200 and −200. χ²=200²/2,000+(−200)²/2,000=20+20=40 on one degree of freedom, giving p≈2.54×10⁻¹⁰. This strongly flags allocation or logging integrity for investigation. It cannot identify the responsible bug by itself, and a clean outcome test should not proceed as though the flag were irrelevant.

Exercise 5 — Avoid a post-assignment denominator

Task. B has 2,000 assigned accounts, only 1,800 fully load the page, and 290 complete registration. Compare the ITT rate with the exposed-only rate. Solution. ITT uses 290/2,000=14.5% because assignment defines the groups. Exposed-only gives 290/1,800≈16.11%, a different conditional population. Restricting to exposed accounts can select on post-assignment behavior and bias a causal comparison. Record delivery failure separately and retain the originally randomized denominator for the policy effect, assuming outcomes are observable.

Exercise 6 — Reconstruct the sample-size plan

Task. With p_A=.12, p_B=.15, two-sided α=.05 and 80% power, approximate n per arm. Solution. Set p-bar=.135; use normal quantiles 1.95996 and .84162. Numerator = [1.95996sqrt(2×.135×.865)+.84162sqrt(.12×.88+.15×.85)]². Divide by (.03)²=.0009 to obtain n≈2,036 per arm. Rounding to 2,100 gives a feasible target but does not automatically protect against more than a few percent unusable data or unusual traffic mix. State assumptions before observing outcomes.

Exercise 7 — Explain significance versus practical value

Task. The two-sided test rejects at 5%, but the planned worthwhile lift is three pp. Is launch established? Solution. No. The point estimate is 2.5 pp, below three; the interval [0.4,4.6] pp crosses that benchmark. A conventional test against zero asks whether evidence supports any nonzero difference, not whether the effect is large enough for rollout. A launch decision also needs measured guardrails, completion of the experiment plan, costs and external validity. The analysis provides favorable evidence for a positive short-term effect without proving the practical requirement.

Exercise 8 — Evaluate an economic claim

Task. Assume 100,000 quarterly eligible accounts, $2 per extra valid registration and a $25,000 quarterly incremental cost. Use the observed 2.5-pp effect. Solution. Estimated additional registrations =100,000×.025=2,500. At $2 each the incremental contribution is $5,000; subtract $25,000 for a −$20,000 estimated quarterly net. This is a hypothetical scenario, not a measured treatment effect on profit. Break-even requires 25,000/($2×100,000)=.125, or 12.5 pp, under these simplified economics. The test has not measured validity or longer-term revenue, so the assumption should be explicit.

Exercise 9 — Identify an invalid early stop

Task. An analyst checks nominal p-values daily and stops on the first day with p<.05. Can the final conventional p-value be interpreted at the ordinary fixed-horizon α? Solution. Not generally. The chance of ever crossing the threshold under the null differs from the chance at a prespecified single look, so the stopping-based selection changes Type I error. Either adhere to a fixed horizon or prospectively adopt a valid sequential procedure and its boundaries. Calling the daily test “exploratory” is appropriate when no valid sequential plan was in place.

Exercise 10 — Interpret a missing guardrail

Task. No counts for technical errors have been supplied. The efficacy p-value is .0197. Can we conclude B is safe? Solution. No: statistical evidence for one endpoint says nothing about an unmeasured endpoint. A non-significant guardrail result would also be insufficient to prove safety unless a suitable margin and precision were established. The report should say “technical-error guardrail: NOT AVAILABLE,” identify what must be collected and state that a launch authorization depending on safety cannot be completed yet.

Exercise 11 — Choose the experimental unit

Task. A service randomizes page views, but each person loads five pages and tends to stay in one behavioral pattern. Is the view count the right independent sample size? Solution. No. Multiple views from a single person are correlated; the view is an event, not necessarily an independently randomized causal unit. Assign at a stable user or account level, or account for clustering if the design truly randomizes larger units. Explain who can affect whom and prevent cross-arm contamination. Increasing view count without independent units can make standard errors implausibly small.

Exercise 12 — Write a defensible recommendation

Task. Summarize the experiment for a product manager, including uncertainty, the three-pp threshold, missing guardrails and the planned 2,100-per-arm sample. Solution. “In the synthetic mature-outcome snapshot, B has 14.5% completion against 12.0% for A: +2.5 pp, approximate 95% CI +0.4 to +4.6 pp, fixed-horizon z-test p≈0.0197. This suggests a positive short-term assignment effect under assumptions but does not establish the planned three-point minimum. Only 2,000 per arm appear in the snapshot rather than the target 2,100, and guardrail outcomes have not been supplied. Do not authorize full rollout on this record; first reconcile protocol completion, missingness and safety metrics. Revisit the decision using the preregistered rule and transparent economics.” The report separates observed evidence from required follow-up instead of treating a single significant p-value as a verdict.

27. Student understanding test and final synthesis

A reader who can calculate the rates, reconstruct the pooled and unpooled standard errors, explain SRM and ITT, write a valid fixed-horizon plan, and distinguish statistical evidence from economic sufficiency has done more than memorize test terminology. The most difficult conceptual bridge is that randomization permits a credible causal contrast for the assigned units and defined outcome, whereas a product decision may require stronger evidence about benefit, cost, future users, and adverse side effects. A/B testing is therefore an applied decision discipline rather than a machine that turns p-values into “wins.”

Before signing a report, ask: What exactly was randomized? Who was excluded and when? Was the outcome window complete? Was the primary metric chosen before results? Does the interval rule out effects too small to matter? What guardrails were actually measured? Was the stopping rule respected? Can someone reproduce all displayed counts and formulas? Which parts of the recommendation are data-supported, and which depend on assumed future volumes or costs? These questions are a reproducible final checklist, not an invitation to rewrite the protocol after a disappointing outcome.

The question that guides our release review is: “If I were a student learning this topic for the first time, could I follow every step, reproduce the calculations, and explain in my own words why it works?” This document attempts to support that through a complete synthetic case and fully worked solutions; it does not claim that actual students have been tested. The next step for the learner is to draft a new preregistered charter for a different service and defend its population, horizon, decision thresholds and integrity checks.
