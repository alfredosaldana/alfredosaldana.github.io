01. The causal question must come before the statistical test

A grower wants to know whether a new fertilizer increases the yield of a crop. A spreadsheet containing yields from fertilized and unfertilized fields is not enough to settle that question. Higher-yield fields might already have better soil, better irrigation or more sunlight. Comparing their observed averages can mix the effect of fertilizer with those pre-existing differences. A randomized experiment attempts to break the systematic relationship between treatment assignment and the features that would otherwise influence an outcome. This is a design strategy, not a magical property of a significance test.

Our illustrative case has sixteen separate agricultural plots, grouped into four soil blocks. A plot is the experimental unit because fertilizer is applied independently to that entire plot. Each block contains four plots. Before observing any final yields, a random allocation selects two plots per block to receive the new fertilizer (T) and two to receive the existing standard fertilizer (C). The outcome is harvested yield in kilograms per plot during one common growing season. All sixteen records will be displayed; the numbers are deliberately synthetic and are not measurements from a farm. The design is an illustration rather than evidence about a real fertilizer.

A good scientific question specifies the population, intervention, comparison, outcome and time horizon. Here the question is: for these eligible plots and this season, what would be the average difference in harvested kilograms if a plot received the new fertilizer rather than standard fertilizer? The experimental design seeks to answer that causal question under explicit assumptions. The statistical test arrives later, after we understand what was assigned, how, and to whom.

02. Prerequisites, units and the meaning of randomization

The essential prerequisites are elementary probability, sample means, subtraction, a basic idea of uncertainty and the distinction between association and causation. A little algebra is enough to reproduce most calculations. We introduce the idea of Potential Outcomes here: it is extra causal vocabulary, not a requirement to have taken an advanced causal-inference course. A potential outcome is the yield a plot would produce under a specified treatment assignment; we will define its notation before using it.

A treatment is a well-defined condition deliberately assigned by the investigator. Our treatment is a specific new fertilizer administered at a fixed dose, not the vague instruction “improve farming.” The control is the established fertilizer, not a plot that receives no attention. A replicate is a distinct experimental unit exposed to a treatment. Measuring one plot on five days does not turn that plot into five independently assigned units. The outcome is a measured response whose units and measurement timing must be prespecified.

Randomization means using a genuine chance procedure to choose the allocation among known eligible assignments. It does not mean selecting whichever plots look mixed after the fact, alternating by convenience, or calling a pre-existing dataset “randomized.” The allocation mechanism defines which assignments could have occurred and with what probability. That mechanism is the foundation of the exact randomization test later in the guide. Random assignment can make treatment groups comparable in expectation, but a single random assignment need not produce perfectly equal baseline characteristics.

03. Distinguish the unit of assignment, observation and analysis

Core equation · verify the design
\[N_{\mathrm{experimental}}=16,\qquad N_{\mathrm{plantas\ medidas}}\ne N_{\mathrm{asignadas}}\]

The most consequential design choice is identifying the experimental unit before allocation. In our example each plot has an individual treatment label, so there are sixteen experimental units. A yield sensor could report values every minute, and a scientist could sample many plants from each plot, but those are measurements within the same plot, not additional independent assignments. Treating them as though sixteen plots had somehow become thousands of plots is pseudoreplication. It makes uncertainty appear artificially small because neighboring measurements share soil, fertilizer and weather.

There are three related concepts. The assignment unit is the smallest entity whose treatment can be independently randomized. The measurement unit is where observations are collected. The analysis unit is the entity contributing independent or appropriately modeled evidence to the estimator. Ideally they match, but they need not. A health trial might randomize clinics, observe patients and fit a model allowing patients within a clinic to be correlated. The clinic remains the assignment unit regardless of the number of patients measured.

Suppose the sixteen plots each contain ten plants. A naive analysis that treats 160 plant yields as 160 randomized units is wrong if the ten plants on a plot all receive the same fertilizer. A valid simple approach is to summarize the ten plants into one plot-level yield measure and analyze sixteen plots. A more elaborate hierarchical model can use plant-level data but must preserve the common-plot dependence. The practical question is always: what was actually independently randomized? State that answer alongside the reported sample size.

05. Blocks are formed before assignment, not after seeing results

A block is a group of experimental units alike with respect to a pre-treatment feature strongly related to the outcome. Here the feature is soil quality. Blocking addresses a design problem: environmental variation may overwhelm a relatively modest treatment effect. By comparing treatment and control plots within similar soil conditions, we remove much of the irrelevant between-block variation from the contrast. The block definition must be determined using information available before treatment outcomes are seen.

The scheme is straightforward: establish four soil blocks A–D; label four eligible plots within each; use a random mechanism to select exactly two of the four labels for treatment; assign control to the other two; document the selection and conceal upcoming assignments from anyone able to manipulate enrollment. This is stratified or blocked randomization, not a claim that every plot in the farm has the same probability distribution of yields. Each eligible plot has a 2/4 = 1/2 marginal probability of receiving T under the planned allocation.

Blocking is beneficial when within-block outcomes are more homogeneous than outcomes from different blocks. If soil quality does not predict yield, blocking may offer little gain and uses design complexity without corresponding value. Blocks with no treatment or no control do not provide a direct within-block comparison. Selecting blocks after looking at harvests can create bias and invalidates a naive design-based randomization analysis. A transparent protocol records which variables defined the blocks, the number of units in each, and the assignment probabilities.

06. How many assignments were genuinely possible?

Core equation · verify the design
\[\boxed{\displaystyle |\Omega|=\left(\binom42\right)^4=6^4=1296}\]

Each block has four labeled plots and exactly two receive treatment. The number of possible treatment pairs is the binomial coefficient \(\binom{4}{2}=4!/(2!2!)=6\). If each of these six pairs is selected with equal probability, a particular within-block pair has probability 1/6. Selections are independently made across the four blocks, yielding \(6\times6\times6\times6=6^4=1296\) possible complete assignments. Each complete assignment has probability 1/1296 under this design.

The six choices for four plots labeled 1, 2, 3, 4 are {1,2}, {1,3}, {1,4}, {2,3}, {2,4} and {3,4}. We are enumerating assignments, not inventing new observed agricultural datasets. In a randomization test under a sharp null, the sixteen observed outcomes can be kept fixed while these possible labels are permuted. That logic would be wrong if the actual researcher allocated the first two plots to treatment in every block without random selection; then there would not be six equally likely choices.

A useful design check is to calculate the inclusion probability of one plot in treatment: it appears in three of the six pairs, hence 3/6 = 1/2. The treatment assignment indicators are not independent within a block because exactly two plots must be treated. If plot 1 is assigned T, only one of the other three can also receive T. This dependence is intentional and must be preserved by any randomization simulation or exact test.

07. Calculate within-block treatment contrasts without shortcuts

Core equation · verify the design
\[\begin{aligned}\widehat\tau_B&=\frac14\sum_{b=A}^{D}(\bar Y_{Tb}-\bar Y_{Cb})\\&=\frac{2+4+1+5}{4}=\boxed{3\ \mathrm{kg/plot}}\end{aligned}\]

Define \(\bar Y_{Tb}\) as the mean observed yield among treated plots in block \(b\), and \(\bar Y_{Cb}\) as the mean control yield. The block contrast is \(d_b=\bar Y_{Tb}-\bar Y_{Cb}\), in kilograms per plot. Compute the four contrasts in order. Block A: (11+13)/2 − (9+11)/2 = 12−10=2 kg. Block B: (23+25)/2 − (19+21)/2 = 24−20=4 kg. Block C: (30+32)/2 − (29+31)/2 = 31−30=1 kg. Block D: (44+46)/2 − (39+41)/2 =45−40=5 kg.

There are four equally sized blocks, each containing four eligible plots, so the natural blocked estimator weights the four contrasts equally. Sum the differences: 2+4+1+5=12 kg. Divide by four: \(\widehat\tau_B=12/4=3\) kilograms per plot. The estimator is a difference in observed means under a randomized design, not an observed treatment effect for any particular plot. We never saw what the same plot would yield under both fertilizers.

Interpretation: in the observed allocation, treated plots outyield controls by an average of three kilograms within matched soil-quality blocks. That comparison is consistent with a positive causal effect under the design assumptions. It is not proof that every plot benefits by three kilograms, or that a different season or farm will show the same result. The block effects range from 1 to 5 kg, so the observed heterogeneity deserves discussion rather than being erased by the average.

08. Verify the result using the pooled observed means

Core equation · verify the design
\[\bar Y_T-\bar Y_C=\frac{224}{8}-\frac{200}{8}=28-25=3\ \mathrm{kg/plot}\]

We can verify the arithmetic another way because all blocks are the same size and have two plots in each arm. The eight observed controls total \((9+11)+(19+21)+(29+31)+(39+41)=20+40+60+80=200\) kg, so their mean is 200/8=25 kg. The eight treated outcomes total \((11+13)+(23+25)+(30+32)+(44+46)=24+48+62+90=224\) kg, so their mean is 224/8=28 kg. The pooled observed difference is 28−25=3 kg, matching the blocked estimate.

This equality is a consequence of the equal block sizes and equal treatment fractions, not a universal identity. If block A had four plots and block D had forty, the average of block contrasts should ordinarily be weighted according to the target population or sampling design. Averaging the four block differences equally would answer a different question: the average effect across blocks considered equally important, instead of the average effect across all eligible plots.

The equality of these two point estimates does not mean blocking is useless. Blocking changes the set of legal random assignments and the appropriate uncertainty calculation. The pooled difference also happens to work as the point estimate here because of symmetry; an unblocked standard error that ignores the planned soil blocks may waste precision or mischaracterize the design. Distinguish a point estimate, an uncertainty estimate, and the allocation mechanism—they play different roles.

09. Potential Outcomes turn the causal question into a precise estimand

Core equation · verify the design
\[\begin{aligned}\tau_i&=Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\\Y_i^{\mathrm{obs}}&=Z_iY_i(1)+(1-Z_i)Y_i(0)\\\tau&=\frac1{16}\sum_{i=1}^{16}\tau_i\end{aligned}\]

For each plot \(i\), define \(Y_i(1)\) as the kilograms it would produce if assigned the new fertilizer and \(Y_i(0)\) as the kilograms if assigned standard fertilizer. The individual causal effect is \(\tau_i=Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\). Only one of those two values is observed for each plot: \(Y_i=Z_iY_i(1)+(1-Z_i)Y_i(0)\), where assignment indicator \(Z_i\) is 1 for treatment and 0 for control. This is the fundamental missing counterfactual problem.

A scientifically useful estimand is the finite-population average treatment effect across the sixteen eligible plots: \(\tau=\frac1{16}\sum_{i=1}^{16}[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)]\). Its units are kilograms per plot. The random allocation supplies information about this quantity even though individual effects cannot generally be recovered. An alternative estimand might focus on the average effect among an identified soil type or the effect on the proportion of plots exceeding a yield threshold. Such alternatives require explicit definition, not a silent change after results appear.

The potential-outcomes notation demands assumptions. The treatment must have a coherent meaning; measured yield under assignment must correspond to the defined condition; and a plot's potential yield should not change because its neighboring plot was assigned a different treatment. This last requirement is no interference. When fertilizer runs off into adjacent plots, interference may invalidate the simple two-outcome representation. The experiment's engineering and layout must therefore support the causal assumptions, not merely its statistical calculations.

10. Why random assignment identifies an average effect in expectation

Core equation · verify the design
\[\mathbb E_Z(\widehat\tau_B)=\tau\quad\text{under the stated blocked randomization}\]

Think of each eligible plot as possessing two fixed potential outcomes before randomization, although we cannot observe both. The chance mechanism selects which one becomes visible. Because every plot has known equal assignment probability within its block, the treated group is not deliberately selected for high or low untreated potential yield. Under this randomization distribution, the expected treated mean within block equals the average of all its treated potential outcomes, and the expected control mean equals the average of all its control potential outcomes.

Subtract those expectations. The expected within-block observed contrast is the block's average causal effect; the expected weighted blocked estimator is the corresponding weighted average of block effects. This is design-based unbiasedness, a property across hypothetical repetitions of the assignment process while holding plots and their potential outcomes fixed. It is not a guarantee that the one assignment we observed equals the true effect. Uncertainty comes from which potential outcomes randomization made visible.

Randomization does not ensure perfect soil balance within each realized sample, fix missing harvest measurements, neutralize spillovers, or establish representativeness of all farms. It controls confounding by the assignment mechanism for the units randomized, provided the design is implemented honestly and outcomes are not selectively observed. Describing the estimand and the design conditions is therefore more informative than saying only that a trial was randomized.

11. Separate observed outcome variation from uncertainty about treatment

Core equation · verify the design
\[\begin{aligned}s_d^2&=\frac{(-1)^2+1^2+(-2)^2+2^2}{4-1}=\frac{10}{3}\\\operatorname{SE}_{\rm blocks}&=\frac{\sqrt{10/3}}{\sqrt4}\approx0.9129\end{aligned}\]

The block-level differences are 2, 4, 1 and 5 kg, with mean 3 kg. Their deviations are −1, 1, −2 and 2 kg. Square these to obtain 1,1,4,4; their sum is 10 kg². The sample variance across four observed block contrasts is \(s_d^2=10/(4-1)=10/3\) kg². The sample standard deviation is \(\sqrt{10/3}\approx1.826\) kg; the standard error of an equally weighted mean over four independently sampled blocks would be \(s_d/\sqrt4\approx0.913\) kg.

That last standard error answers a superpopulation of blocks question under an additional model in which the four block contrasts behave like independent observations of a broader population of blocks. It is not automatically the exact finite-population randomization standard error for these sixteen fixed plots. Distinguish those interpretations rather than mixing their denominators. With only four blocks a normal approximation could be unreliable, and a paired-t reference would need assumptions about the block-level differences.

For design-based uncertainty, we instead consider variability induced by the allowed assignments within each fixed block. This can be estimated from within-arm dispersion but involves an unobserved covariance of potential outcomes, so common variance formulas are conservative under particular conditions. The next section supplies a transparent, small-sample alternative for testing a specific sharp null without treating repeated plant measurements as independent units.

12. A design-compatible exact randomization test

Core equation · verify the design
\[\boxed{\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{exact,2s}}=\frac{\#\{z\in\Omega:|T(z)|\ge3\}}{|\Omega|}=\frac8{1296}=0.00617284}\]

Consider the sharp null hypothesis that the fertilizer changes no plot's yield: \(H_0:Y_i(1)=Y_i(0)\) for every one of the sixteen plots. Under this very strong null, the observed yields are the yields that would have appeared under any permitted assignment. We can therefore hold the sixteen numbers fixed and recompute the blocked mean difference for each of the \(6^4=1296\) legal assignments. The test statistic is \(T=\tfrac14\sum_b(\bar Y_{Tb}-\bar Y_{Cb})\), and the observed statistic is 3 kg.

The randomization distribution is symmetric around zero because complementing all treatment labels changes every difference's sign. An exact two-sided tail count asks how many assignments give \(|T|\ge|3|\). Enumeration finds 8 assignments (including the actual arrangement and suitably extreme alternatives), so the p-value is \(8/1296=1/162\approx0.00617284\). For a prespecified one-sided positive alternative the count is four assignments and the p-value is \(4/1296\approx0.00308642\). Choosing a one-sided alternative only after seeing the positive outcome would be misleading.

A p-value here is a probability computed under the sharp no-effect null and the specified allocation procedure, not the probability the null is true. It tests whether the observed allocation would be unusually aligned with high outcomes if treatment had no effect on any plot. It does not give a 99.38% probability that fertilizer works, nor prove a particular average effect size. Because the example is synthetic, the numerical result teaches the procedure rather than establishing agricultural efficacy.

13. Why the sharp null differs from the average-effect null

Core equation · verify the design
\[H_{0,\mathrm{sharp}}:\ \tau_i=0\ \forall i\qquad\ne\qquad H_{0,\mathrm{average}}:\ \frac1N\sum_i\tau_i=0\]

The sharp null asserts every individual causal effect is zero. A weaker average-effect null asserts only \(\frac1{16}\sum_i \tau_i=0\). The latter allows some plots to benefit and others to be harmed, with positive and negative effects canceling. Under that weak null we generally cannot reconstruct the outcomes that would appear under every alternative assignment, because the unobserved potential outcome for each plot remains unknown. Naively permuting observed yields as if all potential outcomes were identical does not give an exact test of the weak null in general.

This distinction matters for interpretation. Rejecting the sharp null tells us the observed data are incompatible with the hypothesis of no effect for anyone under the randomization mechanism; it does not, by itself, deliver an exact confidence interval for the finite-population average effect. Inversion of randomization tests for a constant additive effect can generate an interval under that stronger model. More general average-effect inference may require Neyman-style variance estimation, asymptotic arguments, or specialized studentized randomization procedures.

A simple counterexample makes the difference visible. Suppose one plot has effect +2 kg and another −2 kg, while the other fourteen have effect zero. The average effect equals zero but the sharp no-effect null is false. A learner should be able to explain this contrast without calculation. Always name which null a test actually addresses; a formula that looks familiar can answer a different scientific question if its assumptions change.

14. Precision, blocking and why a nonrandom control is weaker

Why not simply place all new fertilizer on the fertile half of the farm and compare it with the less fertile half? The answer is confounding: fertilizer and soil quality would be perfectly aligned, so the yield difference could not be attributed to either without strong additional assumptions. Randomization within each known soil block breaks that deliberate alignment. Blocking does not make plots identical; it makes comparisons occur within pre-treatment categories already known to influence yield.

Consider a hypothetical untreated yield structure in which blocks differ by about ten kilograms in average productivity while plot-to-plot differences within a block are only a few kilograms. An overall comparison dominated by which blocks happen to receive treatment is noisy. A blocked contrast subtracts within each band before averaging, canceling the common block-level baseline. The plot outcome remains measured in kilograms; the block contrast measures the incremental treatment-associated yield beyond that baseline. This is the core intuition for a precision improvement.

A control is more than an empty label. It needs a protocol comparable with treatment in all respects except the intervention of interest: same plot size, planting date, water measurements, harvest procedure and follow-up. If treatment plots are watered more often because staff pay them extra attention, the experiment estimates a package of changes, not the isolated fertilizer effect originally proposed. Control protocols and co-interventions must be described before the experiment is run.

15. Blinding, allocation concealment and measurement bias

Allocation concealment means that the person enrolling or placing units cannot foresee the upcoming assignment. It protects randomization from intentional or unintentional selection of favorable units. Blinding means people involved in treatment, measurement or analysis do not know the assigned condition after allocation when that is feasible. They are related but distinct: one protects the allocation mechanism, while the other often protects treatment delivery and outcome assessment.

In the agricultural example, visually identical fertilizer containers labeled by a separate coordinator may help field technicians avoid treating one arm differently. If one fertilizer has an unmistakable smell, full blinding of applicators may be impossible. However, a harvest assessor could still measure yield from coded plots without knowing allocation. The researcher should report exactly who was blinded and how, rather than claiming the study was simply “double-blind” without specifying roles.

Blinding is particularly important for subjective outcomes, but it can matter for objectively measured outcomes if handling, exclusions or timing are discretionary. A kilogram scale reduces discretion, but workers might still select where to harvest or exclude damaged plants differently across arms. Concealment does not repair flawed random sequences; blinding does not make a convenience allocation genuinely random. Both mechanisms belong in the design record and in a reproducible trial report.

16. Balance checks: what they can and cannot tell us

Random allocation makes treatment independent of pre-treatment potential outcomes in the assignment distribution. It does not require every realized treatment group to have identical soil moisture, plot width or pre-season yield. Some imbalance will occur by chance, especially with only sixteen plots. Inspecting a baseline table is useful to understand the realized sample and detect data or implementation errors; a p-value testing baseline imbalance is not a prerequisite to trusting a genuine random mechanism.

The pre-specified blocks guarantee equal numbers treated and controlled within each soil category. They do not guarantee identical moisture within each category or equal baseline yield for every pair. A poorly conducted allocation can also happen to look balanced, so balance cannot substitute for records showing how the random sequence was generated and protected. If the randomization log is missing, observed balance alone is insufficient to reconstruct assignment probabilities.

Analytically adjusting for important pre-treatment covariates can sometimes improve precision, provided the adjustment is compatible with the randomization design and its uncertainty analysis. But choosing covariates solely because their baseline p-values are small encourages instability. Better to prespecify strongly prognostic variables and report both the design-based primary analysis and relevant sensitivity checks. Adjustment cannot repair unrecorded spillovers or systematic missing outcomes automatically.

17. Noncompliance changes the causal question

Core equation · verify the design
\[\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{ITT}}=\overline Y_{Z=1}-\overline Y_{Z=0}\]

So far every plot was assumed to receive its assigned fertilizer. Real projects can have noncompliance: a treated plot is accidentally given standard fertilizer, a control plot receives the new fertilizer, or a dose is incomplete. We must distinguish randomized assignment from actual receipt. Denote assignment by \(Z\) and received treatment by \(D\). When they differ, comparing people or plots by \(D\) may reintroduce confounding because adherence can depend on health, motivation, access or field conditions.

An Intention-to-Treat (ITT) analysis compares outcomes by the original assignment \(Z\), regardless of subsequent receipt. The ITT estimand is the effect of offering or assigning the intervention in this experimental system. It generally differs from the effect of fully receiving treatment when adherence is imperfect. Preserving assignment groups protects the comparability created by randomization, although missing outcomes still create a separate challenge.

For a concrete illustration, suppose eight units are assigned treatment and eight control. Even if only six of the eight assigned-treated units receive the intended fertilizer, the main ITT contrast groups all eight by their original assignment. Excluding two nonadherent units from the treatment denominator would break the randomized comparison. Estimating an effect among compliers can require additional assumptions such as an exclusion restriction, monotonicity and an instrumental-variable framework; those results are not automatic consequences of random assignment.

18. Missing outcomes and loss to follow-up

A plot can fail to yield a measurement because of equipment damage, theft, flooding or a lost record. If a person drops out of a clinical trial, the analogous problem is loss to follow-up. A randomized assignment does not determine why an outcome is missing. If missingness depends on unobserved yield and treatment, simply comparing remaining observed plots can produce a biased estimate despite flawless initial randomization.

Consider the main example with all sixteen outcomes observed. Now imagine that the highest-yield treatment plot in block D has no harvest record. Removing that plot from the calculation changes the observed treatment mean, destroys its intended two-versus-two block comparison and can conceal an outcome-related missingness problem. Treating its value as zero is also a substantive assumption, not a neutral arithmetic convenience. A complete-case comparison may be defensible under a suitable missingness mechanism, but the mechanism must be discussed.

A responsible protocol attempts to prevent missingness, records the number assigned and observed in each arm, explains each missing outcome where possible, and conducts sensitivity analyses for departures from the primary assumptions. Imputation is not a magic repair and should not use information that would not have been available under the proposed model. This distinction appears in CONSORT 2025 reporting guidance: prespecify the analysis population and explain handling of missing outcomes and deviations from treatment assignment.

19. Post-treatment variables are not ordinary baseline confounders

An analyst may measure leaf greenness one month after fertilizer application. Greenness might be a mediator: treatment changes greenness, which changes harvested yield. Regressing harvested yield on assignment while controlling for greenness may block part of the very effect we want to estimate. If greenness is also affected by unmeasured disease, conditioning on it can introduce other forms of bias. Thus adjusting for a variable is not automatically good simply because it predicts yield.

Separate pre-treatment covariates (soil quality, planned plot area, baseline moisture) from post-treatment quantities (new greenness, early plant growth, later irrigation decisions influenced by treatment). For the total causal effect of assignment, a straightforward ITT contrast ordinarily should not adjust for downstream mediators by routine. A direct-effect question would require a different estimand and assumptions; one cannot silently reinterpret the regression coefficient as the total effect.

Randomization identifies an assignment effect under its conditions; it does not randomize post-assignment mediators. A reader should draw the sequence assignment → greenness → yield and ask which arrows would be blocked by adjustment. The practical discipline is to prepare a timeline and causal diagram before selecting analysis variables. This prevents a well-intended precision adjustment from changing the scientific target.

20. Cluster randomization and interference need their own analysis

Suppose fertilizer cannot be confined to individual plots because irrigation carries nutrients across plot borders. Instead, the farm might randomize entire irrigation zones to one fertilizer. The zone becomes the experimental unit, while plots within the zone are nested observations. Randomization of four zones is not equivalent to independent randomization of forty plots inside those zones. Individual outcomes in the same zone can share weather, application schedule, soil and intervention spillovers.

Cluster allocation can reduce interference across assigned units if zones are physically separated, but analysis must reflect the number and structure of independently assigned clusters. A naive standard error based on all plot observations as independent would exaggerate precision. Cluster-robust methods, randomization inference at the cluster level or appropriate hierarchical models may be used, but with very few clusters they can perform poorly and need special caution.

Another design complication is partial interference: units may affect one another within a zone but not across zones. That requires explicit potential-outcome notation allowing outcomes to depend on more than an individual's own assignment, or a policy-level estimand for the whole zone. Our sixteen-plot calculations deliberately assume no spillovers between plots. If that assumption fails, the simple finite-population treatment effect no longer has the interpretation claimed by the main example.

21. Sample size, power and meaningful differences

Before collecting data, researchers should identify the smallest effect worth detecting and the uncertainty they can reasonably expect. Here the measured outcome is kilograms per plot, and a three-kilogram difference might be economically important or trivial depending on production costs and baseline yield. A p-value alone cannot answer that value judgment. The question is not merely “Can I reject zero?” but “Will the experiment be informative about effects that matter?”

Power is the probability a prespecified test rejects its null under a stated alternative effect and a fully specified model or design scenario. Its computation requires plausible effect sizes, outcome variability, allocation probabilities and significance threshold. Blocking may improve power if block membership predicts baseline outcomes, but more blocks do not necessarily compensate for very few units per block. With only four blocks and two observations per arm per block, uncertainty estimates may be unstable.

Precision planning must occur before looking at final treatment comparisons. Exploring many outcomes or subgroups until one is significant increases false-discovery risk, as covered in Guide 46. Prespecify one primary outcome, the ITT estimand and the primary analysis; distinguish exploratory analyses as such. A well-designed null result can be informative if the interval excludes effects of practical importance. An underpowered study, by contrast, may leave both meaningful benefit and harm plausible.

22. Internal validity is not automatic generalizability

Randomization supports a causal comparison for the experimental units and implemented intervention, under the stated assumptions. It does not randomly sample farms, seasons, crops or countries. Our four soil blocks come from a single constructed instructional scenario, so they cannot establish how fertilizer performs in a different climate. That is the distinction between internal validity and external validity or transportability.

Changing the target population requires asking whether important effect modifiers differ: soil texture, rainfall, crop variety, dose, season, irrigation practice and adherence. A small positive effect in well-irrigated plots might fail to generalize to drought conditions. Replication across settings, mechanistic knowledge and transparent eligibility criteria support transportability, but no single p-value can guarantee it. Even a perfectly executed randomized experiment only estimates the intervention as delivered under its particular context.

Ethical and operational conditions matter as well. An intervention with no plausible benefit or unacceptable harms may not justify allocation, and informed consent may be required in human trials. Equitable eligibility and follow-up procedures affect both study integrity and relevance. Describe which units could enter, which did enter, and whether the target inference is about these sixteen plots, eligible plots on this farm or a larger population. Do not silently move among those targets.

23. Prespecification, transparent reporting and the final causal claim

A publishable experiment should retain the protocol, eligibility criteria, planned sample size, randomization scheme, allocation-concealment strategy, block definitions, outcome measurement schedule, handling of missing data and statistical analysis plan. The analysis plan should specify the estimand and the exact or approximate inference procedure. For human randomized trials, CONSORT 2025 emphasizes random-sequence generation, restrictions such as blocks, allocation concealment, blinding, adherence, analysis populations and reasons for missing outcomes.

For this educational case, the prespecified outcome is end-of-season yield, the experimental unit is a plot, the arms are new versus standard fertilizer, and allocation is two-versus-two within each of four soil-quality blocks. The primary estimator is the equally weighted mean of within-block differences. A sharp-null exact randomization test uses the 1,296 allocations permitted by this scheme. Those ingredients make the analysis reproducible and prevent replacing the randomization distribution with one that was not actually used.

Our defensible conclusion is narrow: the fictional observed blocked difference is +3 kg per plot, and the observed assignment is unusual under the sharp no-effect null, with exact two-sided p≈0.00617. Because the data are fabricated, the result proves nothing about fertilizer in a real farm. More importantly, the validity of the causal interpretation depends on true randomization, stable treatment versions, no interference, complete or appropriately handled outcomes, and faithful adherence to the analytic design.

24. A practical experimental design checklist in reasoning form

Start by defining the decision: what intervention could be implemented and what outcome would change the decision? Translate it into a causal estimand with units, target units and time horizon. Select experimental units at the scale where treatment can be assigned without contamination. List pre-treatment characteristics likely to predict the outcome. Decide whether complete randomization, blocked randomization or cluster randomization matches the intervention and dependence structure.

Next, write the random assignment algorithm and determine the possible assignments. In the present design there are six legal pairs per block and 1,296 complete allocations. Specify how the sequence will be generated, who will conceal it, who can be blinded, and how outcome values will be measured. Commit to a primary outcome and analysis, including handling of nonadherence and missingness. Then run the allocation exactly as specified and preserve an audit trail that could be inspected independently.

Finally, calculate a treatment contrast in the units of the outcome, verify it by another arithmetic route, and quantify uncertainty using the design that was really implemented. Explain both the null being tested and the limit of the estimand. Inspect heterogeneity and practical significance without exaggerating small-sample precision. Report deviations and exploratory findings transparently. This is not merely an administrative checklist: every choice changes what counterfactual comparison the analysis is entitled to make.

25. Exercise 1 — Identify the true experimental unit

Problem. Four greenhouses are independently assigned a new climate-control system or standard ventilation; two greenhouses receive each option. Researchers measure twenty seedlings in each greenhouse every week for four weeks. How many experimental units are present? How many plant-by-week measurements could be recorded? Which number governs a simple independent-assignment analysis? Explain what changes if each seedling had instead been randomized individually with a separate independently functioning chamber.

Complete solution. The experimental unit is a greenhouse because its treatment is assigned as one shared condition. There are 4 independently assigned units, not 80 seedlings. There are 4×20×4=320 plant-week measurements, but these observations share both greenhouse and seedling dependence. Even the 80 distinct seedlings are not eighty separate treatment assignments. A simple valid analysis could compare greenhouse summaries, producing only four independently randomized values. A mixed-effects analysis might retain detailed observations but must model clustering and repeated measurement, and four greenhouse clusters still give little robust inferential information.

If seedlings were independently randomized to separate chambers with no interference, the seedling might become the experimental unit; the actual details of treatment delivery decide. The important lesson is to trace treatment assignment rather than count spreadsheet rows. More measurements can improve how precisely we measure a greenhouse, but they cannot manufacture additional independently randomized greenhouses. Reporting n=320 for an independent t-test would confuse measurement resolution with assignment replication and could greatly understate uncertainty.

26. Exercise 2 — Reconstruct the blocked allocation space

Problem. A factory has three shifts. Each shift contains six independent machines, and exactly three machines in each shift will be randomized to a new maintenance schedule. How many legal assignments exist overall if every within-shift assignment is equally likely? What is the marginal probability a given machine receives treatment? Are assignments to two machines in the same shift independent?

Complete solution. First count within a shift: choose three of six, \(\binom63=6!/(3!3!)=20\) choices. Because the three shift allocations are independently drawn, the number of complete assignments is \(20^3=8000\). Each assignment has probability 1/8000 under the stated procedure. A particular machine is included in \(\binom52=10\) treatment sets among twenty legal sets, so its marginal treatment probability is 10/20=0.5.

The indicators are dependent within each shift: after one machine is treated, only two of the other five can be treated, so \(P(Z_2=1\mid Z_1=1)=2/5=0.4\), whereas \(P(Z_2=1)=0.5\). This negative dependence reflects intentional balance. A correct simulation would select exactly three machines in each shift, not flip independent fair coins for all eighteen machines. The latter would sometimes assign unequal counts and therefore simulate a different experiment, invalidating its p-values as tests of the original design.

27. Exercise 3 — Reproduce the main treatment estimate

Core equation · verify the design
\[\begin{aligned}d_A&=2,\ d_B=4,\ d_C=1,\ d_D=5\\\widehat\tau_B&=\frac{2+4+1+5}{4}=3\end{aligned}\]

Problem. From the full crop dataset, compute the treatment-minus-control difference in every block, then the equal-block mean. Independently reconstruct the pooled treatment and control means. State the result with units and one important limitation.

Complete solution. Block A: \((11+13)/2-(9+11)/2=12-10=2\) kg. Block B: \((23+25)/2-(19+21)/2=24-20=4\) kg. Block C: \((30+32)/2-(29+31)/2=31-30=1\) kg. Block D: \((44+46)/2-(39+41)/2=45-40=5\) kg. The blocked estimate is \((2+4+1+5)/4=12/4=3\) kg per plot.

Independently, the control sum is 200 kg among eight plots, mean 25 kg; treatment sum is 224 kg among eight plots, mean 28 kg. The pooled difference is 28−25=3 kg per plot. Both routes agree because the blocks have equal sizes and equal treatment proportions. The number is the observed assignment contrast under the stated randomization, not a direct measurement of the individual effect on every plot. There is no real farm evidence because these values were deliberately invented for teaching. A student should be able to reconstruct this answer without relying on a computer or on any hidden observations.

28. Exercise 4 — Unequal blocks require a declared estimand

Core equation · verify the design
\[\frac4{16}(2)+\frac{12}{16}(5)=4.25\ne\frac{2+5}{2}=3.5\]

Problem. A new study has two blocks. Block A contains four eligible plots and its treatment contrast is +2 kg; block B contains twelve eligible plots and its contrast is +5 kg. All sixteen plots are the intended finite population, with the same treatment probability in both blocks. Calculate the plot-weighted average of the two contrasts and compare it with an equally weighted block average. Why do they differ?

Complete solution. The plot weights are 4/16=0.25 and 12/16=0.75. The finite-population plot-weighted estimate is \(0.25(2)+0.75(5)=0.5+3.75=\mathbf{4.25}\) kg per eligible plot. The equally weighted average over blocks is \((2+5)/2=3.5\) kg. The numerical difference is 0.75 kg because these two estimators place different importance on the larger block. They are not interchangeable summaries of the same target.

If the scientific estimand is the average effect for all sixteen eligible plots, weighting by plot count is natural under comparable within-block assignment fractions. If instead decision makers want an average effect across two soil regions considered equally important regardless of size, equal block weights could be appropriate, but that must be stated before analysis. An estimator is not automatically more rigorous because its formula is familiar; its weights must represent the population and decision that the researcher has chosen to study.

29. Exercise 5 — Verify the exact tail probability

Core equation · verify the design
\[p_{\mathrm{one-sided}}=\frac4{1296}\approx0.00308642,\qquad p_{\mathrm{two-sided}}=\frac8{1296}\approx0.00617284\]

Problem. For the original four-block data, a program enumerates 1,296 legal assignments under the sharp null. It finds four test statistics at least +3 kg and four at most −3 kg. Find the one-sided and two-sided exact p-values. Explain what would change if the researcher had prespecified two-sided testing before observing the results.

Complete solution. The one-sided probability for a positive effect is \(4/1296=1/324\approx0.00308642\). The two-sided probability is \((4+4)/1296=8/1296=1/162\approx0.00617284\). Because the null distribution is symmetric in this assignment scheme, the two tails contribute equally. Under a prespecified two-sided analysis, report 0.00617, even though the observed difference is positive. Switching to one-sided reporting after observing its sign would alter the decision rule in a data-dependent way.

Neither number is the probability that the fertilizer is effective; both are assignment probabilities conditional on a strict sharp no-effect hypothesis. The enumeration requires the true allocation rule: exactly two treated plots per block. Permuting eight treatment labels across all sixteen plots without maintaining the block constraint would define a different sample space and generally a different test. This is why an exact test must begin with the design, not simply a software command named “permutation.”

30. Exercise 6 — Distinguish a sharp null from a weak null

Problem. Imagine four experimental units have individual treatment effects +2, −2, +1 and −1 kg. Calculate their average effect. Does the sharp no-effect null hold? Does the zero-average-effect null hold? Can you generally recover all four effects from a conventional experiment in which each unit receives only one treatment?

Complete solution. Sum the individual effects: \(2-2+1-1=0\) kg. Divide by four: the average effect is 0 kg per plot, so the weak zero-average null is true for this fictional list. However, no individual effect in the list equals zero. The sharp no-effect null, which requires all four individual effects to equal zero, is therefore false. This proves the two hypotheses are not equivalent.

A conventional two-arm trial observes only one potential outcome per unit. If unit 1 receives treatment, we see \(Y_1(1)\), but not \(Y_1(0)\), and hence cannot observe \(\tau_1\) directly. Randomization identifies average effects in an appropriate sense; it does not reveal every unit's counterfactual. The exercise also explains why permuting fixed observed yields gives a straightforward exact test for a sharp null but not necessarily an exact test of a zero average effect with heterogeneous individual effects. Choosing an estimand is separate from selecting a test statistic.

31. Exercise 7 — Estimate variability at the level of blocks

Core equation · verify the design
\[\operatorname{SE}_{\rm blocks}=\sqrt{\frac{10/3}{4}}\approx0.9129\ \mathrm{kg}\]

Problem. Use the four observed block contrasts 2, 4, 1, 5 kg. Calculate their mean, sample variance, sample standard deviation and the conventional standard error of the average if the four block contrasts are regarded as an independent sample from a broader block population. Explain why this is not by itself an exact randomization standard error.

Complete solution. The mean is 3. The four deviations from 3 are −1, +1, −2 and +2, so the squared deviations sum to 1+1+4+4=10. The sample variance is 10/(4−1)=10/3≈3.3333 kg². The sample standard deviation is √(10/3)≈1.8257 kg. With four independent blocks under the stated superpopulation interpretation, the conventional standard error of their mean is 1.8257/√4≈0.9129 kg.

This standard error treats blocks as sampled comparable units. In the finite-population randomization perspective, the sixteen potential-outcome pairs are fixed, treatment labels are random, and uncertainty is determined by the actual restricted allocation. Those are different probability models. Neither approach is automatically forbidden, but they target different repetitions and must be labeled accordingly. If blocks were selected purposively or if only four blocks cover a narrow environmental range, extrapolating to a population of all possible soil blocks needs further assumptions. Do not call 0.9129 an exact design-based standard error merely because it follows a familiar formula.

32. Exercise 8 — A transparent conservative design-based variance calculation

Core equation · verify the design
\[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat\tau_B)=4\left(\frac14\right)^2\left(\frac22+\frac22\right)=0.5,\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}\approx0.7071\]

Problem. In each original block, the two observed control yields differ by 2 kg and the two observed treatment yields also differ by 2 kg. Calculate the two sample variances within each arm and the standard Neyman-style plug-in variance of the equally weighted blocked mean, using arm sample sizes two and block weights 1/4. Explain the unobservable term that this approximation omits.

Complete solution. Two values one unit below and one unit above their mean have squared deviations 1+1=2, and their sample variance is 2/(2−1)=2 kg². Thus \(s_{Tb}^2=s_{Cb}^2=2\) kg² in all four blocks. The estimated variance for one block contrast, omitting the unobserved treatment-effect variance term, is \(2/2+2/2=2\) kg². Weighting and summing four independent block contributions gives \(\widehat V(\widehat\tau_B)=\sum_b(1/4)^2(2)=4(1/16)(2)=0.5\) kg². The associated estimated standard error is √0.5≈0.7071 kg.

The finite-population randomization variance also contains a negative term related to variation in the individual treatment effects within blocks, \(-S_{\tau,b}^2/N_b\), which is generally not identified because we do not observe both potential outcomes on the same plot. The plug-in omits that negative term and is conservative in expectation under the textbook design conditions. With only two observed units per arm and four blocks, estimated variances can be unstable; a conventional normal confidence interval need not have reliable small-sample coverage. The exact sharp-null test answers a different question and avoids this particular normal approximation.

33. Exercise 9 — Preserve ITT when treatment delivery fails

Core equation · verify the design
\[\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{ITT}}=\frac{240}{8}-\frac{216}{8}=30-27=3\]

Problem. An experiment assigns eight units to treatment and eight to control. Of the eight assigned treatment, two do not receive it. The observed outcome totals by original assignment are 240 units of output among the eight assigned T and 216 among the eight assigned C. Calculate the ITT difference. Explain why comparing only the six actual recipients with controls can be biased even though assignment was randomized.

Complete solution. The assigned-treatment mean is 240/8=30 units of output. The assigned-control mean is 216/8=27. Therefore the ITT contrast is 30−27=3 output units per assigned unit. Both of the nonadherent treatment-assigned units remain in the first denominator because ITT compares the groups created by the random procedure. The totals in the question are by original assignment and already include their measured outcomes.

If we discard the two units that did not adhere, membership of the analyzed treatment group is now selected partly by post-assignment behavior. Nonadherence may be associated with underlying outcomes, availability or other factors. The treated-recipient group need no longer be comparable to all originally assigned controls. ITT estimates assignment or offer effects, not necessarily the full biological effect of exposure. Recovering a complier-average effect requires additional assumptions and methods; excluding nonrecipients without justification is not an innocent correction to the arithmetic.

34. Exercise 10 — Missing data cannot be repaired by a convenient zero

Problem. A block has two control yields 20 and 22 kg, and two assigned-treated plots, one observed at 26 kg and the other lost at harvest. A colleague proposes assigning zero to the missing yield and announcing a harmful fertilizer effect. Calculate that zero-imputed contrast and describe why it is not a data-based estimate without an explicit missingness model. Give one sensitivity alternative.

Complete solution. The control mean is (20+22)/2=21 kg. Under the colleague's imposed zero, the treatment mean would be (26+0)/2=13 kg, giving a contrast 13−21=−8 kg. But the missing plot did not necessarily yield zero; the number was supplied by an arbitrary rule. The result tells us what follows if the missing outcome truly equals zero, not what the data establish. Treating an unavailable measurement as a genuine zero outcome is a substantive assumption with potentially large consequences.

A simple sensitivity range could compare plausible missing yields of 18, 24 and 30 kg. The corresponding treated means are (26+18)/2=22, (26+24)/2=25 and (26+30)/2=28 kg; treatment-minus-control contrasts become +1, +4 and +7 kg. This range shows how the conclusion depends on the missing value rather than concealing that dependence. The choice of plausible values requires domain knowledge and documentation. Preventing loss and collecting reasons for missingness are better than relying entirely on post hoc imputation.

35. Exercise 11 — Spot interference and the wrong experimental unit

Problem. A university assigns four dormitories, rather than students, to a building-wide air-filtration system. Two dormitories receive the new system and two retain standard ventilation. Each building houses 100 students. A report claims “n=400 independent randomized students” and computes a student-level standard error assuming independent treatment assignments. Identify two errors and propose a design-compatible analysis.

Complete solution. First, treatment is assigned at the dormitory level, so there are four independently randomized units, not 400. Second, students sharing a dormitory may have correlated outcomes because of common air quality, contacts, infrastructure and treatment exposure. Treating student records as 400 independent treatment assignments artificially inflates information about the assignment effect. The relevant design does not justify a simple individual-independent standard error.

A transparent initial analysis could compute one aggregate outcome per dormitory and compare the two assigned-treated dormitories with the two controls, stating prominently that the sample contains only four assignment units and therefore affords weak precision. A hierarchical or cluster-aware method might use individual data, but robust asymptotic inference with four clusters is highly uncertain. If students move between dormitories, spillovers may also violate partial-interference assumptions. More students observed in the same four buildings improve measurement, not the number of randomized buildings. A new experiment with more buildings would address that limitation more directly.

36. Exercise 12 — Design a complete new blocked experiment

Core equation · verify the design
\[\left(\binom84\right)^3=70^3=343000\]

Problem. A food manufacturer wants to test whether a new cooling schedule reduces product defects. It operates three production lines, each with eight independently schedulable batches over a month. Historical defect rates differ by line. Design a randomized trial identifying the experimental unit, block, arms, allocation, estimand, primary outcome, treatment implementation, ITT strategy, missing-data plan and inferential approach. State one failure mode that could require redesign.

Complete solution. Define a batch as the experimental unit only if cooling schedules can truly be assigned independently without carryover to neighboring batches. Use production line as a pre-treatment block because its historical defect rate predicts the outcome. Within each of the three lines, label eight eligible batches and randomly select four for the new schedule; the remaining four receive the standard schedule. There are \(\binom84=70\) assignments per line and \(70^3=343000\) complete assignments if the three selections are independent. Each batch has treatment probability 1/2.

Prespecify the primary outcome as the number of defective products per 100 inspected items within each batch, with the same inspection protocol for both arms. The finite-batch ITT estimand is the average difference in defects per 100 items under assignment to new versus standard cooling. Compare mean outcomes within each line and average the line contrasts, because all lines contribute equal numbers of batches. Record failures to deliver the new schedule but retain the batch in its originally assigned group for ITT. Prevent missing inspections; report missing counts and perform prespecified sensitivity analyses if outcomes are unavailable. A design-compatible permutation test must maintain four treated batches per line, and an uncertainty interval needs to respect the assignment structure.

Verification and limitation. Check 3×8=24 independently assigned batches, twelve per arm, and 70³=343000 legal allocations. A practical threat is equipment carryover: if cooling one batch changes the next batch's temperature, independently assigning consecutive batches violates the no-interference design assumption. The solution is not to ignore the dependence but to introduce washout periods or randomize longer production runs as clusters, revising both unit and analysis. This exercise completes the learning chain: formulate the causal question, design the allocation, connect estimator to assignment, calculate what can be checked, and explain when the design fails.

37. References, reproducibility and final interpretation

This guide uses a deliberately complete synthetic dataset and self-contained calculations. Readers can reproduce the exact randomization distribution by listing the six treatment pairs in every four-plot block, evaluating the blocked contrast for each, taking the Cartesian product of the four six-element sets, and counting assignments with absolute contrast at least 3 kg. An independent enumeration should produce 1,296 assignments and 8 two-sided extremes. No hidden simulation seed is needed: this is exhaustive enumeration, not a random sample of permutations.

For broader reporting practice, see the CONSORT 2025 Statement (Hopewell and colleagues, JAMA, 2025;333:1998–2005; doi:10.1001/jama.2025.4347) and its explanation-and-elaboration paper. CONSORT concerns reporting randomized trials, especially in health research; applying analogous transparency principles to agriculture is a recommendation rather than a claim that the medical checklist legally governs farms. For further theory, consult Neyman's finite-population randomization approach, Fisher's sharp-null randomization tests, and the potential-outcomes framework. These ideas are distinguished by what randomness and null hypothesis they assume.

The final understanding check is conceptual and quantitative: can you say which units were assigned, list the permitted assignments, explain why the blocked mean is 3 kg, derive the exact p-value, distinguish sharp and weak nulls, handle nonadherence without discarding randomized units, and explain why spillovers or missing outcomes could break the analysis? If any link is missing, revisit the preceding derivation before transferring the approach to a new experiment. A valid randomized experiment is a coherent causal design supported by analysis—not merely a table of different group means.
