MODULE 01 / 34

1. An integrated decision, not a competition for a single number

A survival model is useful only when its predictions can support a well-defined decision. A manager deciding which component modules to inspect before the next planned shutdown does not ask merely, “Which fitted regression has the smallest p-value?” She asks which modules are likely to fail before the next operating interval, how reliably the ranking distinguishes failures from survivors, whether the predicted probabilities correspond to what actually happens, and how uncertain the answer is. Guide 80 integrates the Kaplan–Meier estimator, Cox regression, Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) regression, censoring-aware prediction scores, resampling, and the limits introduced by competing events. This is a capstone that deliberately connects earlier statistical ideas rather than replacing them with a new algorithm.

We will make a decision at a defined horizon of eight units of operating time, where one unit equals 100 hours. The question is whether a module needs additional monitoring before 800 operating hours from enrollment. Failure means first functional failure according to a consistent engineering definition. Preventive replacement, if it makes future failure unobservable, needs special treatment: sometimes it is censoring, sometimes it is a competing event, depending on the estimand. We will keep those cases distinct.

Our twelve-record dataset is entirely synthetic, constructed for instruction, with intentionally small sample size and transparent predictions. Its numerical results demonstrate calculations; they are neither measured reliability results nor evidence of causal effects. A real industrial program would require many independent modules across production batches and prospective validation. The dataset was deliberately designed to exhibit both events and right censoring before and after the horizon, because that is where ordinary binary-classification metrics can mislead.

Learning objectives. At the end you should be able to define the clock and estimand, describe a defensible split, calculate Harrell's concordance index from comparable event pairs, evaluate an Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighted (IPCW) Brier Score, investigate calibration, justify uncertainty assessment, and explain with a numerical example why censoring competing events in a Kaplan–Meier calculation does not estimate cause-specific cumulative incidence. Each result will be interpreted before a decision is made. The task is more than reporting metrics: you must know what quantity each number estimates and which assumptions sustain it.

Orientation: Excellent discrimination does not guarantee accurate probabilities. Good average calibration does not guarantee safety for a particular subgroup. A narrow confidence interval does not rescue a biased measurement design.

MODULE 02 / 34

2. Prerequisites and the conceptual map

This guide assumes knowledge of sampling and estimation from Guide 35; model comparison and validation from Guide 40; uncertainty and confidence intervals from Guide 50; Kaplan–Meier and the log-rank framework from Guide 75; Cox diagnostics from Guide 77; and AFT prediction from Guide 79. We reconstruct the necessary steps here. Students with introductory statistics and Calculus I should remember the distinction between an event probability, a density and a conditional instantaneous rate. A hazard is a rate per unit time conditional on surviving to that time; it is not itself a probability.

There are three complementary prediction questions. Discrimination concerns order: do failures tend to receive higher predicted risk than comparable longer-surviving observations? Calibration concerns magnitude: does a reported 30% failure probability correspond approximately to a 30% event frequency among similarly predicted cases, under proper treatment of censoring? Overall prediction error combines direction and magnitude at a horizon; the Brier Score is a squared-error measure of this kind. None of these captures every operational objective. Decision usefulness also depends on the cost of missed failures, preventive interventions and the reliability of the data-generating process.

The analysis sequence is deliberately fixed. First specify the event and time zero. Second audit follow-up, censoring and independence. Third freeze an evaluation sample that models did not use for tuning. Fourth obtain survival predictions on the same horizon and units. Fifth examine discrimination, calibration and Brier error, checking the censoring weights. Sixth quantify uncertainty using entity-level resampling when justified. Seventh assess whether assumptions and follow-up support the proposed action. Eighth communicate both the decision and reasons not to overstate it.

Vocabulary bridge: “Risk at eight units” means \(P(T\le8\mid x)=1-S(8\mid x)\), not the Cox hazard ratio. An AFT Time Ratio concerns multiplicative duration, not the absolute risk by eight units. A KM curve summarizes a group, not individualized risks without additional structure.

3. Specify the estimand, observation process and time zero

Let \(T>0\) denote time from first monitored operation to first qualifying failure, and \(C>0\) time to the end of observable follow-up. What we record is the smaller of the two: the observed duration \(Y=\min(T,C)\). The event indicator is \(\delta=I(T\le C)\), meaning 1 for a confirmed failure and 0 for right censoring. If a unit is withdrawn at hour 600 while still functioning, we know only \(T>600\). Entering “no failure by hour 800” for that unit would invent an additional 200 hours of observation.

The estimand for our engineering question is the probability of failure from a common origin through 800 hours, for a module with declared characteristics and under the specified operating regime. We should state whether failure risk is predicted under continued operation without replacement, under ordinary operational policy, or under a competing-replacement process; these are different questions. Our initial example uses the hypothetical event-free lifetime to first failure with administratively independent censoring, and later introduces a distinct competing-risk example.

Time zero must be before predictors are measured or at a legitimate landmark when new information becomes available. Starting a “baseline” prediction at 500 hours while demanding that every enrolled unit survive to that landmark quietly selects survivors. If a service treatment is classified as “ever received” using information acquired after enrollment, pre-treatment survival can be incorrectly credited to treatment. That is immortal time bias, not a modeling improvement.

Administrative censoring is reasonable when the test simply closes on a preplanned date, independently of the future unobserved failure time conditional on measured variables. If inspectors preferentially stop observing units with suspicious vibrations, censoring could be informative. Later IPCW calculations rely on a model for the probability of remaining observable and require positivity: observations like those evaluated must have a nonzero chance of remaining observed. The definition of independence is conditional and substantive; it cannot be proved by a p-value.

KEY EQUATION
\[Y_i=\min(T_i,C_i),\qquad \delta_i=I(T_i\le C_i),\qquad R_i(8)=1-S_i(8).\]

MODULE 05 / 34

5. What constitutes valid training and validation?

The same record must not both determine model coefficients and serve as an independent test of those coefficients. A transparent workflow splits data by module identity before fitting transformations, imputers, feature selectors or survival models. If a factory logs multiple sensor windows per module, all windows of one module must remain in the same split. If several components share a manufacturing batch, a cluster- or batch-held-out test is often more honest than a random row split because shared conditions make outcomes correlated.

A chronological split can answer a different operational question: train on historical cohorts, test on modules enrolled later. It guards against some forms of look-ahead but also exposes changing manufacturing and maintenance practices. No splitting scheme is automatically correct. Choose one that matches deployment. Within the training portion, tune preprocessing, regularization and model families using properly nested cross-validation or a separate validation set. Reserve the final evaluation sample for one predeclared assessment; repeated tuning against the test set makes its apparent performance optimistic.

An additional leakage trap occurs when a predictor is measured after the requested prediction time. The indicator “eventually required urgent repair” contains information from the future if the decision is supposed to occur at first installation. At an eight-hour landmark, it could become usable only for modules demonstrably still under observation and if the prediction origin and outcome clock are redefined consistently. That produces a landmark analysis, not a baseline model with a clever feature.

Modeling and validation should share explicit units, horizon and outcome definition. A survival probability at 800 calendar hours is not interchangeable with one at 800 operating hours. A Cox model that predicts first failure and a KM curve that counts replacement as failure have different endpoints. The evaluation notebook or guide should maintain a data dictionary and verify that every prediction is measured on the same target. Here all three families will be conceptual alternatives for the same hypothetical first-failure endpoint.

6. Establish the Kaplan–Meier benchmark

Kaplan–Meier (KM) offers a nonparametric estimate of group survival under the usual independent-censoring conditions. At each distinct failure time \(t_j\), let \(n_j\) be the number still under observation just before \(t_j\) and \(d_j\) the number who fail then. Conditional survival through that step is \(1-d_j/n_j\). Multiply the steps to obtain the estimated survival probability. Censored cases are not counted as failures; they leave subsequent risk sets after their censoring time.

KEY EQUATION
\[\widehat S_{KM}(t)=\prod_{t_j\le t}\left(1-\frac{d_j}{n_j}\right).\]

For our sorted twelve modules, the first two failures occur with risk sets of 12 and 11. Thus survival just after time 2 equals \(11/12=0.91667\); just after time 3 it becomes \((11/12)(10/11)=10/12=0.83333\). Module C is censored at time 4, so the risk set at time 5 is 9 rather than 10. The survival at time 5 is therefore \((10/12)(8/9)=20/27\approx0.74074\). At time 6, the next factor is \(7/8\), giving \(35/54\approx0.64815\). Module F is censored at 7, and at time 8 the risk set is six; one failure gives \(5/6\). Therefore KM survival at our horizon is about 0.54012 and group failure probability is about 0.45988.

KEY EQUATION
\[\widehat S_{KM}(8)=\frac{11}{12}\frac{10}{11}\frac89\frac78\frac56=\frac{175}{324}\approx0.54012.\]

The raw proportion with observed failure by time 8 is \(5/12\approx0.41667\). It differs from \(1-\widehat S_{KM}(8)\) because two participants were censored before 8; under the KM assumptions, each remains relevant through the interval it was observed, not forever. The KM curve is the overall benchmark, not an individualized probability for A or L. We will compare its group trend and support of follow-up with conditional Cox and AFT predictions.

MODULE 07 / 34

7. Cox and AFT: comparable predictions, different parameter meanings

A Cox Proportional Hazards (PH) model expresses instantaneous hazard as a baseline function times a relative-risk multiplier. For covariates \(x\), \(h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)\exp(x^{\mathsf T}\beta)\), and its survival prediction is \(S(t\mid x)=S_0(t)^{\exp(x^{\mathsf T}\beta)}\). A coefficient produces a Hazard Ratio only when compared under the model's assumptions. A constant HR does not imply a constant difference in absolute failure probability; that depends on baseline risk and the evaluation time.

An AFT model instead expresses log time as a linear predictor plus a stochastic error: \(\log T=\alpha+x^{\mathsf T}\gamma+\sigma\varepsilon\). In the common time-scale interpretation, \(\exp(\gamma_k)\) is the Time Ratio associated with a unit covariate change holding other model terms fixed. A Time Ratio of 0.8 indicates a 20% shorter modeled duration quantile, not a 20% higher failure probability. Lognormal and Weibull AFT models yield complete conditional survival curves; the two distributional choices impose different tail behavior.

KEY EQUATION
\[h(t\mid x)=h_0(t)e^{x^{\mathsf T}\beta},\qquad \log T=\alpha+x^{\mathsf T}\gamma+\sigma\varepsilon.\]

The only fair predictive comparison evaluates the same outcome, horizon, held-out modules, and censoring protocol. Cox, AFT and KM offer different flexibility and assumptions; there is no universal numerical winner. Cox may be attractive when relative hazards are interpretable and PH is adequate, while AFT is attractive when duration multipliers and parametric quantiles are useful. The KM curve is a useful reality check but has no individualized covariates unless stratified into groups, which itself may create small unstable risk sets.

Before comparing predictions, examine Cox scaled Schoenfeld residuals versus time, possible nonlinearity using functional-form checks, influential observations and the PH assumption. For AFT inspect residual structure, plausible family, outliers and extrapolated tail behavior. A violation can invalidate the coefficient's interpretation even if a single predictive metric looks favorable. Model evaluation therefore includes diagnostics, not only a leaderboard.

MODULE 08 / 34

8. Why ordinary classification accuracy is wrong under early censoring

A tempting shortcut labels each row “failure by eight” if it failed by time 8, and “no failure” otherwise, then calculates ordinary accuracy or squared error. It would incorrectly place modules C and F in the known-survivor group even though they leave observation at 4 and 7. For C, \(T>4\), but \(T>8\) is unknown. The same logic holds for F. Treating these two labels as known inserts information the dataset does not contain.

One might instead discard C and F. That is not automatically unbiased: the remaining sample overrepresents individuals whose follow-up stayed observable, and the extent of this selection may depend on their covariates and failure risk. Censoring-aware methods reconstruct the evaluation target under assumptions about observation probabilities. They are not miraculous corrections for any missing-data mechanism; informative censoring unaccounted for by the weighting model can bias them.

At horizon \(\tau=8\), define the true survival indicator \(Z_i(8)=I(T_i>8)\). We know \(Z=0\) for A, B, D, E and G because these failed by eight. We know \(Z=1\) for H, I, J, K and L because they remained under observation beyond eight. We do not know \(Z\) for C and F. The corresponding model prediction is \(\widehat S_i(8)\), a continuous probability, not a classification label. The squared error \((Z_i-\widehat S_i)^2\) can therefore be computed for ten cases, with suitable censoring weights to recover population-average meaning.

There is also a distinction between fixed-horizon predictions and a full survival curve. A candidate that is good at eight units may be poor at two or twenty. Horizon selection should follow the operational question before examining outcomes, with a sufficient number of events and censored risk sets at that time. A time-dependent performance curve can supplement the declared primary horizon but does not license choosing the best-looking time after analysis.

MODULE 09 / 34

9. Discrimination as an ordering question

The C-index summarizes whether the model tends to assign greater failure risk to the module known to fail earlier within evaluable comparisons. In Harrell's basic formulation, a pair is comparable if the shorter observed time corresponds to an event. If the shorter observation is censored, we cannot infer which latent failure occurred first, so that pair is excluded. Ties in event time or prediction score require declared conventions; our dataset has unique times and distinct scores, making the core arithmetic transparent.

Because we use \(S(8)\) as a risk ranking, lower survival prediction means higher predicted failure risk. In a comparable pair where A fails at time 2 and H survives under observation to time 9, a concordant prediction has \(S_A(8)<S_H(8)\): \(0.24<0.58\), so the pair is concordant. If a later-failing row has a smaller predicted survival, that pair is discordant. A model assigning identical risk scores would receive half credit for tied predictions under the common convention.

KEY EQUATION
\[\widehat C=\frac{N_{\mathrm{concordant}}+\tfrac12N_{\mathrm{tied\ score}}}{N_{\mathrm{comparable}}}.\]

Interpret the metric as a ranking score over the comparable pairs in this evaluation design, not as the proportion of failures correctly classified. A C-index of 0.8 does not mean 80% of people fail or that predicted probabilities are correct 80% of the time. It is affected by how follow-up, censoring, risk heterogeneity and case selection shape comparable pairs. Harrell's version may be biased under heavy censoring; censoring-adjusted alternatives, including Uno's C, are often worth considering with appropriate uncertainty methods.

We will now enumerate pairs rather than merely trust a library implementation. This is possible only because the synthetic dataset is small. The same logic can be implemented with a double loop on larger validation samples, though production code must handle ties, weights, compute cost and left-truncated risk sets where relevant.

11. Discrimination is not calibration

Suppose one model predicts eight-unit survival probabilities \(0.24,0.32,\ldots\) as in the table. A second model transforms every risk score monotonically, moving them all toward extreme 0 or 1 predictions while preserving their ordering. Its C-index can remain exactly the same: all comparable pair inequalities are unchanged. Yet its predictions may become substantially worse probabilities. This is why a discrimination statistic cannot by itself justify a decision threshold or an expected failure count.

Calibration-in-the-large compares aggregate predicted probability with event frequency or censoring-adjusted event probability. Calibration slope and a calibration curve examine how observed outcomes vary across the risk spectrum, not just the average. In survival settings the relevant outcome at \(\tau\) is not always observed; a calibration plot needs a method, such as group KM estimates with sufficient follow-up or censoring-adjusted techniques, that respects censoring. Ordinary binary grouping of censored cases as non-events biases the comparison.

A model can be well calibrated on average but fail dangerously for a subgroup. For example, perhaps predictions are accurate for standard-load modules but underestimate failure among high-load modules. Aggregated figures can conceal that discrepancy. Conversely, a small validation cohort can produce a jagged calibration curve merely because each bin contains few events. Choosing ten equal-count bins for twelve records would create near-singleton points and false visual precision. The right response is to present informative uncertainty and admit limited resolution.

At our horizon, the mean predicted failure probability from the twelve rows is \(1-\bar S(8)\). Because \(\sum_iS_i(8)=5.74\), mean predicted survival is \(5.74/12\approx0.4783\), and mean predicted failure risk is about \(0.5217\). The overall KM failure estimate is about 0.4599. Their difference is about 0.0618. These are useful descriptive comparisons, not a validated formal recalibration test on twelve synthetic observations.

MODULE 12 / 34

12. An honest small-sample calibration display

A useful calibration display should identify the number of modules per risk group, how their observed survival is estimated, and which uncertainty remains. For demonstration we can divide our scores into a lower predicted-failure group and a higher group. But each group contains few events, and simple KM estimates may be especially unstable around censoring. Rather than produce a smooth curve through a handful of points, display the individual predictions and the overall benchmark first. A second plot can show predicted survival on one axis against observed follow-up, with event/censor markers, making censoring visible.

The mean predicted survival of 0.4783 lies below the unadjusted overall Kaplan–Meier benchmark of 0.5401 at eight. This suggests possible pessimism on average—more predicted failure than the observed group estimate—under the synthetic example, but the two quantities are not guaranteed to coincide in small samples. The sample is not a random draw from a known deployed prediction population, and no confidence interval or formal calibration model is defensibly established by twelve invented points.

Calibration curves are more meaningful with adequate numbers of independent subjects and events, stable estimates of group survival at the horizon, and a prespecified smoothing or grouping strategy. A proper curve may plot estimated observed event incidence on the vertical axis against mean predicted incidence on the horizontal axis. The 45-degree line represents perfect agreement only for comparable definitions and horizons. Confidence bands should reflect variability in both the survival estimate and the prediction procedure where appropriate.

In deployment, check calibration again after retraining, after model transport to a new factory, and when the prevalence of failure or observation policy changes. A model can preserve rank ordering despite baseline-risk shifts while producing wrong probabilities. In that case recalibration may be appropriate, but the recalibration procedure must itself be evaluated on fresh data. Do not select a recalibration function based on an evaluation set and then report accuracy on that same set as independent evidence.

13. From the binary Brier Score to a censored survival Brier Score

For a fully observed binary survival outcome at horizon \(\tau\), the Brier Score is the average squared difference between whether the subject survived and the predicted survival probability. If survival is known, an event by time eight has target \(Z=0\), and someone known to survive beyond eight has target \(Z=1\). A module predicted to survive with probability 0.80 but failing at hour 300 incurs squared error \((0-0.80)^2=0.64\); a module predicted 0.80 and known to survive beyond the horizon incurs \((1-0.80)^2=0.04\).

KEY EQUATION
\[BS(\tau)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl[I(T_i>\tau)-\widehat S_i(\tau)\bigr]^2.\]

However, this expression assumes the true survival status at \(\tau\) is observable for every row. In our data, C and F are censored at 4 and 7, so the target cannot be assigned for them. A naïve Brier Score that declares these rows event-free introduces a false value of 1. A complete-case Brier Score that merely drops them changes the population being averaged. To target the original population's expected squared error, we must correct for the probability that each required observation remains visible.

The idea of Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW) is to upweight observable contributions according to the inverse of their chance of being observed. A failure at time 5 must remain observable through time 5; a survivor at horizon 8 must remain observable through time 8. Under appropriate conditional independence assumptions, weighting restores the target expectation even though not all outcomes are fully observed. Large inverse weights can be unstable, and the method does not repair unmodeled informative censoring.

This is a direct bridge from missing-data weighting to survival analysis. We will compute every required censoring survival factor, substitute them into the score, and interpret the resulting average. No function call is needed to understand the basic arithmetic.

MODULE 14 / 34

14. Estimate the censoring distribution, not the failure distribution

Let \(G(t)=P(C>t)\) be the probability that follow-up has not been censored by time \(t\). A standard way to estimate it is the reverse Kaplan–Meier estimator: treat original censoring times as the events of interest and original failure times as censoring. This is not the same curve as \(S_{KM}(t)\) for failure. The distinction is crucial: inverse weights correct the observation process, not the underlying failure hazard.

In our ordered table, the first original censoring occurs at time 4. At that instant, twelve subjects began, and two (A and B) have already failed, so ten remain under observation. A single censoring event makes the reverse-KM factor \((10-1)/10=9/10=0.9\). Original failures at 5 and 6 reduce subsequent risk sets but not the censoring-survival product. At time 7 there are seven still at risk, and another original censoring event gives factor \((7-1)/7=6/7\). Therefore \(\widehat G(7)=0.9(6/7)=27/35\approx0.77143\). There are no original censorings between time 7 and our horizon 8.

KEY EQUATION
\[\widehat G(4)=\frac9{10}=0.9,\qquad \widehat G(8)=\frac9{10}\frac67=\frac{27}{35}\approx0.77143.\]

For an event occurring before the first censoring, such as at 2 or 3, the observation weight is 1. For failures at 5 and 6, divide by 0.9. For a failure at exactly 8, use the censoring probability just before the event, \(\widehat G(8-)\), which equals \(27/35\) in this no-tie example. For a module followed beyond 8, the relevant denominator is \(\widehat G(8)\). The notation matters in datasets with coincident failure and censoring times, which require a prespecified tie convention.

In applications censoring may depend on measured covariates; a pooled reverse-KM is then not sufficient even if censoring is conditionally independent. One may need \(G(t\mid x)\) estimated with a censoring model. Report the method and inspect whether weights explode because \(G\) approaches zero. Evaluating the Brier Score at a horizon where almost nobody remains under observation is not credible, no matter how elegant the formula looks.

MODULE 15 / 34

15. Derive and calculate the IPCW Brier Score at eight units

The censoring-adjusted Brier Score has two observable types of contributions. A subject who fails by the horizon has target survival zero and contributes the squared predicted survival \(\widehat S_i(8)^2\), divided by the probability of being observable up to its failure time. A subject known to be event-free beyond the horizon has target survival one and contributes \((1-\widehat S_i(8))^2\), divided by the probability of being observable through the horizon. A subject censored before or at the horizon without an event contributes zero to this particular estimating expression, not because the subject is known to survive, but because the outcome is unavailable and weights adjust the observed contributions.

KEY EQUATION
\[\widehat{BS}_{IPCW}(\tau)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{I(Y_i\le\tau,\delta_i=1)\widehat S_i(\tau)^2}{\widehat G(Y_i-)}+\frac{I(Y_i>\tau)(1-\widehat S_i(\tau))^2}{\widehat G(\tau)}\right].\]

Substitute the actual twelve records. A fails at 2 and contributes \(0.24^2/1=0.057600\). B fails at 3 and contributes \(0.32^2/1=0.102400\). D fails at 5, after the first censoring, and contributes \(0.30^2/0.9=0.100000\). E fails at 6, contributing \(0.47^2/0.9\approx0.245444\). G fails at 8, contributing \(0.40^2/(27/35)\approx0.207407\). Modules C and F contribute zero because they are censored before eight and their survival outcomes at eight are unknown.

For modules H, I, J, K and L with follow-up beyond eight, multiply each squared failure-risk prediction by \(35/27\). Their contributions are respectively \(0.42^2(35/27)\approx0.228667\), \(0.46^2(35/27)\approx0.274296\), \(0.32^2(35/27)\approx0.132741\), \(0.26^2(35/27)\approx0.087630\), and \(0.35^2(35/27)\approx0.158796\). Note that these five values come from \(1-S_i(8)\), not from \(S_i(8)\).

KEY EQUATION
\[\widehat{BS}_{IPCW}(8)=\frac{0.057600+0.102400+0.100000+0.245444+0.207407+0.228667+0.274296+0.132741+0.087630+0.158796}{12}\approx\boxed{0.132915}.\]

This score is a mean squared probability error adjusted for censoring under the assumptions of the weighting procedure. Smaller is generally better when calculated on the same target population and horizon. The value is not a probability of failure, a hazard ratio or a causal effect. Do not compare it directly with a Brier Score computed at a different horizon or in a different case mix and infer superiority without qualification.

The unweighted but incorrectly relabeled Brier Score obtained by treating every censored-before-eight module as a survivor would be about 0.13745 for these chosen predictions. The difference from 0.132915 is not enormous here, but that does not validate the shortcut. In other datasets it can be substantial and directionally unpredictable. Correct methodology is not determined by whether two answers happen to be close in one constructed example.

The score can be plotted across a clinically or operationally meaningful time grid to form a time-dependent Brier curve. An Integrated Brier Score (IBS) averages Brier performance over a specified time interval, using an integration rule and scaling. State the interval because late times may have inadequate censoring support. A candidate can have a lower score around eight units and a higher score around twenty; averaging hides which operational decisions are affected. More complicated software often estimates censoring weights from training data or a separate model; the evaluation design and assumptions should be documented explicitly.

Sanity check: Predictions of survival at any horizon must be between 0 and 1 and must not increase with the horizon for a valid survival curve. A model can produce attractive isolated values at eight that would be internally inconsistent if its predicted survival curve rises from time seven to time eight.

17. Compare against an explicit reference predictor

A metric is difficult to interpret without an alternative. One simple benchmark assigns every subject the same survival probability at eight, estimated from appropriately separated training data. On an evaluation cohort it is tempting to use the cohort's KM estimate itself to define the constant benchmark, but that is an in-sample comparison and may be optimistic. For pedagogy, consider the overall KM survival of about 0.5401 as a descriptive baseline, while acknowledging that it uses outcomes from the illustrative cohort.

A fair test computes the candidate-model and reference-model Brier Scores on exactly the same evaluation subjects, under the same censoring weights and at the same horizon. The difference \(\Delta BS=BS_{candidate}-BS_{reference}\) is negative when the candidate has lower average squared error. Whether the improvement is operationally important depends on its size, uncertainty, resource cost and decision consequences. It is inappropriate to declare “better” simply because a tenth-decimal place differs between two scores.

Discrimination also needs context. A constant-risk baseline has tied scores for every comparable pair and obtains C-index 0.5 under the usual half-credit rule. Our synthetic scores yield 0.8936, but the example was designed to make ranking instructive; this is not evidence that a Cox or AFT model would achieve that performance on new modules. Improvements over a constant benchmark may come from genuine predictive information, leakage, or unrealistic splits. The evaluation protocol distinguishes those possibilities.

When comparing model families, train KM baseline, Cox and AFT only on the training data and evaluate their frozen predictions in the test data. Use the same target definition and common time horizon. Provide the number of observed events, the censoring distribution, group sizes, and a plot of the survival predictions. Model selection via training-set log-likelihood or AIC answers a different question from test-set predictive loss; an excellent fit inside the sample need not translate into good out-of-sample prediction.

MODULE 18 / 34

18. Uncertainty is about independent units, not pair counts

A point estimate of C-index or Brier Score is incomplete as an evaluation report. Repeated samples of independent modules would generally produce different values. The uncertainty interval should reflect which units are independent, how models were trained and how censoring was estimated. In our example, forty-seven comparable pairs come from only twelve modules; pretending these are forty-seven independent Bernoulli trials would drastically misunderstand the dependence structure.

For a reasonably large collection of independent modules, a subject-level bootstrap draws module IDs with replacement, carrying all of a module's follow-up and predicted values together. For each resample, re-estimate the censoring distribution and recompute the metric. If the goal is uncertainty for the entire training-and-deployment pipeline, repeat the training procedure within each resample or use a nested design, rather than bootstrapping only a fixed test score and claiming to cover model-training uncertainty. Resampling only evaluation records targets a narrower, conditional question.

When multiple modules share a factory line or batch, resample at the cluster level if those clusters are the independent sampling units; when deployment is forward in time, a temporal validation approach may be more relevant than exchangeable bootstrap sampling. Some bootstrap resamples may contain no comparable events or have a zero censoring-survival estimate by the horizon; their scores can be undefined. Predefine the handling procedure and report how often it occurs. This is particularly important with very small samples.

A percentile interval from many replicates would select, for example, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the replicate metrics. Those bounds are not automatically valid merely because one ran 2,000 resamples. Dependence, few effective clusters, heavy censoring, misspecification and adaptive model selection may undermine coverage. We do not publish a fictitious confidence interval for the twelve synthetic cases. Instead, we explain precisely what a larger study should estimate and how it should evaluate interval credibility.

MODULE 19 / 34

19. Horizons, follow-up support and extrapolation

A survival prediction is always indexed by time. Asking whether a module fails by 400 hours and asking whether it fails by 2,000 hours are different targets with different numbers of events and observable outcomes. Our fixed horizon of eight hundred-hour units lies inside the observed follow-up range of two to thirteen units, with several subjects still followed beyond the horizon. That does not make the dataset sufficient for high-confidence industrial decisions; it only makes the arithmetic of an eight-unit score possible.

Later horizons encounter fewer at-risk subjects and possibly much lower censoring survival \(G(t)\). IPCW terms then become unstable because they contain \(1/G(t)\). A parametric AFT model can continue to output predictions beyond the longest observation, but such numbers depend heavily on Weibull or Lognormal tail assumptions, not direct evidence. A Cox estimator may be unable to identify a useful baseline survival beyond its observed event support. The inability to calculate a robust tail prediction is itself important information, not a software defect.

Specify in advance a primary horizon aligned with the intervention schedule, then consider secondary horizons as sensitivity analyses. A plot of the number at risk below the KM curve should remind readers when the observed evidence thins. For a calibration plot at the primary horizon, show how many records are censored before that time, because these records require specific estimation treatment. If a subgroup has no observable subjects near the horizon, do not force a subgroup calibration claim.

A good decision report states both the range of reliable observation and the range of model-based extrapolation. It distinguishes “the fitted Weibull model predicts” from “we observed.” This distinction matters when maintenance costs, patient safety or economic forecasts are affected. Mechanistic considerations and external validation may help judge extrapolation plausibility, but cannot replace actual long-term outcome information.

MODULE 20 / 34

20. Competing risks: define which event you mean

So far, our endpoint was first functional failure, with independent loss of observation treated as censoring. In many studies, another event prevents the event of interest from being observed in its original form. A device may be permanently replaced for another mechanical reason, and a patient may die from an unrelated cause before the disease-specific event. These are competing risks, not simply administrative missing follow-up. The quantity “failure from cause A by time \(t\)” is now different from survival free of any event.

Let \(T\) denote time to the first of several mutually exclusive event types, and \(J\) the type that occurs first. The cause-specific cumulative incidence function is \(F_k(t)=P(T\le t,J=k)\). It asks for the probability that type \(k\) has occurred by the horizon in the world where other causes can happen. Overall event-free survival is \(S_{all}(t)=P(T>t)\), and under standard competing-risk definitions, the cumulative incidences sum to \(1-S_{all}(t)\).

KEY EQUATION
\[F_k(t)=P(T\le t,J=k),\qquad \sum_kF_k(t)=1-S_{all}(t).\]

A cause-specific hazard \(\lambda_k(t)\) is the instantaneous rate of cause \(k\) among individuals still free of every event. Its cumulative incidence depends both on that hazard and on survival from all causes: \(F_k(t)=\int_0^tS_{all}(u-)\,d\Lambda_k(u)\). This is why a high cause-specific hazard does not automatically imply the same absolute cumulative incidence when competing events are common. The risk set can disappear through other causes.

A Kaplan–Meier calculation that treats other event types as censored can describe a hypothetical net event quantity under extra interpretations, but \(1-KM\) from that calculation does not equal the observed-world cumulative incidence of the chosen cause. We will show the discrepancy with a complete four-subject example, avoiding an appeal to theory alone.

MODULE 21 / 34

21. Four-subject competing-risk counterexample

Consider four hypothetical identical-start units, all followed completely until their first terminal event or until the study ends at time 5. There is no ordinary censoring before the horizon. At time 1, one unit develops failure type A; at time 2, one different unit experiences competing type B; at time 3, another unit develops type A; and the last unit remains event-free through time 5. We want the absolute probability of having experienced type A by time 4 in this synthetic cohort.

Unit Time Outcome
P 1 Failure type A
Q 2 Competing type B
R 3 Failure type A
S 5 Event-free administrative end

At time 1, all four are at risk. The observed type-A increment in cumulative incidence is \(S_{all}(1-)\cdot(1/4)=1\cdot1/4=0.25\). Immediately after A, event-free survival is \(3/4\). At time 2, the type-B event reduces overall event-free survival to \((3/4)(2/3)=1/2\). At time 3, two units remain at risk, and type A occurs; its increment is \((1/2)(1/2)=1/4\). Thus the actual type-A cumulative incidence by time 4 is \(0.25+0.25=0.50\). Type-B cumulative incidence is 0.25, and the remaining event-free proportion is 0.25; the three probabilities add to one.

KEY EQUATION
\[\widehat F_A(4)=1\cdot\frac14+\frac12\cdot\frac12=\boxed{0.50}.\]

Now incorrectly censor Q at time 2 and apply a Kaplan–Meier curve only to A events. At time 1, survival from A has factor \(3/4\). Q is then removed as a censoring, so at time 3 the risk set is two and the factor for the second A event is \(1/2\). The resulting curve is \(\widehat S_{KM,A}(4)=(3/4)(1/2)=3/8\). Its complement is \(1-3/8=5/8=0.625\), not 0.50. It overstates observed-world type-A incidence in this example by 0.125, or 12.5 percentage points.

KEY EQUATION
\[1-\widehat S_{KM,A}(4)=1-\frac38=\boxed{0.625}\ne\widehat F_A(4)=0.50.\]

The discrepancy is structural. Censoring type B pretends that Q can simply disappear without contributing to the probability of an alternative terminal outcome, whereas cumulative incidence must acknowledge that once B happens, A cannot become the first event for Q. More sophisticated competing-risk regression, including Fine–Gray, is outside this guide's central scope. The essential transferable lesson is to define the desired probability before choosing an estimator.

MODULE 22 / 34

22. The cause-specific decomposition, worked another way

We can verify the preceding example using a probability accounting table at each event time. Start with overall event-free survival 1. At time 1, one A event among four leads to \(\Delta F_A=1\cdot(1/4)=0.25\) and overall remaining survival 0.75. At time 2, the B event among three leads to \(\Delta F_B=0.75(1/3)=0.25\), leaving 0.50 event-free. At time 3, the A event among two leads to another \(0.50(1/2)=0.25\), leaving 0.25 event-free. By time 4, \(F_A+F_B+S_{all}=0.50+0.25+0.25=1\).

Time Risk set just before Event type Overall survival before CIF increment
1 4 A 1.00 A: 0.25
2 3 B 0.75 B: 0.25
3 2 A 0.50 A: 0.25

This is a small-sample form of the Aalen–Johansen cumulative-incidence calculation. It is not simply a renaming of the Cox cause-specific hazard; it uses the overall probability of remaining event-free as the multiplier of each cause-specific event increment. The observed outcomes partition the first-event possibilities. If censoring were present, additional assumptions and risk-set adjustments would be required, but the target definition would remain the same.

Why does this matter for the integrated analysis? If maintenance replacement is a competing event, a model predicting the latent failure time under continued use can answer a hypothetical engineering question, whereas the actual probability of a failure before replacement is a competing-risk quantity. Both may be useful but they are not interchangeable. Decisions about replacement policy can alter the occurrence of the competing event itself, which further complicates interpretation. A descriptive prediction model does not identify the causal effect of introducing a new policy.

The four-unit calculation also illustrates a general check: cumulative incidence curves are nondecreasing and their sum cannot exceed one; all-event survival is nonincreasing. If a plot has type-A incidence 0.7 and type-B incidence 0.6 at the same horizon, at least one estimate, definition or display is wrong, because the mutually exclusive first-event probabilities sum to more than one.

MODULE 23 / 34

23. From evaluation to threshold-based action

A manager may need to choose an intervention threshold. Suppose an inspection costs \(c_I\) per unit, while a failure that would have been preventable costs \(c_F\). A simplistic decision rule might inspect whenever predicted failure risk \(r_i(8)\) exceeds \(c_I/c_F\), but this follows only under strong conditions: inspection must completely avert the relevant failure, the costs must be additive and correctly measured, and there must be no harms or downstream effects. If inspection prevents only a fraction \(e\) of failures, the threshold would involve \(c_I/(e c_F)\) in that simplified expected-cost model.

KEY EQUATION
\[\text{Inspect if }r_i(8)\,e\,c_F>c_I\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r_i(8)>\frac{c_I}{e c_F}.\]

For an illustrative purely hypothetical cost ratio, set \(c_I=20\) cost units, \(c_F=200\), and inspection effectiveness \(e=0.5\). The threshold is \(20/(0.5\cdot200)=0.20\). In our constructed table, all twelve predicted failure risks exceed 0.20, so the simplified policy would inspect everyone. This result exposes rather than hides a problem: the ratio and intervention effect, not only the discrimination metric, determine whether the decision rule is useful. Changing the assumed effectiveness or action capacity changes the choice.

If only three inspections are possible, one could rank the highest predicted failure risks: A 0.76, D 0.70 and B 0.68. Their ranking can be computed from the table, but expected prevention depends on the absolute calibration of those risks and the causal efficacy of inspections. We cannot infer from the synthetic validation records that inspecting these modules would prevent their failures. In real decision analysis, evaluate costs, benefits, equity or operational allocation and potential harms, alongside calibration and uncertainty.

This leads to an important distinction between prediction and intervention. A calibrated model may predict outcomes under historical maintenance policies; acting on its predictions can change the outcome distribution. Deploying a monitoring policy can also change censoring and competing events. Performance after deployment must therefore be monitored, and causal claims about policy benefit demand a suitable experimental or quasi-experimental design, not merely a survival C-index.

MODULE 25 / 34

25. Model diagnostics and sensitivity analyses

A model comparison should deliberately challenge its assumptions. For Cox, inspect whether the association of covariates with hazard is constant in time: plot appropriately scaled Schoenfeld residuals against follow-up and consider time interactions or stratification if a violation is substantively meaningful. For continuous features, check nonlinear effects and influential subjects. A nonsignificant PH test does not prove PH; it may simply have low power, especially with few events. The decision should also consider visual evidence and domain knowledge.

For AFT, check the chosen parametric distribution (Weibull, Lognormal or alternatives), residual patterns and tail behavior. An AFT model's Time Ratio can be intuitive, but an incorrect family may distort absolute horizon risk even when the time coefficient direction is plausible. Compare modeled survival with KM within sensible groups; check whether a probability curve leaves the unit interval or increases with time. Parameter confidence intervals do not capture all uncertainty from misspecification.

For IPCW performance, vary the censoring model and evaluate whether censoring appears related to measured characteristics. Display the smallest estimated \(G(8\mid x)\), largest weights and number of effective contributors. A stabilizing truncation of extreme weights may reduce variance but can introduce bias; it should be prespecified and reported as sensitivity analysis, not silently selected to improve the score. Explore nearby operational horizons while keeping eight as the declared primary endpoint.

Finally examine subgroup transportability: product batches, factories, operating conditions, installation dates and any protected or operationally meaningful groups. Differences in baseline failure risk or censoring can produce calibration drift even when overall C-index appears stable. A robust conclusion is one that explains which assumptions, groups and horizons support it and where it becomes uncertain. “No significant difference” in a small subgroup is not evidence of equivalence.

34. Final synthesis: what constitutes a completed survival evaluation?

A credible survival-model evaluation begins before fitting any mathematical formula: the event, time zero, unit, horizon and observation process must have a coherent scientific meaning. Otherwise a precise C-index or a beautiful survival curve can summarize the wrong target. Our constructed example showed why censoring is not ordinary absence of a binary outcome and why a failure at the horizon must count as a failure if that is the declared rule.

We connected three complementary views. Kaplan–Meier estimated overall survival at eight as about 0.54012 under independent censoring. The fixed illustrative risk ranking gave 42 concordant comparisons among 47 comparable event pairs, or C≈0.8936. The IPCW Brier Score, incorporating a censoring-survival estimate of 27/35 at eight, was about 0.132915. Aggregate predicted failure risk of about 0.5217 could be compared descriptively with KM event probability of about 0.4599, but twelve synthetic records cannot establish deployed calibration. None of those metrics substitutes for the other.

We also derived the decisive competing-risks warning: in a four-unit study, the absolute incidence of type A by time four was 0.50, while one minus KM after censoring type B was 0.625. The discrepancy is not rounding; it comes from asking different scientific questions. This distinction is essential whenever death from other causes, permanent replacement, loan prepayment, customer migration or another terminal outcome prevents the original event from occurring as the first event.

The final practical rule is to make performance claims no broader than the evidence. Validate on genuinely independent subjects and relevant future cohorts; compare identical horizons and endpoints; inspect diagnostics and censoring weights; quantify uncertainty at the unit or cluster level; and judge decision utility with real intervention benefits and harms. A model can order cases well yet predict the wrong absolute probabilities, and a calibrated risk prediction cannot by itself demonstrate that acting upon it will improve outcomes. The evidence must survive these distinctions. That is the transferable competency of this final guide.
