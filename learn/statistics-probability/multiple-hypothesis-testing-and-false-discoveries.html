Preparation and notation

Prerequisites are introductory p-values, Type I error, power and hypothesis testing (Guides 37 and 40). Permutation methods and categorical tests (38 and 45) are helpful but not required. Here alpha denotes the family-wise FWER level; q is the target FDR level; m is the number of hypotheses; and R is the number of rejections.

1. Why one significant result is not enough

Imagine that a product team tests whether twenty different interface changes improve a conversion metric. Every change is ineffective in the population, but each test is performed at a significance level of 0.05. A single test can falsely reject its null hypothesis. When the team looks at twenty tests, the opportunity for at least one false alarm increases. This is not a defect in the definition of a p-value. It is a consequence of making a decision after inspecting a collection of opportunities for significance. The relevant question changes from “How often can this individual test reject incorrectly?” to “How often can this entire research process produce one or more incorrect claims?”

A null hypothesis is a specific statement about a population, such as no difference between two conversion probabilities. A p-value is the probability, calculated under a specified null model, of a result at least as incompatible with that model as the observed result, according to the chosen test statistic. It is not the probability that the null is true. The significance threshold alpha is a rule selected before inspecting results. A false positive, or Type I error, occurs when a true null is rejected. A true discovery occurs when a false null is rejected. A false discovery is a rejected hypothesis whose null was actually true. These terms describe the unknown population state, not something that can ordinarily be read directly from a dataset.

This guide studies how to define a family of tests, quantify the accumulation of false claims, and choose among procedures with distinct error guarantees. The central theme is not that all small p-values should be distrusted. It is that the unit of error control must match the unit of scientific decision making. If the team plans to advertise any successful change, a family-wide guarantee may be appropriate. If a screening program expects a list of candidates for later validation, controlling the expected fraction of false leads among reported leads may be more useful. Neither choice excuses poor study design, selective reporting, invalid individual tests, or exaggerated causal conclusions.

2. Families and the probability of at least one error

A family is the set of hypotheses treated together for a scientific claim or decision. It must be defined from the research question, not chosen afterward to make an adjustment look favorable. All outcomes in a prespecified primary analysis might form one family; a separate set of genuinely distinct secondary questions might form another, provided that separation is scientifically justified. Merely calling each result a different family defeats the purpose. The number m denotes how many hypotheses belong to the family. The number m0, generally unknown, denotes how many of those null hypotheses are actually true.

Suppose m independent tests have true null hypotheses, each rejects with probability alpha, and the tests are exact at that level. The probability that no test falsely rejects is (1−alpha)^m: independence permits multiplication of the probabilities of avoiding an error. Therefore the probability of one or more false rejections is 1−(1−alpha)^m. At alpha=0.05 and m=20, this is 1−0.95^20, approximately 0.6415. Thus there is roughly a 64% probability of at least one false alarm under this particular global-null, independence scenario. The expected number of false rejections is m alpha=1, because expectations add even without independence. Expecting one false rejection does not mean exactly one occurs, and the 64% result is not universal: dependence changes the probability of at least one error.

If all twenty tests are perfectly identical, their outcomes rise and fall together. The probability of at least one false rejection is then 0.05, not 0.6415. More generally, when individual tests have valid Type I error at most alpha, the union bound guarantees that the chance of at least one false rejection is no more than m alpha, capped at one, regardless of dependence. The independence calculation illustrates a risk; the union bound gives a conservative guarantee. Confusing these statements leads to unjustified precision.

\[\Pr(V\ge1)=1-(1-\alpha)^m,\qquad E[V]=m\alpha\quad(\text{all nulls true})\]

3. The error-counting table: V, S, R and m

To compare error criteria we need a bookkeeping system that separates decisions from the truth. Among m hypotheses, m0 null hypotheses are true and m−m0 are false. The procedure rejects R hypotheses. Of these, V are false discoveries: true null hypotheses rejected by mistake. The remaining S=R−V are true discoveries. Among hypotheses not rejected, m0−V are true nulls correctly retained, while m−m0−S are false nulls not detected. These four cells sum to m. Although R is observed, V and S normally are not; they require knowledge of the true population state.

The family-wise error rate, FWER, is P(V≥1), the probability of making at least one false discovery. Strong FWER control at level alpha means that this probability is at most alpha for every configuration of true and false null hypotheses allowed by the method's assumptions. Weak control only guarantees the bound under the global null, when all hypotheses are true. A method can control weakly without controlling strongly, so the distinction matters when real effects coexist with null effects.

The false discovery proportion, FDP, is V/R when R>0 and is defined as zero when R=0. The false discovery rate, FDR, is E[FDP], the expectation of this random fraction over repeated realizations of the whole analysis. If a method controls FDR at q=0.05, it does not promise that at most 5% of the discoveries in this particular dataset are false. It promises an average property across repeated studies under its conditions. Nor does it assign a 5% posterior probability of falsity to each selected hypothesis. When R=0, the zero convention prevents division by zero and gives an intelligible error proportion: no discoveries means no false discoveries among discoveries.

\[\mathrm{FWER}=\Pr(V\ge1),\qquad \mathrm{FDP}=\frac{V}{\max(R,1)},\qquad \mathrm{FDR}=E[\mathrm{FDP}]\]

4. Bonferroni: a guarantee without independence

Bonferroni controls FWER by requiring each unadjusted p-value to be no greater than alpha/m. Why divide by m? For a true null, validity of a p-value means P(p≤t)≤t for thresholds t between zero and one. The probability that any true null is rejected is at most the sum of its individual rejection probabilities, by the union bound. If there are m0 true nulls, this is at most m0 alpha/m≤alpha. The proof does not require independence among p-values and works under any configuration of true nulls. It does require valid p-values and a properly specified family.

Consider eight prespecified product outcomes with p-values 0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.019, 0.041, 0.060, 0.210, and 0.640. The values are deliberately constructed for teaching, not collected from customers. At alpha=0.05, the Bonferroni cutoff is 0.05/8=0.00625. Only 0.001 passes. The p-value 0.008 is significant as a single unadjusted test but does not pass the family-wise rule. That is not proof that its population effect is absent; it means the evidence does not satisfy the selected error guarantee.

An adjusted Bonferroni p-value is min(1,m p). For the second outcome, it is min(1,8×0.008)=0.064. We can compare 0.064 with the familiar 0.05 instead of comparing 0.008 with 0.00625. The first adjusted value is 0.008. The adjusted value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and multiplying by m is not a correction to the estimated effect size. Bonferroni is simple and broadly valid, but it can lose substantial power when the family is large or many tests are correlated.

\[p_i\le\frac{\alpha}{m},\qquad p_{i,\mathrm{Bonf}}^{\mathrm{adj}}=\min(1,mp_i)\]

5. Holm: step-down family-wise control

Holm improves on Bonferroni without assuming independence. Sort p-values from smallest to largest: p(1)≤…≤p(m). Compare p(1) with alpha/m, p(2) with alpha/(m−1), and in general p(i) with alpha/(m−i+1). Continue rejecting while comparisons succeed; stop at the first failure and retain that hypothesis and all later ones. The word step-down refers to the fact that we begin with the strongest evidence and move toward weaker evidence, while the denominator decreases. This procedure provides strong FWER control for valid p-values under arbitrary dependence.

For our eight ordered p-values the successive thresholds are 0.00625, 0.0071429, 0.0083333, 0.010, 0.0125, 0.0166667, 0.025, and 0.05. The first p-value 0.001 passes 0.00625. The second p-value 0.008 exceeds 0.0071429. We must stop, even if a later comparison would happen to pass its own threshold. Consequently Holm rejects only the first hypothesis in this example. With a different set of p-values Holm can reject more than Bonferroni, but never fewer at the same alpha. In this particular example their decisions coincide; that is a useful reminder that improved potential power does not imply a different decision in every dataset.

Holm-adjusted p-values can be computed by taking cumulative maxima of (m−i+1)p(i), capped at one. For our ordered data the raw products are 0.008, 0.056, 0.072, 0.095, 0.164, 0.180, 0.420, and 0.640. These are already nondecreasing, so the adjusted values equal these products. The cumulative maximum is essential in general: an adjusted p-value sequence must respect the ordering and the stopping rule. To verify the second result, 7×0.008=0.056>0.05, so Holm does not reject it. The adjusted p-value remains a frequentist threshold summary, not a posterior probability.

\[p_{(i)}\le\frac{\alpha}{m-i+1};\qquad p_{(i),\mathrm{Holm}}^{\mathrm{adj}}=\min\left(1,\max_{j\le i}(m-j+1)p_{(j)}\right)\]

6. Benjamini–Hochberg: controlling a different target

The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, abbreviated BH, targets FDR rather than FWER. Order the same m p-values. For a desired FDR level q, draw the increasing critical line i q/m for ranks i=1,…,m. Find the largest rank k satisfying p(k)≤k q/m, and reject all hypotheses with ranks 1 through k. Unlike Holm, BH is a step-up rule: we look for the largest qualifying rank rather than stopping at the first failed comparison. This distinction is important because an earlier p-value can fail its individual rank comparison while a later one passes; the step-up rule then rejects all earlier ranks as well.

At q=0.05 and m=8 the BH thresholds are 0.00625, 0.0125, 0.01875, 0.025, 0.03125, 0.0375, 0.04375, and 0.05. Compare the ordered p-values: 0.001 passes, 0.008 passes, 0.012 passes, 0.019 passes, 0.041 fails, and the remaining values fail. The largest qualifying rank is four. BH therefore rejects the first four hypotheses, while Holm and Bonferroni reject only one. It is incorrect to say BH “found three extra true effects”: it made three additional rejections under a different error criterion, and the truth of those hypotheses is unknown.

The BH-adjusted p-value at rank i is the minimum of 1 and all later ratios m p(j)/j for j≥i. Compute from the largest rank backward using cumulative minima. For this example the adjusted values, in ascending p-value order, are 0.008, 0.032, 0.032, 0.038, 0.0656, 0.080, 0.240, and 0.640. The third adjusted value equals 0.032; it agrees with the third raw rank ratio 8×0.012/3=0.032 of this example's ratios. Each of the first four adjusted values is no greater than q=0.05. The adjusted BH p-values are sometimes called q-values informally, but a formally defined q-value can refer to a related yet distinct concept, depending on the framework.

\[k=\max\{i:p_{(i)}\le iq/m\};\qquad p_{(i),\mathrm{BH}}^{\mathrm{adj}}=\min\left(1,\min_{j\ge i}mp_{(j)}/j\right)\]

7. Assumptions, dependence, and what the guarantee covers

The guarantees of a multiple-testing method begin with valid individual p-values. Under a true null, a valid p-value is super-uniform: P(p≤t)≤t for every t between zero and one. Continuous exact tests often achieve equality; discrete tests can be conservative, with fewer possible p-values. Invalid tests, data leakage, inappropriate independence assumptions, or unreported analytic choices can destroy the premise of the correction. Adjusting invalid p-values does not automatically make them valid.

Bonferroni and Holm control FWER under arbitrary dependence among valid p-values. The classical BH FDR guarantee holds under independence of the p-values and under certain positive dependence conditions, often summarized by positive regression dependence on a subset (PRDS) of true-null p-values. It does not follow automatically under arbitrary dependence. In the independent case a classical result bounds FDR by (m0/m)q≤q. This theorem is cited here; a full proof requires conditional probability arguments beyond our introductory scope. Its intuition is that the increasing rank thresholds allocate a larger cutoff when the procedure discovers more hypotheses, while the expected proportion of erroneous selections remains controlled under the dependence conditions.

For arbitrary dependence, Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) offers a more conservative step-up adjustment using thresholds i q/(m H_m), where H_m=1+1/2+…+1/m is the harmonic sum. For m=8, H_8≈2.7179, so the BY thresholds are BH thresholds divided by about 2.718. This additional safeguard can greatly reduce discoveries. Another valid strategy is to use Holm when the primary priority is avoiding any false positive. Neither adjustment solves problems of confounding or makes observational associations causal. State the scientific design and the multiplicity guarantee separately.

8. Planning, reporting, and scientific interpretation

Multiplicity is not only a calculation at the end of a spreadsheet. Researchers make choices about outcomes, subgroups, transformations, time windows, model specifications, and stopping rules. If they try many alternatives and report only the most favorable, the effective search family may be far larger than the visible table. The adjustment must match the opportunities that influenced selection. A preregistered analysis plan can identify primary outcomes, family boundaries, thresholds, exclusions, and planned contrasts before results are known. Preregistration does not guarantee valid inference, but it makes the intended decision process inspectable.

A confirmatory claim typically calls for a carefully justified family-wise error criterion, particularly when even one false positive carries high costs. Exploratory research may reasonably prioritize FDR to produce candidates for follow-up. However, a reported FDR of 5% is not a guarantee that 95% of the selected hypotheses are true in this study. Nor is an adjusted p-value a measure of clinical importance or practical value. Always report effect estimates, uncertainty intervals where appropriate, sample size, measurement units, design limitations, and the multiplicity procedure. A statistically detectable change of 0.01 percentage points may have little practical relevance, while a meaningful effect can remain uncertain because the study has low power.

A useful report might state: “Eight outcomes were prespecified as one family. Holm's procedure at FWER 0.05 rejected one null hypothesis; BH at target FDR 0.05 rejected four. These decisions reflect different error guarantees. Results are exploratory and require independent confirmation.” This wording does not claim to know which findings are false, and it does not turn a screening result into a causal conclusion. The family definition, test validity, and dependence assumptions are part of the result, not footnotes to be forgotten.

Deep study: proofs, decisions and applications

These sections develop the underlying logic, not just a recipe. All numerical examples are hypothetical and their assumptions are stated explicitly.

1. A diagnostic example: twenty outcomes in one study

A public health team conducts one observational study and reports twenty tests of associations between a dietary measure and twenty health outcomes. The twenty null hypotheses are not identical: each refers to a different outcome. Yet the decision to announce any association makes the entire set relevant. Suppose, only for this illustration, that all twenty population associations are zero, every individual p-value is valid and uniformly distributed under its null, and tests are independent. For one outcome, the chance of a p-value at most 0.05 is 0.05. For two outcomes the chance that neither is below that threshold is 0.95 times 0.95, or 0.9025. Extending to twenty gives 0.95 raised to twenty, approximately 0.3585. Subtract from one: 0.6415. This number describes repeated studies conducted under those stated assumptions, not the probability that a particular published claim is false.

Now distinguish the experiment's two error summaries. Let X_i equal one when test i falsely rejects, otherwise zero. Then the number of false discoveries V is the sum of these twenty indicators. By linearity of expectation E[V] is twenty times 0.05, or one. Linearity does not need independence; independence was used for the probability of at least one error. In some repeated experiments no error occurs, in some one, and in others two or more. Their average count can be one while the chance of at least one error is about 0.64. These answer different questions and must not be presented interchangeably.

What should a reader do if the outcomes are correlated? Do not apply the 0.6415 number as a fact. Correlation of physiological outcomes can induce correlation between test statistics. Independence is a property of the joint distribution, not merely of different outcome names. A safely conservative bound follows without that property: the probability of the union of twenty false rejection events is at most the sum of their probabilities, twenty times 0.05. That upper bound is one after capping, and is therefore not informative here. Correction procedures choose smaller per-test thresholds to obtain a useful family-wide bound. The individual association tests also remain subject to confounding: finding a relationship in an observational study, corrected or not, does not demonstrate that diet caused the outcome.

Finally consider a different scientific purpose. If the team declares a single primary outcome in advance and labels nineteen genuinely secondary outcomes as exploratory, it should clearly explain the primary claim, its prespecified analysis, and the multiplicity policy for secondary findings. The family cannot be retroactively redefined after observing which of the twenty tests happened to be significant. The act of designing and reporting the analysis is as important as the adjustment formula.

2. What makes a p-value valid for adjustment?

Multiplicity adjustment starts with the validity of each input p-value. For a true null hypothesis, a valid p-value P obeys Pr(P at most t) at most t for every threshold t between zero and one. This property is called super-uniformity. A continuous exact test often has equality. A discrete exact test may have strict inequality, because its possible p-values jump in steps. In both situations Bonferroni and Holm can work with the same bound. An invalid p-value can be overly small too often; multiplying it by the number of tests does not magically repair a misspecified test or a broken sampling process.

For example, suppose a test reports 0.02. That does not mean there is a two percent chance the null is true. The computation assumes a null model, a statistic, a sampling procedure, and a definition of outcomes at least as extreme. If researchers choose the statistic after seeing the data, ignore dependence among observations, omit an important design feature, or stop data collection whenever a favorable result appears, the nominal p-value need not be valid. Correcting twenty invalid p-values might still produce invalid claims. The logical order is design, individual-test validity, family definition, then multiplicity adjustment.

Check the distinction with an exact coin experiment. Under a fair-coin null, let X count heads in four independent flips. The possible outcomes for X are zero through four with probabilities 1/16, 4/16, 6/16, 4/16, and 1/16. For a one-sided test favoring unusually many heads, observing X=4 has p-value 1/16=0.0625; observing X=3 has p-value 5/16=0.3125. There is no attainable p-value between 0.0625 and 0.3125 for this test. At a significance threshold of 0.05, it never rejects, so its actual null rejection probability is zero. This is conservative rather than invalid. It teaches why not every p-value has an exactly uniform null distribution and why ordinary calculations need careful qualification.

A practical checklist asks whether data points are independent when assumed, whether paired observations were treated as paired, whether estimates share an appropriate standard error, whether missing data change the target population, and whether model assumptions are credible. Those questions belong to the individual tests. Multiple-testing adjustment answers a subsequent question about the collection of valid tests. It does not replace subject-matter expertise or data-quality assessment.

3. Deriving the Bonferroni guarantee, step by step

Why is alpha divided by m? Imagine m hypotheses and focus only on the m0 hypotheses whose nulls are truly correct, even though we do not know which ones they are. Under the Bonferroni rule, reject a null only if its unadjusted p-value is at most alpha/m. Let A_i denote the event that true null i is rejected. The probability of one or more false discoveries is the probability of the union of the A_i events over the true nulls. The union bound says this probability is at most the sum of the individual event probabilities. Valid p-values guarantee that each term is at most alpha/m. There are m0 terms, giving FWER at most m0 alpha/m, which is at most alpha because m0 is at most m. This argument never multiplies joint probabilities; independence is unnecessary.

Substitute alpha=0.05, m=8. The individual cutoff is 0.00625. If all eight null hypotheses are true, the bound is eight times 0.00625, exactly 0.05. If only three are true, the bound is three times 0.00625, or 0.01875. This latter number is an upper bound, not an exact achieved error rate. The method does not need to know m0; using all m possible hypotheses is precisely how it remains safe. The guarantee is strong because it holds regardless of which hypotheses are truly null, provided their p-values are valid.

The adjustment can also be read through a threshold reversal. A hypothesis with original p=0.004 passes a 0.00625 cutoff. Alternatively compute its Bonferroni adjusted p-value min(1,8 times 0.004)=0.032 and compare that with 0.05. Both approaches agree. For p=0.008, eight times 0.008 equals 0.064, above 0.05, so it does not pass. For p=0.20, multiplying by eight gives 1.60, but p-values cannot exceed one; the reported adjusted value is capped at 1.00. This cap changes no decision at thresholds below one.

Bonferroni can be conservative when tests are strongly dependent or many nulls are false. Conservative means its actual error probability can be smaller than the nominal ceiling, not that its conclusions are automatically correct. A failure to reject does not establish a zero effect; it can reflect insufficient precision. The logical tradeoff is fewer false positive opportunities in exchange for potentially fewer detected real effects. A scientifically valuable report therefore couples rejection decisions with estimated effect sizes and uncertainty intervals, adjusted for multiplicity when simultaneous interval claims are intended.

4. Holm: why its step-down proof works

Holm improves on Bonferroni by using a sequence of thresholds. After sorting p-values from smallest to largest, compare the smallest with alpha/m, the second with alpha/(m−1), and so on. Stop immediately when a comparison fails. This is not a heuristic: the stopping rule is essential to the guarantee. Later thresholds are larger because fewer hypotheses remain under consideration, but the procedure never skips a failed earlier comparison to accept a later one. The result is at least as many rejections as single-step Bonferroni for the same valid p-values.

A proof sketch clarifies the protection. Suppose exactly m0 nulls are true. Order all m p-values and look at the smallest p-value belonging to a true null. Imagine the procedure is about to reject this first true null after perhaps rejecting several false nulls. Before reaching this position, it could have encountered at most m−m0 false nulls. Therefore at least m0 hypotheses remain at this step. The Holm threshold used here is no larger than alpha/m0. If the procedure ever commits a false rejection, the smallest true-null p-value must be at most alpha/m0. Applying the union bound across the m0 true nulls gives probability at most m0 times alpha/m0, equal to alpha. No joint independence assumption was used. This explains strong FWER control rather than merely asserting it.

Work through four values: 0.003, 0.014, 0.028 and 0.20 at alpha=0.05. The ordered cutoffs are 0.05/4=0.0125, 0.05/3≈0.016667, 0.05/2=0.025 and 0.05/1=0.05. At rank one, 0.003≤0.0125: reject. At rank two, 0.014≤0.016667: reject. At rank three, 0.028>0.025: stop. The fourth result is not considered for rejection, even if a hypothetical later comparison might pass. Bonferroni would reject only the first result because 0.014 exceeds 0.0125. The extra rejection from Holm is compatible with the same family error guarantee.

The adjusted p-value calculation implements exactly this sequential logic. First compute raw values 4×0.003=0.012, 3×0.014=0.042, 2×0.028=0.056 and 1×0.20=0.20. Then take running maxima: 0.012, 0.042, 0.056 and 0.20. At level 0.05 the first two pass. If a later raw value were smaller than an earlier one, the running maximum would prevent an adjusted p-value from decreasing with rank. Finally, restore the original hypothesis labels if the input was not already sorted. Sorting is a computational convenience, not a permission to relabel the scientific questions.

5. Benjamini–Hochberg as a step-up decision

BH makes a different promise from Holm. It seeks to control the expected proportion of false discoveries among all reported discoveries, with the proportion defined as zero when nothing is rejected. Fix a desired FDR level q before inspecting the p-values and sort the m values. At ordered rank i, the BH reference line has height i q/m. Find the largest rank k where the observed p-value is no larger than that reference value, then reject all hypotheses at ranks one through k. The phrase largest rank is crucial: BH is a step-up procedure, not a rule that stops as soon as an early comparison fails.

Consider five hypothetical tests at q=0.05. Their sorted p-values are 0.012, 0.019, 0.029, 0.041 and 0.40. The thresholds by rank are 0.010, 0.020, 0.030, 0.040 and 0.050. At rank one, 0.012 exceeds 0.010 and fails. At rank two, 0.019 passes 0.020. Rank three passes because 0.029≤0.030. Rank four fails because 0.041>0.040, and rank five fails because 0.40>0.05. The largest passing rank is three. BH therefore rejects the first three hypotheses, including rank one even though its individual point lay above the first threshold. The interpretation is not that the first comparison was retrospectively mistaken. It is that the global step-up rule bases its rejection set on the largest qualifying rank.

We can reproduce the same decision using adjusted p-values. Compute preliminary ratios m p_(i)/i: 5×0.012/1=0.060; 5×0.019/2=0.0475; 5×0.029/3≈0.048333; 5×0.041/4=0.05125; 5×0.40/5=0.40. These preliminary ratios are not necessarily monotone. Move backward from the largest rank, taking the smallest ratio seen to the right, and cap at one. The resulting adjusted values are 0.0475, 0.0475, approximately 0.048333, 0.05125 and 0.40. Compare each with q=0.05: exactly the first three qualify. This backward cumulative minimum is as important as the multiplication itself.

What can this result claim? Under the standard BH conditions, which include valid null p-values and suitable independence or positive dependence conditions, the FDR does not exceed q; in the classical independent setting a sharper expression is m0 q/m. It does not mean that each accepted hypothesis has no more than five percent probability of being false. Nor does it ensure that there will be no false discovery in a particular dataset. A useful application is candidate screening where later verification is built into the research program.

6. Interpreting FDR with repeated experiments

The word expected in the definition of FDR deserves its own calculation. Suppose we imagine four equally likely kinds of study outcomes, not four hypotheses: in the first repetition R=0 and V=0; in the second R=4 and V=0; in the third R=2 and V=1; in the fourth R=5 and V=1. Compute the false discovery proportion FDP in each: zero by convention, zero divided by four equals zero, one divided by two equals 0.50, and one divided by five equals 0.20. The mean across these four hypothetical repetition types is (0+0+0.50+0.20)/4=0.175. This toy repetition distribution has FDR 17.5 percent. It is deliberately not a claim that a real BH experiment at q=0.05 would produce this distribution; it is an exercise showing what an expectation averages.

Contrast FWER using exactly the same four repetitions. In the first two repetitions V=0; in the last two V≥1. Thus the probability of at least one false discovery in this illustrative distribution is two out of four, or 0.50. Notice how FWER=0.50 and FDR=0.175 summarize the same outcomes differently. In the third repetition the false discovery fraction was 50 percent, much greater than 17.5 percent; a bound on an average does not bound each realization. The fourth repetition has one false claim but a smaller proportion because five claims were reported. Neither error measure tells us which individual null hypothesis is actually true.

A related quantity is the positive false discovery rate, sometimes written pFDR, the expected FDP conditional on at least one rejection. It differs from FDR whenever there is positive probability of R=0. The present guide uses ordinary FDR, with FDP set to zero when no discoveries occur. Do not silently replace one criterion with another. Other approaches, such as local false discovery rates, use additional modeling and can answer different probabilistic questions. In particular, a posterior probability of null status requires a model and prior information not contained in the frequentist BH-adjusted p-value.

Translate the mathematics into a communication rule. Instead of saying “this specific selected finding is 95 percent likely to be real,” write “the selection procedure was designed to control the expected false-discovery proportion at five percent under the specified assumptions.” The former is a statement about an individual hypothesis after seeing data; the latter is a repeated-sampling guarantee for a procedure. The difference survives translation into any scientific domain, from genomic screening to online experiments. It also survives when adjusted p-values look impressively small.

7. Dependence: what can and cannot be guaranteed

Tests are rarely isolated. Two clinical endpoints may measure overlapping aspects of the same condition; several marketing metrics may share the same users; thousands of gene-expression measures may belong to correlated biological pathways. Dependent tests are not automatically invalid tests. The relevant question is whether dependence breaks a theorem used to justify the chosen multiple-testing procedure. Bonferroni and Holm require valid null p-values but not independence among those p-values. Classical BH has a more qualified guarantee: independence is sufficient, and important positive-dependence conditions also permit FDR control. Arbitrary dependence is not covered by the basic BH assertion.

One safe alternative for FDR control under arbitrary dependence is the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure. It replaces the BH reference level q by q/c_m, where c_m is the harmonic sum 1+1/2+...+1/m. For m=4, c_4=1+0.5+0.333333+0.25≈2.083333. If q=0.05, the effective value is approximately 0.024. The rank thresholds become (i/4)×0.024: about 0.006, 0.012, 0.018 and 0.024. Compare these with ordinary BH thresholds 0.0125, 0.025, 0.0375 and 0.05. BY is more stringent because it is buying a broader dependence guarantee.

Apply this to p-values 0.003, 0.014, 0.028 and 0.20. Under ordinary BH at q=0.05, the first three ranks pass and rank four fails; three hypotheses are selected. Under BY, rank one passes because 0.003≤0.006, rank two fails because 0.014>0.012, rank three fails because 0.028>0.018 and rank four fails. The largest passing rank is one, so only the first is selected. This difference does not prove ordinary BH was wrong for those data. It demonstrates that the chosen assumptions and error guarantee alter how conservatively we select findings.

Dependence requires substantive judgment. Positive correlation alone is not a universal certificate of the precise mathematical positive dependence condition required by a theorem, and negative or complex dependencies can be harder to characterize. A researcher may instead use a justified resampling-based simultaneous method, but its validity depends on the experimental design and exchangeability conditions. If dependence cannot be characterized, present the uncertainty explicitly and choose a procedure whose assumptions can be defended. In teaching and reporting, the conditional phrase “under the stated assumptions” is part of the theorem, not a disclaimer that can be detached from it.

8. One complete family, three procedures, one decision record

Return to the eight prespecified hypothetical outcomes used in the comparison table. They have p-values 0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.019, 0.041, 0.060, 0.210 and 0.640. None is a measured dataset: these values are educational inputs. Set m=8 and alpha=q=0.05. Bonferroni compares each with 0.05/8=0.00625. The first p-value 0.001 passes and the second 0.008 fails; all others are larger. Thus Bonferroni rejects only H1. The result is a claim about which statistical decisions pass the rule, not about how many population nulls are actually true.

For Holm, preserve the sorted order and write every threshold: 0.00625, approximately 0.007143, approximately 0.008333, 0.010, 0.0125, approximately 0.016667, 0.025 and 0.05. H1 passes because 0.001≤0.00625. H2 fails because 0.008>0.007143. The step-down algorithm stops; no further hypotheses are rejected. Holm therefore also reports H1 alone. Its adjusted p-values, using running maxima of (8−i+1)p_(i), are 0.008, 0.056, 0.072, 0.095, 0.164, 0.180, 0.420 and 0.640. Check rank three: the raw product is 6×0.012=0.072; because the previous running maximum is 0.056, the adjusted value is 0.072. At rank six, 3×0.060=0.180, larger than the previous 0.164. All reported values are nondecreasing.

BH has reference cutoffs 0.00625, 0.0125, 0.01875, 0.025, 0.03125, 0.0375, 0.04375 and 0.05. Rank one passes, rank two passes, rank three passes and rank four passes because 0.019≤0.025. Rank five fails because 0.041>0.03125; ranks six through eight fail as well. The largest passing rank is four, so BH reports H1 through H4. The adjusted BH values are 0.008, 0.032, 0.032, 0.038, 0.0656, 0.080, 0.240 and 0.640. Verify the shared value at ranks two and three: their raw ratios are 8×0.008/2=0.032 and 8×0.012/3=0.032. At rank four, 8×0.019/4=0.038. At rank five, 8×0.041/5=0.0656, which is above 0.05.

A transparent decision record should retain the original outcome name, unadjusted p-value, family size, chosen method, adjusted p-value, threshold, rejection decision and assumption statement. A report that lists only “four significant results” hides the difference between testing for any false rejection and controlling a mean false-discovery fraction. The procedures do not rank effects by importance; a small p-value can reflect a modest but precisely estimated effect. Do not use the number of rejections as a substitute for estimated practical benefit or causal credibility.

9. Choosing the family before examining outcomes

The family is not necessarily every hypothesis ever studied by a laboratory, nor automatically every row in a computer file. It is the set of opportunities for error relevant to a defined claim and reporting rule. Consider a clinical trial with one prespecified primary endpoint, three prespecified co-primary endpoints required jointly for success, and ten secondary endpoints. The analysis strategy depends on the claim: does success require all co-primary tests, any one of them, or a hierarchical sequence of questions? These rules have distinct error structures. Without defining the decision rule, saying “there were fourteen tests” is not enough to select a principled adjustment.

A useful example is a company evaluating a new product through six subgroup analyses: new users, returning users, desktop users, mobile users, users in region A and users in region B. These groups overlap, and the company intends to advertise the most favorable subgroup. Even if each subgroup comparison uses different observations in part, the marketing claim arises from searching among six opportunities. A reasonable family includes the six prespecified subgroup claims. Calling the best subgroup the “primary hypothesis” after inspecting its p-value would hide the selection step. If a single primary comparison was genuinely fixed before the experiment, its evidentiary status is different, but the analyst must demonstrate that it was fixed rather than retrospectively nominated.

Separate families can be justified when there are different scientific questions and independently articulated decisions. For example, a trial might have a specified confirmatory effectiveness family and a descriptive safety surveillance family. But splitting families changes the collection of claims protected by each error guarantee. If investigators subsequently make one broad claim such as “the product improves at least one measured dimension,” dividing the dimensions without accounting for that overarching decision may understate the chance of false claims. The family definition should be written in ordinary language before numerical adjustment begins.

Hierarchical testing offers another design. Under a valid prespecified gatekeeping rule, one hypothesis is tested first and a later family is examined for confirmatory claims only after the gate passes. The exact strong-control property depends on the specified gatekeeping method; merely doing two rounds of unadjusted testing is not an automatic guarantee. Similarly, prespecifying weights for more important hypotheses can produce weighted corrections, but choosing favorable weights after seeing data defeats the intended protection. The general principle is that flexibility must be included in the error accounting.

Document the final family as a reproducible object: its hypothesis labels, test definitions, population, outcome measurement times, exclusions, subgroup boundaries, direction of alternatives, and decision policy. If this documentation changes after the data are observed, explain the change and distinguish exploratory analysis from originally planned confirmatory inference. Transparency about family definition is not administrative paperwork. It is part of what makes an error-rate statement meaningful.

10. Selection, optional analysis and unseen tests

A spreadsheet with eight p-values is not necessarily a study with only eight opportunities for significance. Imagine an analyst considered four definitions of the outcome, five demographic subgroups and three alternative covariate adjustments, then displayed only the combination with the smallest p-value. Even if these choices are not all independent, the visible result has emerged from a much larger search space. A correction computed only across the eight rows in the final table may not account for the earlier selection. The crucial issue is not the number of lines in the published report but the set of data-dependent choices that could have generated a claim.

Consider repeated peeking. A product team runs a two-sample test every day and promises to stop collecting data as soon as an ordinary p-value falls below 0.05. The usual fixed-sample p-value calibration generally does not justify this sequential stopping rule. Even if the team later applies Holm to several product metrics, that adjustment addresses multiplicity across metrics, not automatically the additional repeated looks over time. Appropriate sequential monitoring requires a design and inference method tailored to optional stopping, such as a prespecified group-sequential procedure or other valid always-valid approach. The present guide does not derive those methods; it identifies a boundary of ordinary corrections.

Selection may also enter through model choice: trying ten covariate sets, reporting only the model with a favorable coefficient, and presenting its p-value as though the model was fixed in advance. Another path is selective publication. If many studies are conducted but only those with small p-values appear, an individual published table can suggest more convincing evidence than the full research process supports. No post hoc multiplication by the number of visible findings can fully reconstruct unknown, unreported analyses. This is why prespecified analysis plans, registered reports, shared code and complete outcome tables complement formal multiple-testing procedures.

A practical exercise in reporting is to distinguish three statements. “We planned eight tests and applied Holm to all eight” describes confirmatory procedure design. “After seeing the data we chose eight attractive tests from fifty candidates and applied Holm to eight” does not offer the same protection for the original search. “We screened fifty candidates using BH at a prespecified FDR target, then independently validated selected leads” describes an exploratory discovery pipeline with explicit follow-up. These strategies are not interchangeable. The third can be scientifically valuable even when it is not confirmatory evidence for each lead.

When results were genuinely exploratory, present them as such. State which decisions were post hoc, how many alternatives were considered when known, and what new data would be needed for validation. Scientific integrity does not require pretending that useful patterns were never discovered by exploration. It requires not rebranding a data-driven discovery as an untouched prospective hypothesis.

11. Power, effect size and simultaneous uncertainty

Multiplicity correction changes detection thresholds; it does not change the physical size of an estimated effect. Suppose a product experiment estimates an increase of 0.3 percentage points in conversion, with a standard error of 0.1 percentage points. The ratio of estimate to standard error is three. That ratio can yield relatively strong evidence against a zero-effect null under suitable approximate testing assumptions. But the business decision may depend on whether a 0.3 percentage-point gain pays for implementation costs. A p-value, adjusted or unadjusted, cannot answer that financial question without effect magnitudes, uncertainty, costs, and context.

Consider two hypothetical endpoints in the same study. Endpoint A has estimated effect 0.2 units with standard error 0.05 units, giving statistic 4. Endpoint B has estimated effect 10 units with standard error 5 units, giving statistic 2. Under a simple normal-reference illustration, A has much stronger evidence against its zero null, while B has the larger numerical effect in its own units. We cannot compare practical magnitudes without understanding the units and consequences. Adjustments operate on evidentiary quantities derived from tests, not on a universal scale of value. A corrected result that is statistically convincing may still be substantively trivial.

The cost of correction often appears as lower power, the probability of rejecting when a specified alternative effect truly exists. For a two-sided normal approximation, the unadjusted rejection boundary might use absolute z greater than about 1.96 at alpha=0.05. With eight Bonferroni tests, each uses alpha/8=0.00625, producing a more stringent critical z magnitude, approximately 2.73. Holding sample size, effect and noise fixed, it is harder for the statistic to cross 2.73 than 1.96. Hence power generally decreases. This does not mean corrections are mistakes; the study has deliberately accepted a stricter evidentiary requirement for a particular error guarantee.

Researchers can respond by planning sufficient sample size, prioritizing scientifically central hypotheses, reducing redundant outcomes where substantively justified, using a more powerful valid procedure such as Holm instead of Bonferroni, or adopting a preregistered exploratory screening stage followed by independent replication. Increasing sample size solely to force small p-values, however, can make negligible effects look highly significant. Design must consider minimal meaningful effects and harms as well as test power.

Confidence intervals also require careful handling. Separate 95 percent intervals do not generally ensure that all statements about a family of parameters are simultaneously correct with probability 95 percent. One simple simultaneous strategy uses Bonferroni-adjusted confidence levels, for instance each interval at 1−0.05/8=99.375 percent for eight parameters, under valid individual interval construction. The union bound then limits the probability that at least one interval misses its true parameter to at most 0.05. These wider intervals reflect the same simultaneous error objective. The detailed construction depends on the parameter estimator and sampling model, and should not be confused with BH-adjusted p-values.

12. A second case: screening biomarkers responsibly

Imagine a laboratory screening six biological biomarkers for association with a clinical outcome. The following six p-values are constructed, not observed in patients: 0.002, 0.009, 0.024, 0.047, 0.11 and 0.70. The team plans an exploratory shortlist, has a justified independence model for the tests in this simplified exercise, and chooses BH with q=0.10 before seeing results. This higher q illustrates a screening priority; it is not a universal recommendation for medical research. It still requires honest disclosure and later verification, especially if false leads would cause costly downstream decisions.

Compute the six BH thresholds: at ranks one through six, i×0.10/6 gives approximately 0.016667, 0.033333, 0.050000, 0.066667, 0.083333 and 0.100000. Compare in order. The first p-value 0.002 passes; the second 0.009 passes; the third 0.024 passes; the fourth 0.047 passes; the fifth 0.11 fails; and the sixth 0.70 fails. The largest passing rank is four. Four biomarkers enter the shortlist. Under the stated assumptions, BH controls the expected false-discovery proportion at the prespecified target; that does not identify which selected biomarker associations truly exist.

Apply Holm at FWER alpha=0.05 to the same table. The first cutoff is 0.05/6≈0.008333, which 0.002 passes. The second cutoff is 0.05/5=0.010, which 0.009 passes. The third cutoff is 0.05/4=0.0125, which 0.024 fails. The Holm stopping rule leaves only two discoveries. Observe that both methods operate on exactly the same data but use different error concepts and, in this example, different numerical targets. It would be misleading to attribute the difference solely to the algorithm while overlooking q=0.10 versus alpha=0.05. If comparison at the same nominal 0.05 is desired, recalculate BH thresholds rather than mixing guarantees.

Before elevating these results into medical recommendations, the study needs independent validation and substantive scrutiny. Are biomarker measurements reproducible? Was the clinical endpoint prespecified? Does the association remain under appropriate adjustment for confounders? Are missing samples associated with disease severity? Does the biomarker predict an outcome in a new sample, and is prediction performance stable? Even a correctly selected biomarker is not necessarily causally responsible for disease or useful for clinical decisions. Statistical discovery is one step in a larger evidence process.

A clear report would provide the full list of six tests, the target FDR, the assumed dependence structure, the four selected candidates, estimated effect sizes with units, and the planned validation stage. It would also distinguish the population association question from any later diagnostic or treatment claim. A technically correct BH computation is necessary for the stated screening guarantee, but it is not sufficient for clinical validity.

13. Reproducing calculations in a spreadsheet or Python

A reproducible analysis should not require trusting a single statistical library. Start with a table containing the original hypothesis identifiers and p-values. Confirm that every p-value is numeric, lies between zero and one, corresponds to a clearly defined test, and is not duplicated accidentally. Count m from the prespecified family, including nonsignificant results. Sort only a working copy by ascending p-value and retain a column with the original order. Ties may occur and should be processed consistently; none of the algorithms permits silently dropping ties or changing the family size.

For Bonferroni, multiply every original p-value by m and cap at one. For Holm, sort ascending, multiply ordered p-values by m, m−1 and so forth, then replace each result by the largest value seen up to that rank; cap at one. For BH, sort ascending, multiply each p-value by m divided by its rank, then work backward taking cumulative minima; cap at one. To verify a row, compare its adjusted value with the chosen alpha or q. To verify the whole set, separately apply the direct rank-threshold algorithm. Independent agreement between adjusted-value and threshold implementations is a meaningful diagnostic, not proof that the underlying tests were statistically valid.

For the eight-test example, check three landmarks manually. First, Bonferroni for H2 is eight times 0.008, giving 0.064 and hence no rejection at 0.05. Second, Holm for H2 is the maximum of 8×0.001=0.008 and 7×0.008=0.056, giving 0.056 and no rejection. Third, BH for H4 is the minimum over later ranks of 8×p_(j)/j; the value at j=4 is 8×0.019/4=0.038, and no later ratio is smaller, so its adjusted p-value is 0.038 and it passes. These spot checks exercise three distinct mathematical operations: multiplication, running maximum and backward running minimum.

Python software can independently reproduce the table through the `statsmodels.stats.multitest.multipletests` function with method names `bonferroni`, `holm`, and `fdr_bh`. This is a computational check, not a required prerequisite for the guide; the formulas above are sufficient to compute every result by hand. Software versions, input order, missing-value treatment and method options should be recorded if code is used for publication. Do not claim that merely executing a library function establishes valid study design or that every dependence structure satisfies the classical BH theorem.

The final quality check should compare decisions obtained from unadjusted thresholds with decisions obtained from adjusted p-values at the same target. For example, Holm H2 fails because its ordered p-value 0.008 is greater than alpha/7≈0.007143; its adjusted value 0.056 is also greater than alpha=0.05. BH H4 passes because 0.019≤4×0.05/8=0.025; its adjusted value 0.038 is also at most q=0.05. Agreement confirms the arithmetic for those rows while leaving scientific assumptions as a separate audit.

14. How to select a procedure and explain the tradeoff

Method selection begins with a decision question, not a desire to maximize significant results. Ask first what would make a false positive costly. If claiming that a new treatment works on at least one of several confirmatory endpoints would alter patient care, an investigator may choose strong FWER control through Holm or another justified confirmatory design. If identifying dozens of candidate mechanisms for future laboratory verification is the stated aim, an FDR procedure may align better with the cost of missing real candidates. In both settings the test assumptions, family and reporting policy must be settled before results are known.

Next ask whether the dependence assumptions can be defended. Holm is broadly robust to dependence when individual null p-values are valid. Ordinary BH calls for appropriate conditions, such as independence or the relevant positive-dependence structure. BY is an FDR alternative with a broader dependence guarantee but typically more conservative thresholds. None of these procedures rescues fabricated data, confounding, data leakage, sample selection problems or a misspecified null. They govern statistical errors conditional on the assumptions, rather than all possible errors in research practice.

Then check the communication goal. Does the report promise “the chance of any false positive among the family is at most five percent,” or does it promise “the expected fraction of false discoveries among selections is controlled at five percent”? The first is a FWER statement and the second an FDR statement. Both are repeated-sampling descriptions, not posterior probabilities that a particular reported result is true. If the audience cannot distinguish these, report the definition next to the number rather than displaying an unexplained acronym.

Finally, report outcomes that do not pass. Omitting nonrejections prevents readers from verifying the family size and can conceal selective reporting. A useful summary includes the full unadjusted and adjusted p-value table, estimated effects with units, suitable intervals, sample sizes, the sampling design, limitations of the relevant procedure, and any prespecified replication stage. The last paragraph should not simply celebrate the number of discoveries. It should say what the decision does and does not justify. An exploratory finding may motivate further testing without licensing a causal, clinical or policy recommendation.

Transfer exercises with full solutions

Solve each problem before expanding the explanation. All data are constructed for education.

1. Independent false alarms

Under the global null, six independent exact tests use alpha=0.10. Calculate the chance of at least one false rejection and the expected number of false rejections. Explain why they differ.

Show worked solution

The probability is 1−0.9^6=1−0.531441=0.468559. The expected count is 6×0.10=0.6. The first is the probability of a nonzero count; the second averages the count itself, including repetitions with two or more errors.

Verification: 0.9 squared is 0.81, cubed is 0.729 and to the sixth is 0.531441. The complement is therefore 0.468559. This computation requires independent tests and true nulls. The expected value 0.6 does not require independence, but it does assume each false rejection probability equals 0.10. If tests are merely conservative, the expected count is at most 0.6.

2. Bonferroni decisions

Four p-values are 0.003, 0.014, 0.028, 0.20. Apply Bonferroni at alpha=0.05, including adjusted p-values.

Show worked solution

The cutoff is 0.05/4=0.0125. Only 0.003 is at most the cutoff. Adjusted values are min(1,4p): 0.012, 0.056, 0.112, 0.800. This controls the probability of any false rejection, not the probability that each rejected null is true.

Check the alternative decision method: multiplying the original values by four gives the adjusted list. Comparing each adjusted value with 0.05 gives exactly the same single rejection as comparing raw values with 0.0125. The original family includes all four hypotheses even though only one passes. No interpretation of effect magnitude or causality follows from these p-values alone.

3. Holm versus Bonferroni

Use p-values 0.003, 0.014, 0.028, 0.20. Apply Holm and explain why the result differs from Bonferroni.

Show worked solution

Holm thresholds are 0.0125, 0.0166667, 0.025, 0.05. The first two p-values pass; the third 0.028 fails 0.025, so stop. Holm rejects two; Bonferroni rejects one. The step-down thresholds become less stringent after smaller p-values pass, while retaining strong FWER control.

Compute Holm adjusted values: raw sequential products are 0.012, 0.042, 0.056 and 0.20; running maxima are unchanged. The first two adjusted values are below 0.05, confirming the two rejections. Bonferroni adjusted values for the second hypothesis are 0.056, so it fails that method. This difference illustrates different computational rules under the same strong FWER objective.

4. BH step-up

Apply BH at q=0.05 to the four p-values in the preceding exercise. Identify the largest qualifying rank.

Show worked solution

The thresholds are 0.0125, 0.025, 0.0375, 0.05. The first three p-values pass and the fourth fails. The largest qualifying rank is three; reject the first three. The target is FDR rather than FWER, so this extra rejection is not automatically an error.

For the same four values, BH adjusted ratios are 0.012, 0.028, approximately 0.037333 and 0.20. The backward cumulative minima do not change them, so three adjusted values are at most 0.05. Do not report that all three effects are genuine; their population truth status is unknown. The guarantee refers to the expectation of a false-discovery fraction across repeated studies satisfying the assumptions.

5. A step-up trap

At q=0.05, consider sorted p-values 0.011, 0.018, 0.030, 0.90. What does BH reject? Explain why one must not stop at the first failed comparison.

Show worked solution

The rank thresholds are 0.0125, 0.025, 0.0375, 0.05. Ranks one, two, and three pass; rank four fails, so BH rejects three. To see the actual trap, change the first p-value to 0.013: it fails its own 0.0125 cutoff, but rank three still passes 0.0375, and the step-up procedure rejects all three. A step-down stopping rule would be wrong for BH.

For the modified sequence 0.013, 0.018, 0.030 and 0.90, the first comparison 0.013>0.0125 fails, while the second and third pass 0.025 and 0.0375. Thus the largest passing rank remains three and BH selects three. The computation demonstrates that an early failure cannot trigger the Holm stopping rule when the method is BH. The input must remain sorted.

6. FDP and FDR

In a hypothetical repetition, a procedure rejects five hypotheses, of which two are true nulls. Compute FDP. Can this one repetition establish FDR?

Show worked solution

V=2 and R=5, so FDP=2/5=0.40. FDR is the expectation of FDP over repeated datasets, not 0.40 merely because this realization had that fraction. In real research, which rejected nulls are true is usually unknown.

One observed fraction, 0.40, cannot estimate a repeated-sampling expectation reliably without additional information. If the next repetition rejects nothing, its FDP is defined as zero; if another rejects ten with one false, its FDP is 0.10. FDR averages such realizations according to their probabilities. A single study normally cannot label which particular rejections are false.

7. Dependence and procedure choice

A researcher tests 30 correlated endpoints and cannot justify independence or the positive dependence conditions needed for classical BH. Recommend an error procedure for a confirmatory claim and explain its limits.

Show worked solution

Holm at a prespecified FWER alpha is a defensible choice because it controls strong FWER under arbitrary dependence of valid p-values. It may be conservative, and it cannot repair biased sampling, invalid p-values, or outcome selection. BY could target FDR under arbitrary dependence if exploratory screening were the goal.

A fully justified confirmatory plan states the family of thirty endpoints, the significance level, the source of each valid p-value, and whether endpoints were prespecified. Holm provides strong control under arbitrary dependence; choosing FDR is a different scientific decision. If screening rather than confirmation is intended, BY could be considered for arbitrary dependence, at a potential cost in power.

8. Hidden analyses and transfer

A team tried 40 outcomes, reported only the smallest eight p-values, and applied BH treating m=8. Explain the problem and propose a better plan.

Show worked solution

The eight reported values were selected after examining 40 opportunities. Treating them as a prespecified family of eight understates the multiplicity of the selection process and does not inherit the claimed BH guarantee. Report all 40 prespecified tests, define the family and q before looking at results, verify test validity and dependence conditions, then apply an appropriate method to the full family. Distinguish exploratory findings from confirmatory replication.

An improved report explicitly says which outcomes were planned and which were investigated after the results appeared; lists the eight unadjusted and adjusted values; and gives effect estimates and uncertainties. A difference between one Holm rejection and four BH rejections is not a contradiction: the methods target different errors. An independent replication addresses whether discoveries persist, while multiplicity correction alone cannot establish causality.
