1. A prediction is not yet an inference

Suppose an energy analyst studies the relation between outdoor temperature and the electrical energy required by a small temperature-controlled installation during an operating period. A line can summarize six observations. But three different questions must be separated. What straight line best describes the recorded pairs? What can we infer about the population mean energy consumption at a given temperature? What range might plausibly contain the consumption of one future period? The last question requires allowing for the variability of a new observation, not only the uncertainty in the fitted mean. By the end of this guide you will be able to derive the line, calculate residual variability, test the slope, and construct and interpret both intervals without silently exchanging their meanings.

The unit of analysis is one operating period. The explanatory variable X is outdoor temperature in degrees Celsius (°C), and the response Y is consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh) over that period. All six pairs in our main table are SYNTHETIC, created only to teach reproducible arithmetic; they are not measurements from a power company, building or experiment. That distinction is substantive: a computed p-value from invented numbers demonstrates a statistical method but cannot establish a real-world energy effect. We use ordinary algebra, averages and derivatives from Calculus I, then explicitly introduce the small portion of two-parameter differentiation needed for least squares. Essential prerequisites are standard error, Student's t, confidence intervals, and the distinction between association and causality from Guides 27, 30, 34 and 50.

Every symbol will have a role. A lower-case xᵢ and yᵢ are recorded values for period i; n=6 is the number of independent units assumed for the inferential illustration. The subscript i labels a paired observation and never means that time dependence can be ignored in a genuine time series. The slope's unit is kWh per °C, the intercept's unit is kWh, and their uncertainty measures have corresponding units. Keep these dimensions visible to catch mistakes: a fitted consumption of 35 °C would be an invalid interpretation, even if the arithmetic produced 35.

3. Population model, conditional mean and noise

Population mean / Media poblacional
\[\begin{aligned}Y_i&=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\varepsilon_i\\[3pt]\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X=x)&=\beta_0+\beta_1x\end{aligned}\]

The random error is different from an observed fitted residual. The model describes an expected response for a given temperature.

The simple linear regression model is Yᵢ=β₀+β₁xᵢ+εᵢ. For a fixed observed xᵢ, β₀+β₁xᵢ is the population conditional mean E(Yᵢ|X=xᵢ), while εᵢ is the random error that makes an individual response differ from that conditional mean. The unknown population intercept β₀ has units kWh, and β₁ has units kWh/°C. Under the standard mean specification E(εᵢ|X)=0, the line describes the mean response. We use a lowercase x when conditioning on the observed design; X may be a random variable in broader sampling settings. A model is a claim requiring checks, not a line that becomes true because software can fit it.

The population error εᵢ is unobservable: even after observing Yᵢ we do not know the true β coefficients. Later we obtain fitted values ŷᵢ=b₀+b₁xᵢ and residuals eᵢ=yᵢ−ŷᵢ. A residual is a computable difference from an estimated line, not the original random error εᵢ. Confusing the two is especially harmful when discussing standard errors and statistical assumptions. The model supposes one conditional mean for each temperature, but it does not assert that every operating period at 15 °C consumes exactly that mean.

For the familiar classical small-sample t formulas, a sufficient working assumption is that errors are independent, normally distributed with common variance σ² and mean zero, conditional on the x values. Full-rank x variation is also necessary: at least two distinct temperatures and n greater than two for estimating residual variation. Independence can fail with repeated measurements taken on consecutive days; constant variance can fail if variability increases with load; normality can be a poor approximation when Y has strong bounds or outliers. Least squares arithmetic still produces a line without normality, but exact small-sample t and prediction-interval guarantees do not automatically survive these violations.

4. Why use the vertical least-squares criterion?

OLS criterion / Criterio OLS
\[\underset{a,b}{\operatorname{minimize}}\;Q(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-a-bx_i)^2\]

We choose the intercept and slope that make the sum of squared vertical residuals as small as possible.

For any proposed coefficients a and b, the vertical prediction at xᵢ is a+bxᵢ. Its residual is yᵢ−a−bxᵢ, measured in kWh. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) chooses coefficients that minimize Q(a,b)=Σᵢ[yᵢ−a−bxᵢ]². Squaring prevents positive and negative residuals from canceling, penalizes large departures, and creates a differentiable objective with a closed-form solution. Its unit is kWh². This choice is not required by logic alone; alternative losses can be preferable with outliers, asymmetric costs or another scientific target.

Why vertical rather than perpendicular distance? We are using x to predict Y; the conditional model treats fluctuations in Y around its mean for a given x. If measured temperature also has substantial random error, this conventional specification may not capture the measurement process and estimated slope can be biased. The minimum of Q does not mean the fitted line passes through all points. In fact, the residual variance is precisely the information needed to express uncertainty around the estimated mean and a new observation.

To see the geometry, imagine rotating the line: a steeper negative slope better follows the broad decline, but a slope that is too negative raises residuals at one end. Moving the intercept shifts all predictions simultaneously. The optimum balances both types of movement and satisfies two equations. We will derive those equations rather than quoting the slope formula without explanation.

5. Bridge from Calculus I: two partial derivatives

Normal equations / Ecuaciones normales
\[\begin{aligned}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i&=0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_ie_i=0\\[4pt]b_1&=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}},\qquad b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\end{aligned}\]

The two first-order conditions yield the fitted slope and intercept; each symbol is defined in the surrounding derivation.

Calculus I teaches that at an interior minimum of a differentiable one-variable function the derivative is zero. Here Q depends on two variables a and b. A PARTIAL derivative means differentiating with respect to one coefficient while holding the other fixed. No general course in multivariable calculus is presumed: for each squared residual, apply the chain rule and treat the unchanged coefficient as a constant. For a term (yᵢ−a−bxᵢ)², its derivative with respect to a is −2(yᵢ−a−bxᵢ); with respect to b it is −2xᵢ(yᵢ−a−bxᵢ).

Therefore ∂Q/∂a=−2Σ(yᵢ−a−bxᵢ)=0 and ∂Q/∂b=−2Σxᵢ(yᵢ−a−bxᵢ)=0 at the OLS optimum. Divide by −2, denote the fitted coefficients by b₀ and b₁, and obtain the NORMAL EQUATIONS: Σ(yᵢ−b₀−b₁xᵢ)=0 and Σxᵢ(yᵢ−b₀−b₁xᵢ)=0. These equalities express that residuals are orthogonal, in the sum-of-products sense, to the constant column and to the measured x column. They will later give us two independent checks on arithmetic.

The first equation gives Σyᵢ=nb₀+b₁Σxᵢ, so b₀=ȳ−b₁x̄. This is why the fitted line passes through (x̄,ȳ). Substitute into the second equation, collect centered terms and get b₁=Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)/Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²=Sxy/Sxx. The denominator Sxx must be positive; otherwise every x value is identical and the slope cannot be identified. Q is a convex quadratic in the two coefficients. When Sxx>0, its Hessian is positive definite, ensuring the stationary point is the unique global minimum—not merely an arbitrary flat point or maximum.

6. Compute the means, centered products and slope

Fitted line / Recta ajustada
\[\hat y=55.5714286-1.3714286x\]

Here x is measured in °C and the fitted energy response is in kWh.

For the six temperatures, Σx=10+12+14+16+18+20=90, hence x̄=90/6=15 °C. For energy, Σy=42+39+36+34+31+28=210 and ȳ=35 kWh. The six x deviations are −5,−3,−1,1,3,5. The y deviations are 7,4,1,−1,−4,−7. Their pairwise products are −35,−12,−1,−1,−12,−35, adding to Sxy=−96 °C·kWh. The squared x deviations are 25,9,1,1,9,25, adding to Sxx=70 °C².

Substitute: b₁=Sxy/Sxx=−96/70=−48/35≈−1.3714286 kWh/°C. The negative sign means the fitted mean energy drops by about 1.37 kWh for each additional degree Celsius within the observed temperature domain and conditional on this model. The intercept is b₀=ȳ−b₁x̄=35−(−48/35)(15)=389/7≈55.5714286 kWh. Our OLS line is ŷ=55.5714286−1.3714286x, with x in °C and ŷ in kWh.

Check the fitted center: at x=15, ŷ=55.5714286−20.5714286=35 kWh, exactly ȳ. At x=10, ŷ≈41.857143; at x=20, ŷ≈28.142857. The intercept at 0 °C lies OUTSIDE the measured range 10–20 °C. Mathematically it is required to place the line correctly, but its interpretation as a realistic mean at 0 °C requires additional assumptions about extrapolation. The observed relationship gives no direct evidence for that distant temperature.

8. Decompose total variation into explained and unexplained

Sum of squares / Sumas de cuadrados
\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{SST}&=\mathrm{SSR}+\mathrm{SSE}\\R^2&=1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}}\end{aligned}\]

The explained and unexplained sums of squares partition the total variation under an OLS fit with an intercept.

Let SST=Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)², the total sum of squares about the observed mean. With deviations 7,4,1,−1,−4,−7, SST=49+16+1+1+16+49=132 kWh². The explained sum SSR=Σ(ŷᵢ−ȳ)² and the residual sum SSE=Σ(yᵢ−ŷᵢ)². The exact OLS identity, when an intercept is included, is SST=SSR+SSE. This follows because yᵢ−ȳ=(ŷᵢ−ȳ)+eᵢ, and the mixed cross-product sum vanishes by the normal equations. It is not simply a rule to memorize.

We can compute SSR directly as b₁²Sxx=(−48/35)²×70=4608/35≈131.657143 kWh². Consequently SSE=132−4608/35=12/35≈0.342857 kWh². Directly squaring the six residuals gives the same result: (1/7)²+(−4/35)²+(−13/35)²+(13/35)²+(4/35)²+(−1/7)²=12/35. Comparing the two methods catches algebra or transcription errors.

The coefficient of determination is R²=SSR/SST=1−SSE/SST=1−(12/35)/132≈0.9974026. It says that the fitted linear function accounts for about 99.74% of the sample's variation in Y around its sample mean, under this particular descriptive decomposition. It does not claim that temperature causally determines 99.74% of electricity usage, nor that future prediction will have the same performance, nor that the model's assumptions hold because R² is high.

9. Residual degrees of freedom and the error scale

Noise estimate / Escala residual
\[\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n-2},\qquad s=\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}\]

Two parameters are estimated, leaving n − 2 residual degrees of freedom.

Population error variance σ² has unit kWh² and measures variation around the true conditional mean. We estimate it using MSE=SSE/(n−2), not SSE/n, because the two fitted coefficients consume two degrees of freedom. For six observations, n−2=4, so MSE=(12/35)/4=3/35≈0.0857143 kWh². Its square root is the residual standard error s=√(3/35)≈0.292770 kWh. The square root returns us to the original energy units; it offers a scale for deviations around the fitted line.

Why four degrees of freedom rather than six? Our six residuals obey two independent constraints: their sum is zero and their x-weighted sum is zero. Only four residual components can vary freely once the least-squares line is estimated. Under the usual independent common-variance model with a correctly specified conditional mean, SSE/σ² follows a chi-square distribution with n−2 degrees of freedom if errors are normal. We use this established distributional result when deriving exact t procedures; the general proof requires tools beyond the declared prerequisites and is cited rather than silently treated as elementary algebra.

Do not confuse s with the standard error of the slope, the standard error of a fitted mean, or the standard error of a future observation. All are measured in kWh or kWh/°C as appropriate, but they answer different questions. The residual standard error s estimates a common underlying noise standard deviation at a given temperature, whereas the standard error of b₁ quantifies uncertainty in a coefficient estimated from six observations.

10. The assumptions required for trustworthy inference

The structural mean assumption is linearity: E(Y|X=x)=β₀+β₁x over the target temperature range. A residual plot against temperature can reveal systematic curvature when this approximation is poor. The equal-variance assumption states Var(ε|X=x)=σ², independent of temperature. If residual spread grows with x, standard errors based on constant variance can misrepresent uncertainty. Independence means distinct operating periods carry no correlated errors after conditioning as specified; a smooth sequence of adjacent days may violate it. Conditional normality supports exact finite-sample t statements, especially important with n=6; without normality, large-sample procedures may still sometimes work but need a separate justification.

Treat these as assumptions about the data-generating process, not as properties verified by obtaining a small p-value or a large R². A graph with six points offers limited power to detect heteroscedasticity or nonnormality. In practice, inspect residual-versus-fitted and residual-versus-x plots, time order, normal quantile plots, leverage and influence, and measurement protocol. Guide 54 develops diagnostics in greater depth; this guide focuses on what the assumptions mean for the present formulas.

A further requirement is that the data have a meaningful sampling story. If the six points were deliberately selected to line up, confidence levels do not magically generalize to a population of all operating periods. Our example is synthetic, so inferential probabilities are conditional demonstrations of how a procedure behaves under an assumed sampling model, not empirical statements about a real facility. Missing values, retrospective predictor selection and unrecorded operational changes are additional limitations that must be examined when transferring the method.

11. Derive the standard error of the slope

Slope uncertainty / Incertidumbre de pendiente
\[\operatorname{SE}(b_1)=\frac{s}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\]

More variation in observed temperatures (larger Sxx) helps estimate the slope more precisely, other factors fixed.

Write b₁=β₁+Σ(xᵢ−x̄)εᵢ/Sxx when the mean model is correct and x values are fixed. This identity follows by replacing yᵢ in Sxy with β₀+β₁xᵢ+εᵢ and using Σ(xᵢ−x̄)=0. Conditional on x, the numerator's variance is σ²Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²=σ²Sxx when errors are independent and have common variance. Therefore Var(b₁|x)=σ²/Sxx. Greater spread in predictor values increases Sxx and can reduce slope uncertainty, all else equal; increasing n without extending or diversifying x may have a different effect.

Because σ is unknown, replace it by s to obtain SE(b₁)=s/√Sxx=0.292770/√70≈0.0349927 kWh/°C. This calculation shows both the numerator (unexplained noise) and denominator (temperature information). If all x were 15 °C, Sxx would be zero and the coefficient would not be identifiable. If s became larger while Sxx stayed fixed, confidence limits would widen.

A standard error is the estimated sampling standard deviation of an estimator across hypothetical repetitions, not the amount by which the estimate is guaranteed to differ from the unknown truth. The true slope β₁ is fixed under the classical model; b₁ varies from sample to sample. That conceptual separation is indispensable before using a t distribution.

12. Test whether the population slope equals zero

Slope t statistic / Estadístico t de pendiente
\[t=\frac{b_1-\beta_{1,0}}{\operatorname{SE}(b_1)},\qquad \mathrm{df}=n-2\]

The statistic compares the estimated slope with the proposed population slope, in standard-error units.

The two-sided null hypothesis is H₀:β₁=0 against H₁:β₁≠0. Under H₀ and the classical normal-error assumptions, t=(b₁−0)/SE(b₁) follows Student's t distribution with n−2=4 degrees of freedom. For our line, t=−1.3714286/0.0349927≈−39.1918. The two-sided p-value is about 0.000002532, obtained from both tails of t₄ beyond the observed |t|. We state the assumptions because such an extreme statistic would be misleading if the data were mechanically manufactured to follow a line—which they were for teaching.

The p-value is the probability, under the specified null and statistical model, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, the probability that the estimated slope is a causal effect, or the probability that the next observation lies near the line. A rejection at α=0.05 is a decision about statistical compatibility with zero slope in a model. Practical importance requires evaluating units, effect size, cost and plausible nonlinearity; a small p-value alone is not an operational recommendation.

The confidence interval gives an additional view by stating a range of coefficient values not rejected by the corresponding two-sided t test at the chosen level. Both interpretations depend on the same model; neither removes hidden confounding or biased measurement. For example, warmer periods might also have different occupancy, which could affect energy usage even at identical outdoor temperatures.

13. Build and interpret the slope confidence interval

Slope interval / Intervalo de pendiente
\[\boxed{\displaystyle b_1\;\pm\;t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\operatorname{SE}(b_1)}\]

Estimate ± t critical value × standard error. Both interval endpoints have units of kWh per °C.

\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\beta_1)&=-1.371429\pm 2.776445(0.034993)\\&\approx[-1.468584,\;-1.274273]\ \mathrm{kWh}/{}^\circ\mathrm C\end{aligned}\]

For confidence level 1−α=0.95 and 4 degrees of freedom, the two-sided t critical value is t₀.₉₇₅,₄≈2.776445. A 95% interval for β₁ is b₁±tcrit SE(b₁). Substitute −1.3714286±2.776445(0.0349927)=−1.3714286±0.0971553, giving [−1.468584,−1.274273] kWh/°C. Each endpoint is an estimate of a plausible population change in mean kWh for a one-degree increase within the modeled temperature domain.

Because zero lies outside the interval, the interval agrees with the two-sided rejection at α=0.05. This compatibility is not an accident: t testing and t intervals use the same pivot and critical value. If one endpoint had crossed zero, we would not reject at that level. The whole interval lies below zero, supporting a negative conditional linear association under the working assumptions. The narrowness arises from the strongly aligned synthetic values and should not be mistaken for realistic field precision.

A correct frequentist interpretation is that the PROCEDURE would cover the fixed true β₁ in 95% of repeated samples satisfying its assumptions. Once we compute a particular interval, its endpoints are fixed; it is not formally correct to say there is a 95% chance that this fixed β₁ happens to lie inside these fixed endpoints, unless a different probabilistic model for the parameter is adopted.

14. What exactly is the estimated mean at x₀?

Mean standard error / Error estándar de media
\[\operatorname{SE}_{\mathrm{mean}}(x_0)=s\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}\]

This quantifies uncertainty in the estimated conditional mean at x₀, not the spread of future individual outcomes.

Let μ(x₀)=E(Y|X=x₀)=β₀+β₁x₀ denote a population mean response, and let ŷ₀=b₀+b₁x₀ estimate it. At x₀=15 °C, ŷ₀=35 kWh. This is the expected consumption among hypothetical comparable operating periods at 15 °C according to the linear model. It is not a prediction for a named period with all its own unmeasured influences held fixed.

An important identity is ŷ₀=ȳ+b₁(x₀−x̄). At the sample's center x₀=x̄=15, the slope contribution is zero, so the fitted mean equals ȳ. Away from x̄, slope estimation uncertainty matters increasingly. Under independent homoscedastic errors and a correct linear model, Var(ŷ₀|x)=σ²[1/n+(x₀−x̄)²/Sxx]. The first term reflects uncertainty in the average vertical level; the second reflects uncertain slope multiplied by the distance from the center. The form also shows why inference is most precise at the center of the observed x design.

Estimate σ using s and calculate SEmean(x₀)=s√[1/n+(x₀−x̄)²/Sxx]. At 15 °C this is 0.292770√(1/6)≈0.119523 kWh. Notice that 0.119523 is much smaller than the residual standard error 0.292770: averaging across independent observations improves precision for the population mean, even though an individual period still varies around that mean.

15. Confidence interval for one conditional mean

Pointwise mean interval / Intervalo puntual de media
\[\boxed{\displaystyle \hat y_0\;\pm\;t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,s\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}}\]

For a fixed x₀, this is a pointwise confidence interval for the unknown population mean response.

\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\mu(15))&=35\pm2.776445(0.119523)\\&\approx[34.668,\;35.332]\ \mathrm{kWh}\end{aligned}\]

At x₀=15, the point estimate is 35 kWh and SEmean=0.119523 kWh. The pointwise 95% confidence interval is 35±t₀.₉₇₅,₄(0.119523)=35±0.331849, or approximately [34.668151,35.331849] kWh. Every part of the calculation has meaning: 35 is the fitted mean, 2.776445 is a t multiplier reflecting only four residual degrees of freedom, and 0.119523 quantifies sampling uncertainty in the fitted mean at the sample center.

The word POINTWISE matters. This 95% interval targets the population mean at the one temperature selected for the calculation. If we draw such intervals at dozens of temperatures and demand that the resulting entire curve cover the true regression line simultaneously, these separate pointwise intervals do not automatically give 95% simultaneous coverage. A simultaneous confidence band requires a different multiplier, such as the Working–Hotelling procedure under its corresponding conditions; we will state the distinction but not obscure the principal contrast with an individual prediction.

A narrow mean-confidence interval does not imply small uncertainty for a new period. If a stakeholder asks, “How much should we budget for the next single 15 °C shift?” a mean-confidence interval is the wrong uncertainty target. The population mean could be estimated precisely while individual periods vary substantially; the next section accounts for both sources.

16. A future observation contains new randomness

Individual prediction interval / Intervalo predictivo individual
\[\boxed{\displaystyle \hat y_0\;\pm\;t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,s\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}}}\]

The extra 1 represents the new, unpredictable error of a future individual response, making this interval wider.

\[\begin{aligned}\mathrm{PI}_{95\%}(Y_{\mathrm{new}}\mid x=15)&=35\pm2.776445(0.316228)\\&\approx[34.122,\;35.878]\ \mathrm{kWh}\end{aligned}\]

For one future operating period at x₀, write Ynew=β₀+β₁x₀+εnew, where εnew is a fresh independent error with variance σ². The point prediction is still ŷ₀. But the prediction error Ynew−ŷ₀ contains the future error εnew PLUS the uncertainty in estimating the regression line. Conditional on the observed x design, these pieces are independent under the model. Therefore Var(Ynew−ŷ₀)=σ²{1+1/n+(x₀−x̄)²/Sxx}. The extra 1 comes from the random deviation of a single future operating period around its population mean.

The standard error for predicting an individual response is SEpred(x₀)=s√{1+1/n+(x₀−x̄)²/Sxx}. At 15 °C this becomes 0.292770√(1+1/6)=0.316228 kWh, compared with SEmean=0.119523 kWh. The fitted center is the same 35 kWh for both targets, and the same t critical value is used in this classical pointwise example, but the prediction standard error is larger because the underlying question is different.

A 95% PREDICTION INTERVAL at 15 °C is 35±2.776445(0.316228), yielding [34.122011,35.877989] kWh. Such intervals would capture individual new responses in 95% of repeated model-and-data repetitions under the stated assumptions. They are not guarantees for every period, and they may fail badly after a regime change, serial correlation, nonconstant error variance or extrapolation. The distinction is conceptual rather than cosmetic: including or omitting the leading 1 changes the question being answered.

17. See the difference at a noncentral temperature

At x₀=18 °C, the fitted response is 55.571429−1.371429×18=30.885714 kWh. The distance from the temperature mean is x₀−x̄=3 °C and the leverage term is 3²/70=9/70≈0.128571. Hence SEmean(18)=0.292770√(1/6+9/70)≈0.159079 kWh. Multiply by the t critical value to obtain a pointwise mean interval [30.444040,31.327388] kWh.

For a future individual period at 18 °C, include the extra 1: SEpred(18)=0.292770√(1+1/6+9/70)≈0.333197 kWh and the 95% prediction interval is [29.960610,31.810818] kWh. Both bands are centered on the same fitted line but spread out as |x₀−x̄| grows. The prediction band remains wider at every x₀ because 1+q is always larger than q for nonnegative q.

For a visual check, compare the half-widths: near 15 °C, mean half-width≈0.331849 and prediction half-width≈0.877989; at 18 °C they become about 0.441674 and 0.925104. Their differences reflect the geometry of the design, not a change in the estimate of σ across these temperatures. On the accompanying graph, the inner ribbon depicts the pointwise confidence intervals for the conditional mean and the outer ribbon depicts the prediction intervals for a new period. They are calculated from the same formulas, not hand-drawn for decoration.

18. Why both bands widen away from x̄

The term h₀=1/n+(x₀−x̄)²/Sxx is sometimes called the leverage for predicting a mean at x₀ in a simple regression with an intercept. Its first part cannot disappear in a finite sample, and its second part grows quadratically with the distance from the observed temperature center. This is why a fitted straight line can look visually certain in the middle and increasingly uncertain at the edges. Temperature values far from x̄ are supported less directly by the sample even though the algebraic line has a value at every real x.

At 24 °C, which is outside our observed 10–20 °C range, the equation gives ŷ≈22.657143 kWh. It is possible to mechanically calculate an interval using h₀=1/6+9²/70≈1.32381, leading to a pointwise mean interval [21.721891,23.592394] and a prediction interval [21.418015,23.896270] kWh. But these formula-based bands only quantify uncertainty IF the same linear model remains valid at 24 °C. They do not account for a possible change in heating/cooling regime. A scientifically responsible answer labels this an extrapolation, not a validated forecast.

Another distinction is simultaneous versus pointwise uncertainty. A smooth plot containing many separate 95% pointwise intervals is not automatically a 95% band for the entire population line. Conversely, the outer prediction ribbon at each x is not a promise that all future periods across many temperatures will fall inside it. Explicitly specify the coverage target and how many statements are being made when designing an uncertainty graphic or reporting an interval.

19. Student-friendly derivation of the mean-variance formula

The expression ŷ₀=ȳ+b₁(x₀−x̄) makes the derivation accessible. Under the fixed-x independent equal-variance model, Var(ȳ)=σ²/n and Var(b₁)=σ²/Sxx. In addition, Cov(ȳ,b₁)=0 because Σ(xᵢ−x̄)=0. Apply the elementary variance identity Var(A+cB)=Var(A)+c²Var(B)+2cCov(A,B) with c=x₀−x̄. The covariance term vanishes, leaving σ²/n+(x₀−x̄)²σ²/Sxx, which is exactly σ²h₀.

This derivation explains where the formula comes from rather than asking you to memorize a mysterious square root. A mean is estimated from n units, producing a variance term of order 1/n. A slope is estimated using the centered predictor spread, producing the Sxx denominator. The squared distance from x̄ translates uncertainty in the slope into uncertainty about a particular fitted mean. If x₀=x̄, the second contribution becomes zero.

For an individual future response, subtract ŷ₀ from β₀+β₁x₀+εnew. The new independent ε contributes another σ² to the variance; hence σ²(h₀+1). Both expressions require the independence and constant variance assumptions stated earlier. A student should be able to explain the additional 1 in ordinary language before attempting any interval arithmetic: one future measurement contains new variation not eliminated by fitting the average line.

20. Diagnostics are a question, not a decorative plot

Plot residuals against fitted values and temperature. Under a sensible linear mean model, residuals should show no obvious systematic curved pattern. The six fitted residuals alternate in sign and have magnitude below 0.4 kWh. That visual behavior is compatible with a good fit but is not a statistical certification of assumptions. A residual-versus-time plot matters in a series of operating periods: adjacent positive residuals may indicate autocorrelation even when a residual-versus-temperature plot looks harmless. A normal Q–Q plot can suggest outliers or skewness, but with six observations conclusions are necessarily tentative.

Leverage describes how unusually far a predictor value lies from x̄; at x=20 the in-sample leverage is hᵢ=1/6+(20−15)²/70≈0.52381. Influence describes how much an estimated coefficient changes if an observation is removed. A high-leverage point is not necessarily harmful; its impact depends also on its residual. Avoid deleting unusual but valid periods solely because they weaken a preferred relationship. If a cooling unit activates above a threshold, a curved or piecewise model may be more scientifically appropriate than forcing a straight line over the entire operating domain.

Guide 54 will teach a fuller set of residual diagnostics. Here the inferential discipline is to state what a plot could reveal, inspect it with appropriate caution, and not treat a visually beautiful fitted line as evidence of independence, normality, causality or external validity. If diagnostics find a problem, first investigate measurement and model design; do not cosmetically report the same confidence intervals as if nothing changed.

21. Compare regression, correlation and causal effects

For one predictor with an intercept, the sample Pearson correlation r and OLS slope share the same sign; the identity is b₁=r(sᵧ/sₓ), where the sample standard deviations use the same denominator. Here r≈−0.998700, so r²≈0.997403 equals the reported R². But the quantities measure different things. r is unitless, b₁ is a change in kWh per °C, and R² is a within-sample squared-deviation proportion. Changing the unit of temperature changes the numerical slope but not r or R².

An observational slope is not by default a causal effect. Temperature may correlate with sunlight hours, occupancy patterns or equipment operation, all of which can influence energy consumption. If the intended question is “what would happen if temperature were actively changed while other influences remained fixed?”, a causal design or defensible adjustment model is needed. This guide estimates a single conditional linear association. The next guide will introduce multiple predictors and conditional interpretation; it will not automatically solve every causal problem either.

The fitted line's attractive R² can be particularly deceptive with selected synthetic data. Real work requires documented data origins, measurement units, missingness, holdout performance where prediction is the goal, and appropriate operational checks. One model can be excellent for describing the six training points yet inadequate for predicting the next season.

22. Reporting uncertainty without overclaiming

A transparent summary names the estimand, the units, the design and the assumptions before the headline statistic. For example: “In six constructed temperature–energy pairs, the fitted OLS slope was −1.371 kWh/°C, residual standard error 0.293 kWh and in-sample R² 0.9974. Under independent normal errors with constant variance, the two-sided t test of zero slope gave t(4)≈−39.192, p≈2.53×10⁻⁶; the 95% slope confidence interval was [−1.469,−1.274] kWh/°C.” The wording does not pretend these numbers came from a real facility.

Continue with the target-specific statements: “At 15 °C the fitted mean was 35 kWh; the pointwise 95% confidence interval for the mean was [34.668,35.332] kWh, whereas a 95% prediction interval for one future comparable period was [34.122,35.878] kWh.” The narrower interval concerns an unknown average; the wider concerns an additional random outcome. Neither is a simultaneous guarantee across all temperatures, and neither validates extrapolation or a causal claim.

The source of each number is reproducible from the six pairs, not a hidden proprietary output. Explain rounding and note that an estimated 95% interval is a procedure with a repeated-sampling property conditional on the model. This practice makes a result useful even to readers who disagree about the appropriateness of the assumptions.

37. Design variation: what happens if we add a duplicate x?

A useful design thought experiment is to add one more operating period at 15 °C. Adding an observation at the current predictor center provides additional information about the average consumption around that center, but little direct leverage for distinguishing one slope from another. The algebra explains this distinction: an extra x value equal to the existing x̄ contributes zero to the centered Sxx, provided the mean remains at 15. A record at 25 °C, by contrast, contributes substantial predictor spread, but it may also enter a new heating/cooling regime and invalidate the assumed linear conditional mean. More “information” in the denominator is therefore valuable only if the scientific specification remains credible.

Consider a second thought experiment: temperature readings are rounded to the nearest 5 °C, but consumption is measured accurately. The resulting x values no longer reflect the original continuous information. If predictor measurement error is random rather than mere rounding, ordinary OLS typically does not estimate the intended structural slope without further assumptions. No amount of precision in y or a small p-value repairs the omitted measurement model. The inferential lesson is to consider the physical meaning and quality of x before treating Sxx as pure information.

If we add repeated periods from the same physical installation, independence is not automatic. Two records might share the same equipment fault, occupancy schedule or weather regime. Increasing the count n in the formulas would then overstate the amount of independent information. A responsible analyst chooses the observational unit, studies clustering and time order, and justifies whether the formulas describe genuinely independent disturbances. This is why data design belongs before hypothesis testing rather than being relegated to a footnote after seeing attractive estimates.

38. A nonzero intercept is not necessarily an attainable baseline

The estimated intercept 55.571429 kWh is the line's value at x=0 °C. Its meaning requires separating algebraic parameters from scientifically accessible settings. With measurements only between 10 and 20 °C, the data do not directly support the claim that the installation would consume precisely 55.57 kWh at 0 °C. The line may be useful inside the measured range while behaving poorly outside it. This issue is especially visible when the physical process is nonlinear, bounded or changes regimes.

A technique called centering can change the intercept's interpretation without changing the predictions. Let z=x−15. Then x=z+15 and ŷ=55.571429−1.371429(z+15)=35−1.371429z. The new intercept equals 35 kWh and describes the fitted mean when z=0, that is, temperature x=15 °C. The slope stays −1.371429 kWh per degree because a one-unit increase in z is the same change as a one-degree increase in x. The fitted values, residuals, SSE, R² and slope p-value remain unchanged.

This transformation makes the intercept more interpretable and clarifies why the mean response is most precise around the center. However, centering does not create a randomized experiment, ensure homoscedastic errors or justify extrapolation. It changes parameterization, not evidence. If the line is misspecified, reporting a centered intercept may make its meaning clearer but cannot remove bias due to omitted variables or model failure. Use transformations to simplify interpretation, not to conceal limitations.

39. The estimated intercept also has uncertainty

Because b₀=ȳ−b₁x̄, intercept estimation involves both the average response and the estimated slope. Under the fixed-design classical model, Var(b₀|x)=σ²[1/n+x̄²/Sxx], with estimated SE(b₀)=s√[1/n+x̄²/Sxx]. For our data, substitute s=0.292770, x̄=15 and Sxx=70: SE(b₀)=0.292770√(1/6+225/70)≈0.538327 kWh. A 95% interval for the raw intercept is b₀±2.776445(0.538327), approximately [54.0768,57.0661] kWh. The numbers are valid as a formal parameter interval under the model, even though x=0 is unobserved and external interpretation remains hazardous.

If we instead center temperature z=x−15, the new intercept equals ȳ=35 and its standard error simplifies to s/√n≈0.119523 kWh. This is exactly the fitted-mean uncertainty at the old center. The seeming change in precision is not contradictory: the raw intercept refers to the mean at an unsupported x=0, while the centered intercept refers to the well-supported mean at x=15. They are different parameters derived from an equivalent fitted line, so their standard errors need not agree.

The slope and intercept estimates are usually correlated when x̄ is not zero, which is why deriving a mean-response Standard Error by independently squaring two separate coefficient SEs would be wrong. The covariance term must be included. Centering removes that covariance under the fixed-x classical model and makes the variance decomposition easier to see. This is an instructive reason to derive the fitted-mean formula rather than combining uncertainty estimates by intuition alone.

40. Interpret a change over several degrees, not just one

The slope estimates a mean change for one additional Celsius degree. For a five-degree increase, the line implies an estimated conditional mean difference of 5b₁=5(−1.371429)=−6.857143 kWh. This is a comparison of two fitted conditional means within the modeled domain, not a predicted individual-period energy saving after a controlled intervention. The intercept cancels: [b₀+b₁(x+5)]−[b₀+b₁x]=5b₁. This cancellation shows why slope is the relevant parameter for comparisons over a specified temperature difference.

If the model assumptions hold, the Standard Error for this estimated five-degree mean contrast is 5SE(b₁)≈0.174964 kWh, because multiplying a random estimator by 5 multiplies its standard deviation by the absolute factor 5. A 95% interval for the five-degree mean contrast is 5 times the slope interval: [−7.342920,−6.371366] kWh. Each endpoint retains the meaning of a difference of mean responses across a five-degree increment, not the value of a single future consumption measurement.

Contrast this with constructing a Prediction Interval for a future period at a particular temperature: that problem contains a new individual error and requires the leading 1 in its variance expression. A contrast of two unknown population means, a confidence interval for one mean and a prediction interval for one new response are three distinct targets. Even if they all involve the same fitted slope, you must choose the formula that matches the question rather than using whichever interval seems narrowest.

41. An internal consistency checklist for every regression report

Before releasing a result, check whether x and y have identifiable observational units, whether each pair really comes from the same unit, whether a graphical inspection supports at least an approximate line, and whether an intercept belongs in the scientific model. Reproduce x̄,ȳ,Sxx,Sxy from the raw records and confirm the units of their sums. Verify b₁=Sxy/Sxx and b₀=ȳ−b₁x̄. Inspect the residual list rather than trusting SSE alone. Check Σe≈0, Σxe≈0, SST≈SSR+SSE and R²=1−SSE/SST. These checks are arithmetic validations, not probabilistic validations.

Next verify the uncertainty pathway. Confirm n−2 residual degrees of freedom, calculate s, SE(b₁), the test statistic and its t distribution under declared assumptions. For x₀, calculate h₀ carefully, ask whether x₀ lies inside the observed range, and choose between s√h₀ for the conditional mean and s√(1+h₀) for an individual future response. Apply the appropriate t multiplier. If a report labels a band “95%,” specify whether it is pointwise or simultaneous; if it claims causality, identify the design and assumptions beyond regression.

Finally, explain the result in plain language with units and limitations. Could an introductory student reproduce the calculations without seeing a proprietary software output? Could they explain why least squares has TWO normal equations, why residual df is n−2, and why prediction intervals are wider? If not, the solution is incomplete even if software produces perfect numbers. Mathematical reproducibility, inferential calibration and scientific usefulness are related, but each must be evaluated separately.

42. A final pathway: from raw observations to defensible claims

The analysis has a dependency structure. Start by defining the observation unit and a meaningful explanatory variable and response, inspecting a scatterplot and preserving the original pairs. State a conditional mean model and its error assumptions. Next minimize squared residuals; derive the two Normal Equations with partial derivatives and solve them, confirming the fitted line passes through the sample centroid. Construct predictions, signed residuals, SSE, SST, SSR and R². Estimate the noise scale using n−2 degrees of freedom. Only after completing these descriptive steps should you use the t reference distribution for a slope hypothesis or confidence interval.

At a target temperature x₀, distinguish the population mean μ(x₀), its fitted estimate ŷ₀, and a future random response Ynew. The first is an unknown fixed conditional expectation under the model; the second is an estimator based on the sample; the third includes its own fresh random deviation. The mean interval uses 1/n+(x₀−x̄)²/Sxx. The individual prediction interval uses 1+1/n+(x₀−x̄)²/Sxx. Both may widen toward the sample edges, but extrapolation remains a separate scientific uncertainty not captured by substituting x₀ into a formula.

This is the transferable reasoning skill: describe, estimate, quantify residual noise, declare assumptions, calibrate inference, identify the uncertainty target, and state limitations. Regression is more than drawing a line and displaying a p-value. It is a model of conditional averages and uncertainty; without a plausible sampling story and careful interpretation, mathematically correct output can still support the wrong decision.
