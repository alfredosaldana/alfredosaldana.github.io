01. Forecasting is a decision made at a particular time

A forecast is not a description of what already happened. It is a claim made at a forecast origin, using information genuinely available at that origin, about a later outcome. If a retailer orders inventory on Sunday night, the next three weeks of demand do not yet exist as observations. A prediction must be built from history, not from a smoothing calculation performed after the season ends. This distinction separates legitimate forecasting from retrospective curve fitting.

In this guide we evaluate four reference procedures—Mean, Naive, Seasonal Naive, and Drift—before considering more complicated models. A baseline is not necessarily a poor model. It is the minimum credible competitor that a proposed sophisticated method should beat. We will also compare point forecasts using MAE, RMSE, and MASE; carefully explain when MAPE fails; and build simple horizon-specific intervals whose observed coverage we can check. The purpose is not to find a universal winner from a tiny dataset, but to learn what a defensible comparison must control.

Our data are 24 synthetic consecutive weeks of unit demand. The imposed four-week operating cycle is not claimed to be an annual retail season. This artificial example is small enough to disclose completely; conclusions describe these numbers, not real stores. Prerequisites include means and variability, prediction intervals in regression, trend and seasonality, and serial dependence. Each needed idea is reintroduced.

02. Learning contract: what you must be able to reproduce

At the end you should be able to identify the target variable, its unit and time frequency; choose a defensible seasonal period; calculate each of four baseline forecasts without looking forward; construct a rolling-origin evaluation table; compute error metrics from the same scored pairs; calculate a common MASE denominator from training observations only; and evaluate interval coverage separately at horizons one, two and three. You should also be able to identify at least three distinct forms of leakage and propose a correction.

A forecast horizon \(h\) is the number of future sampling intervals between origin \(o\) and outcome \(y_{o+h}\). An origin \(o\) is the most recent observation permitted when training. A window is the slice of data available for model estimation, while a test observation is an outcome reserved for later scoring. A forecast error will be defined consistently as actual minus prediction, \(e_{o,h}=y_{o+h}-\widehat y_{o+h\mid o}\). Positive error therefore means underprediction. Every following table uses these definitions; mixing sign conventions can reverse a discussion of systematic bias.

The running example is intentionally simple, but its evaluation discipline is the same one required for richer time-series models. The final exercises require explaining recommendations in business units, not just repeating formula names.

03. Complete synthetic weekly demand series

The dataset contains exactly 24 consecutive weeks and one target: demand \(y_t\) in units per week. There are no missing weeks, duplicate timestamps, refunds, or multiple stores in the illustrative definition. Weeks are numbered \(t=1,\ldots,24\), so a lag of four represents four weeks, not four months. We choose a synthetic four-week operating cycle to make a seasonal baseline computable by hand; in real commerce, a retailer must establish meaningful periods from calendars and operational evidence instead.

The data were constructed for teaching by combining a gradual upward level, a four-week pattern, and predetermined small deviations. They are not observations from a commercial source. Each row of the following table displays one weekly demand. For example, the demand in week 12 is 71 units and the demand in week 13 is 69. We will repeatedly freeze the information set at origin 12: week 13 may then be used for scoring but not for computing the week-13 forecast.

This full disclosure is important: it permits verification of a mean, a seasonal lag, and an entire evaluation grid. Because the series has a trend, the global historical mean may react slowly; because it has a four-week pattern, a seasonal repetition may perform differently from a plain last-value forecast. Neither observation determines the winner before the actual errors are calculated.

04. One forecast origin, three future horizons

Imagine the decision maker has observed weeks 1 through 12 at the end of week 12. A legitimate three-week forecast has three values, indexed \(\widehat y_{13\mid12},\widehat y_{14\mid12},\widehat y_{15\mid12}\). The corresponding outcomes—69, 82, and 87 units—are unknown when the predictions are issued. In this educational retrospective they are visible so we can score them afterward. Treat those outcomes as hidden until the scoring step.

The one-step-ahead problem asks about week 13, not about the entire subsequent quarter. The three-step-ahead problem asks about week 15, which can have different uncertainty because three future transitions intervene. A technique that appears strong at \(h=1\) can deteriorate quickly at \(h=3\), especially when it extrapolates a drift or repeats a level. We must therefore preserve the horizon label with each error instead of pooling all numbers indiscriminately.

When a method produces all three values at origin 12, that is a multi-step forecast issued once. It is not valid to update its week-15 prediction with actual week-13 or week-14 demand unless the evaluation explicitly defines a different, newly updated forecast. Confusing those two operations gives a supposed long-horizon model information it would not have had in production.

\[\widehat y_{13\mid12},\ \widehat y_{14\mid12},\ \widehat y_{15\mid12}\]

05. Baseline 1: historical mean

The Mean method forecasts every horizon as the arithmetic mean of all observations available at the origin. At origin \(o\), \(\widehat y_{o+h\mid o}^{\mathrm{mean}}=\bar y_{1:o}\) for every \(h\ge1\). It represents a stable-level reference: if the process fluctuates around a constant mean with little structure, averaging can reduce noisy changes. However, a sustained trend means old, lower observations can pull the estimate downward.

At origin 12 the first twelve demands sum to 757 units, so the forecast is \(757/12=63.0833\) units for weeks 13, 14, and 15. Substituting actual future demand gives errors \(69-63.0833=5.9167\), \(82-63.0833=18.9167\), and \(87-63.0833=23.9167\). Notice the signed errors become increasingly positive. That is a descriptive indication of lagging behind the recent rise, not proof that the data-generating process has a permanent deterministic trend.

The mean must be recomputed using only weeks through the current origin. At origin 15 its value becomes \(995/15=66.3333\) units. Using the mean of all 24 weeks for a week-13 forecast would silently incorporate the outcomes from weeks 13–24 and contaminate the evaluation. A valid baseline can be simple; its information discipline cannot be optional.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat y^{\mathrm{mean}}_{o+h\mid o}=\bar y_{1:o}=\frac1o\sum_{t=1}^{o}y_t\]

06. Baseline 2: Naive or last observation

The Naive method repeats the most recently observed outcome. Its formula is \(\widehat y_{o+h\mid o}^{\mathrm{naive}}=y_o\) for every evaluated horizon. It is the natural forecast for a random walk without drift under squared-error loss, but that theoretical statement is not a claim that our synthetic retailer follows a random walk. Operationally, the baseline says: until evidence supports a change, use the latest level.

At origin 12 the last known demand is \(y_{12}=71\) units, so the three predictions are 71, 71, 71. Actual outcomes are 69, 82, 87, producing errors \(-2\), \(+11\), \(+16\). The negative first error means we overpredicted week 13 by two units. The positive second and third errors mean we underpredicted by 11 and 16 units. Squaring these errors yields \(4\), \(121\), and \(256\) squared units, which will later explain why RMSE reacts strongly to a bad distant prediction.

Naive differs from using the latest future observation. If the origin is still week 12, week-14 Naive remains 71 even after an analyst retrospectively sees week-13 demand. Only a new forecast issued at origin 13 may use 69 as its latest observed value. We will distinguish these information sets throughout rolling evaluation.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat y^{\mathrm{naive}}_{o+h\mid o}=y_o\]

07. Baseline 3: Seasonal Naive

A seasonal reference repeats the observation in the corresponding position of the previous cycle. For a known period \(m\), the one-cycle-ahead rule is \(\widehat y_{o+h\mid o}^{\mathrm{SNaive}}=y_{o+h-m}\) when \(1\le h\le m\) and that observation is already available. For horizons beyond one full cycle, repeat the last observed corresponding seasonal position, using a valid index and never reading an unknown future outcome. Here \(m=4\) represents an invented four-week operational rhythm, not conventional yearly retail seasonality.

At origin 12 the predicted weeks 13, 14 and 15 reuse weeks 9, 10 and 11: \(64,70,80\) units. Thus the forecast errors are \(69-64=5\), \(82-70=12\), and \(87-80=7\). The source dates are part of the explanation: for week 15, the reference is week \(15-4=11\), not the latest week 12. A student should be able to identify that index before making any numerical substitution.

The rule can outperform a last-level baseline when a stable cycle is strong. It can also lag behind a rising level and systematically underestimate. It requires adequate history and a credible period; choosing \(m\) by inspecting the full dataset—including the held-out test period—creates model-selection leakage. Evaluate whether the cycle exists using only the data available in each training window.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat y^{\mathrm{SNaive}}_{o+h\mid o}=y_{o+h-m}\quad(1\le h\le m)\]

08. Baseline 4: Drift

The Drift method extends a straight line from the first observed value to the last observed value in the training window. Its per-period slope is \((y_o-y_1)/(o-1)\) units per week, provided there are at least two observations. For horizon \(h\), \(\widehat y_{o+h\mid o}^{\mathrm{drift}}=y_o+h(y_o-y_1)/(o-1)\). Unlike Naive, this baseline permits a gradual linear change; unlike fitting a general regression, this particular rule uses only the endpoints to estimate its slope.

At origin 12, the first observed demand was 46 and the last was 71. Their increase is \(71-46=25\) units across 11 one-week steps. The slope is \(25/11=2.2727\) units per week. The forecasts at \(h=1,2,3\) are \(71+25/11=73.2727\), \(71+50/11=75.5455\), and \(71+75/11=77.8182\). Errors are approximately \(-4.2727\), \(+6.4545\), \(+9.1818\). Every term has a time index and a measurement unit.

Drift is sensitive to an unusual first or last observation: an extreme endpoint can generate implausible extrapolation. If demand has a cycle, extrapolating a line between two seasonal positions can confound trend with seasonality. A sophisticated model should be compared against Drift, not automatically assumed superior to it.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat y^{\mathrm{drift}}_{o+h\mid o}=y_o+h\frac{y_o-y_1}{o-1}\]

09. One origin is a demonstration, not a performance study

Our calculations at origin 12 are transparent, but three future outcomes at one origin cannot establish lasting superiority. The demand process could change level, seasonal amplitude, inventory availability, or operating practices in the following weeks. A reliable assessment repeats the forecast process at several origins, each time freezing history, issuing predictions, and scoring them only after outcomes become available.

A single holdout takes one last block as test data; a rolling-origin backtest creates repeated historical test decisions. The backtest has two requirements that are easy to violate. First, each model must be refitted or its sufficient statistics updated using only its permitted training window. Second, the future outcomes must be matched to the correct original forecast, not to a later revision. A forecast for week 16 issued at origin 13 is different from one for week 16 issued at origin 15 because they use different information and horizons.

We will use origins 12, 15, 18 and 21, with horizons one to three, for a completely hand-checkable comparison. These origins yield nonoverlapping three-week outcome blocks, although the training histories overlap heavily. A separate set of earlier and later origins will illustrate interval calibration without using the same outcomes for width selection and final coverage scoring.

10. Expanding versus sliding training windows

An expanding window always starts at week one and extends through the latest origin: \(1{:}12\), then \(1{:}15\), then \(1{:}18\), then \(1{:}21\). It preserves all historical data and is useful if older observations remain representative. A sliding window keeps a fixed number of recent weeks; with length eight, windows at origins 12 and 15 are \(5{:}12\) and \(8{:}15\). Sliding discards old information on purpose and may adapt faster if the demand process changes.

These are not interchangeable evaluation choices. Mean and Drift may change substantially because sliding resets the first observation and the sample size. Naive is unaffected by the distant history once the last observation is known. Seasonal Naive requires at least the seasonal lag and the horizon-specific predecessor, so a window shorter than \(m\) cannot support its intended rule. We must specify the initial training length before seeing test outcomes.

A fair comparison must put the window-selection decision inside the training protocol. If an analyst tries twenty window lengths on the final test period and reports only the best, the test set has become a tuning set. The final reported score will be optimistically selected. In complex workflows use nested temporal validation or a truly untouched terminal test interval.

11. Rolling-origin matrix: exact information boundaries

The four training origins are \(o=12,15,18,21\); each issues three predictions. At origin 12 the scored target weeks are 13, 14, 15. At origin 15 they are 16, 17, 18. At origin 18 they are 19, 20, 21. At origin 21 they are 22, 23, 24. Thus there are 12 evaluated origin–horizon pairs for each baseline. A row in the evaluation matrix must identify \(o\), \(h\), \(o+h\), the actual \(y_{o+h}\), the issued forecast, and the resulting error.

For example, Seasonal Naive at \((o,h)=(15,2)\) uses \(y_{15+2-4}=y_{13}=69\) and is compared with \(y_{17}=79\), yielding \(e=79-69=10\) units. Naive at that same pair uses \(y_{15}=87\) and yields \(-8\) units. The distinction is not cosmetic; a spreadsheet with misaligned lag references may reverse a ranking even when every cell contains a plausible number.

The matrix shown below is the authoritative computational skeleton of our comparison. Every training period has at least twelve weeks; every forecast at horizons one to three uses an observed value when issued; and no row can read its eventual target when computing the prediction. A reproducible automated evaluation would assert these conditions before calculating metrics.

12. Evaluating error: sign, magnitude and units

The signed forecast error is \(e_{o,h}=y_{o+h}-\widehat y_{o+h\mid o}\). Its unit is demand units. A negative error is overprediction, and a positive error is underprediction. If inventory shortage has a higher cost than excess stock, the two signs may have unequal business consequences; the default absolute-error and squared-error scores do not encode those costs. A full decision analysis should add cost-weighted or service-level metrics alongside generic forecast scores.

Consider the first-origin Naive errors \((-2,11,16)\). Their sum is 25, so the mean signed error is \(25/3=8.333\) units. But positive and negative errors can cancel. A model with errors \((-10,+10)\) has zero average signed error while being inaccurate in every case. We therefore distinguish bias diagnostics, which retain signs, from accuracy metrics, which use absolute values or squares. A baseline may be nearly unbiased on average and nevertheless produce dangerous large misses.

All comparisons require the same scored set, or a clearly justified weighting scheme. If one model is scored on twelve rows and another on the nine easiest rows, a lower MAE is not evidence of better forecasting. Record missing outcomes and exclusion decisions before comparing models, and identify whether the error is in levels, log units or some transformed scale.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\operatorname{MAE}=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}|e_j|\]

13. Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

MAE averages absolute errors: \(\operatorname{MAE}=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|e_j|\). It measures average error magnitude in the same units as the outcome. Absolute values prevent cancellation between underpredictions and overpredictions. MAE can be understood directly by a manager: MAE of 7.75 units means the absolute gap between forecast and observed weekly demand averaged 7.75 units over the evaluated decisions. It does not mean the forecast was always within 7.75 units.

For Seasonal Naive across our twelve backtest pairs, the absolute errors are \(5,12,7,6,10,6,7,12,5,9,9,5\). Their sum is \(93\) units, so \(\operatorname{MAE}=93/12=7.75\) units. Verification: the first three terms come from the known values at origin 12; the next three from origin 15; the remaining six from origins 18 and 21. The sum rather than just the final average is disclosed so a reader can check every contribution.

MAE is resistant to an occasional extreme miss relative to RMSE, but it does not ignore extreme mistakes: doubling one large absolute error still raises the total. It treats positive and negative mistakes of equal size equally. That may be a sensible default, but a real inventory operation should additionally consider asymmetry between stockouts and excess inventory.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}e_j^2}\]

14. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)

RMSE takes the square root of the average squared error: \(\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e_j^2}\). Its final unit is again the outcome unit; the inner sum temporarily has squared units. Squaring places greater relative weight on unusually large mistakes. This is useful when a very large miss has a disproportionate operational penalty, but it also makes the result sensitive to one exceptional week.

For Seasonal Naive the twelve squared errors are \(25,144,49,36,100,36,49,144,25,81,81,25\). Their sum is \(795\), so \(\operatorname{MSE}=795/12=66.25\) squared units and \(\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{66.25}=8.1394\) demand units. Compare with MAE \(7.75\): RMSE is larger because the squared calculation emphasizes the two 12-unit errors. Neither score by itself reveals whether the errors concentrate at longer horizons or in a particular stage of the cycle.

Always state the normalization convention and sample set. Dividing by \(N\) gives the descriptive test RMSE used here; it is not the residual standard error of a fitted regression, where degrees of freedom may lead to a different denominator. Avoid comparing scores from different demand scales without an explicit normalized comparison.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[d_{\mathrm{train}}=\frac1{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^T|y_t-y_{t-m}|,\quad\mathrm{MASE}=\frac{\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{test}}}{d_{\mathrm{train}}}\]

15. Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE)

MASE compares the out-of-sample absolute forecast error to a typical in-sample seasonal-naive difference. For seasonal period \(m\) and training length \(T\), the seasonal scaling quantity is \(d_{\mathrm{train}}=(T-m)^{-1}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}|y_t-y_{t-m}|\). Then \(\operatorname{MASE}=\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{test}}/d_{\mathrm{train}}\). The numerator and denominator have the same physical units, so MASE is dimensionless. Unlike MAPE it does not divide by each observed target and can handle zero-valued individual outcomes, provided its training scale is nonzero.

For weeks 1–12 and \(m=4\), the eight differences in absolute value are \(7,7,11,5,11,6,8,9\). Their sum is 64, so the frozen training scale is \(d_{\mathrm{train}}=64/8=8\) units. Our Seasonal Naive MAE is 7.75 units, giving \(\operatorname{MASE}=7.75/8=0.96875\). A value below one means the evaluated average absolute error is smaller than this particular training-derived seasonal-naive scale. It does not prove it beats seasonal naive on the test set or on every horizon.

We deliberately use the same scale from initial training weeks 1–12 for all methods and evaluation pairs to make the teaching comparison transparent. Other protocols recompute scale at each origin, but then the aggregation and weighting must be explicitly defined. If the training series is constant within every seasonal pair, \(d_{\mathrm{train}}=0\) and MASE is undefined; report this, rather than dividing by zero or silently adding a small constant.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\operatorname{MAPE}=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\frac{e_j}{y_j}\right|\quad(y_j\ne0)\]

16. Why MAPE can mislead and even be undefined

MAPE typically averages \(100|e_t/y_t|\) across evaluated observations. The denominator is the actual outcome. For a positive demand of 100 units and absolute error 5, the percentage error is 5%; for actual demand 1 unit with the same absolute error, it is 500%. If the actual outcome is zero, the fraction is undefined. This is a mathematical problem, not something solved by a better chart.

MAPE may also reward forecasts that systematically undershoot relative to those that overshoot, depending on how errors are generated and aggregated. It is unstable near zero and can mislead when comparing series that differ in their share of low-volume weeks. Our main synthetic dataset has strictly positive demand, so its MAPE could be computed; nevertheless, we do not need it to establish a baseline ranking. Reporting MAE in units, RMSE for large-error sensitivity, and MASE for scaling gives a more transparent introduction.

A practical safeguard is to inspect zero and near-zero observations before choosing a metric and define the business loss. Some organizations prefer WAPE or scaled errors, but those have their own interpretation and denominator requirements; a percentage from aggregated volume cannot be treated as an interchangeable substitute for average percentage error. No single score captures all forecast consequences.

17. A common twelve-pair comparison of four baselines

From exactly the same twelve origin–horizon pairs, our results are: Mean MAE \(19.4256\), RMSE \(20.6022\); Naive MAE \(8.4167\), RMSE \(10.1201\); Seasonal Naive MAE \(7.7500\), RMSE \(8.1394\); Drift MAE \(8.6636\), RMSE \(9.5523\). Using the common initial MASE scale of 8 units, the corresponding scaled errors are approximately \(2.4282\), \(1.0521\), \(0.9688\), and \(1.0830\). Every value follows from the table of predictions; nothing was tuned to obtain a predetermined winner.

On this particular test set, Seasonal Naive is best under both MAE and RMSE. Drift is better than Naive in RMSE despite having a slightly worse MAE, illustrating that metrics can order methods differently when their error distributions differ. Mean is weak because the underlying series rises across the 24 weeks. These are observations about an intentionally constructed example, not statements of universal performance.

Before choosing a method, look at what the backtest omitted: there are only four origins; the synthetic cycle is known in advance; changes in promotion policy, stockouts, holidays, and measurement errors are absent. A method selected on the same backtest is at risk of selection optimism. A later, untouched test period and operational monitoring would be necessary for a credible deployment recommendation.

18. Accuracy by horizon instead of one pooled score

The pooled MAE of 7.75 units for Seasonal Naive hides three different questions. At horizon one its absolute errors across origins 12, 15, 18, 21 are \(5,6,7,9\), giving MAE \(27/4=6.75\) units. At horizon two the absolute errors are \(12,10,12,9\), giving \(43/4=10.75\) units. At horizon three they are \(7,6,5,5\), giving \(23/4=5.75\) units. The middle horizon happens to be the hardest in this small constructed sample.

Why can horizon two be worse than three? The relationship need not be monotonically worsening for a seasonal baseline: different horizons correspond to different positions in the four-week cycle. It would be incorrect to assume a general law from four observations per horizon. Presenting the horizon-wise table reveals operational decisions, such as whether a two-week replenishment plan requires wider safeguards than a three-week plan under the current data.

An overlapping rolling evaluation with every weekly origin creates even more scores but not necessarily more independent information. Adjacent origins reuse almost identical training windows, and their forecast errors can be correlated. Standard errors or confidence intervals around performance differences must respect that dependence; treating hundreds of overlapping errors as independent observations can make claims appear too precise.

19. Transformations belong inside each training window

Suppose we standardize demand by subtracting a mean and dividing by a standard deviation, or estimate a seasonal index, choose a Box–Cox transformation parameter, impute missing values, or fit a regression with external predictors. Each of these steps learns information from data. At origin 12 the parameters must be obtained from weeks 1–12 only. The held-out weeks 13–24 must not affect the transformation before their forecasts are issued.

A transparent evaluation pipeline does the following in order: define an origin; isolate its training history; estimate all transformations and model parameters on that history; transform future predictor information only if it would actually have been known; generate forecasts; invert transformations using training-fitted quantities; then compare with the untouched real outcomes. Repeat the full procedure at each origin. Fitting a scaler once to all 24 weeks and then splitting afterward violates the time information boundary even if the forecasting formula itself looks innocent.

For log forecasts, inversion deserves particular attention. If a forecast is for mean log demand, simply exponentiating often estimates a median on the original scale rather than the conditional mean; bias correction may be needed for expected-value forecasting. If only a baseline in original demand units is needed, avoiding unnecessary transformations simplifies the audit. The core rule is consistent: every learned operation must be reconstructed as it would have existed at forecast time.

20. Interval forecasts answer a different question

A point forecast supplies one target value, such as 80 demand units. A prediction interval supplies a range intended to contain the future observation with a specified long-run coverage frequency under stated modeling and evaluation conditions. For example, an 80% interval aims to cover about 80% of future observations across comparable forecast occasions. It does not promise that a particular week has an 80% chance of falling inside an interval in every inferential framework, and a single interval cannot be validated by itself.

A confidence interval for a regression mean and a prediction interval for one new demand value are different objects. The latter accounts for variability of individual future outcomes in addition to uncertainty about their expected level. Time-series errors also depend on horizon, serial correlation, distributional shape, regime changes and possibly seasonal position. It is therefore unsafe to take a single normal critical value multiplied by one pooled residual standard deviation and call the result universally valid for every horizon.

This guide uses a deliberately simple empirical absolute-error interval around Seasonal Naive forecasts. We will calibrate widths using earlier, historical rolling errors and then evaluate their observed coverage on later origins. These intervals are an educational construction; because the residuals are dependent and the historical sample is tiny, no formal finite-sample coverage guarantee will be claimed.

21. Calibration data versus final evaluation data

To construct prediction intervals without looking ahead, separate an initial history, a calibration segment of errors whose outcomes are already known, and a final evaluation segment whose outcomes must not select widths. Choose calibration origins 8,9,10,11,12,13, at each horizon h=1,2,3. Their latest target week is 13+3=16. At the close of week 16, all eighteen calibration errors are available, provided the reporting system has finished publishing that week's outcome. This is a fully chronological calibration boundary.

Freeze the widths at origin 16. Evaluate archived forecasts issued from origins 16 through 21. Their target outcomes occur in weeks 17 through 24, none of which has entered the calibration sample. Each original forecast uses only the last-known weekly series, so both the interval center and its width are time-valid. Errors for different origins and horizons can be correlated, and the seasonal-naive method is not estimated here using test outcomes, but the evaluation is genuinely out of calibration.

Using origin 15 at horizon three for calibration at origin 16 would violate the same rule: that calibration error requires the outcome of week 18. The general eligibility criterion is r+h<=o, where r is a historical forecast origin, h is its target horizon, and o is the current issuance time. A real system must additionally consider publication delays. This is not a guarantee of future coverage, merely correct separation of information.

KEY EQUATION · symbols defined in the explanation
\[\widehat I_{o,h}=\bigl[\widehat y_{o+h\mid o}-q_{h,o},\ \widehat y_{o+h\mid o}+q_{h,o}\bigr]\]

22. A stricter online interval protocol and no-lookahead checks

Forecast errors have release dates just like observations. A forecast issued at origin r for horizon h can be evaluated only when the observed target y_(r+h) is published. Its error becomes eligible for interval calibration at a current origin o only when r+h<=o. Our fixed calibration origins 8 through 13 satisfy this requirement for horizons one to three when widths are frozen at the close of origin 16.

An online alternative recalculates the width q_(h,o) at every origin using all eligible earlier errors and documenting how many have become available. It issues intervals [forecast-q_(h,o),forecast+q_(h,o)], then stores the actual outcome once observed. This procedure may adapt to changing volatility, but it must not tune quantile levels after inspecting the untouched final test. The online and fixed-width protocols answer different questions and should not be mixed within one reported score.

Operational timestamp is more precise than a period label. If the week-16 forecast is issued before the week-16 demand report is finalized, the last error of our calibration set has not yet become usable. The decision maker must either delay issuance or remove the unavailable calibration error and recalculate the quantile. An auditor should reconstruct a record of original issuance time, permitted training observations, available calibration outcomes and score timestamp for every row.

23. Empirical widths by forecast horizon

For the six calibration origins 8–13, horizon-one absolute Seasonal Naive errors are 11,6,8,9,5,12 units, ordered as 5,6,8,9,11,12. At horizon two the six absolute errors are 6,8,9,5,12,7, ordered as 5,6,7,8,9,12. At horizon three they are 8,9,5,12,7,6, also ordered as 5,6,7,8,9,12. The source for each error is its archived forecast issued no later than week 13 and its actual target observed by week 16.

For an illustrative nearest-rank 75th percentile choose rank ceil(0.75*6)=5. Thus the horizon-specific half-widths are q1=11, q2=9 and q3=9 units. At origin 16, the horizon-one Seasonal Naive center is week-13 demand 69 and the resulting interval is [69-11,69+11]=[58,80]. Week-17 demand 79 lies within the interval. At horizon two the center is week-14 demand 82 and the interval [73,91] contains week-18 demand 88.

The nominal 75% is a label for an empirical quantile, not a coverage theorem. Six calibration examples, serial dependence and process changes can invalidate simple nominal interpretations. The matching widths at horizons two and three are an accident of the example; a longer horizon may need a wider interval in another dataset. The calculation is transparent precisely so its limitations can be examined.

24. Coverage by horizon: numerators and denominators

Coverage is the fraction of evaluated outcomes inside their corresponding bands, including boundary values. For horizon h, the observed rate is the number of evaluation errors whose absolute value is no greater than the frozen half-width qh divided by the number of evaluation errors at that horizon. The widths were fixed after week 16; the scored outcomes from weeks 17–24 did not select those widths. This separation is an out-of-calibration evaluation, albeit a tiny and dependent sample.

At horizon one, six errors at evaluation origins 16–21 are 10,6,7,12,5,9. With q1=11 the procedure covers five of six, or 83.33%. At horizon two errors are 6,7,12,5,9,9; with q2=9 the result is again five of six. At horizon three errors are 7,12,5,9,9,5; with q3=9 we obtain five of six. Pooled coverage is 15/18=83.33%. Because the same outcome can be scored from different horizons, the eighteen indicators are not necessarily independent.

The observed coverage exceeds the calibration quantile label of 75%, but that difference is not a reliable finding from only six cases per horizon. A useful report provides counts 5/6, interval half-widths 11,9,9, the calibration date and explicit uncertainty limits. Neither this number nor the small synthetic example proves a formal future-coverage guarantee. Operational assessment requires additional data and checks for dependence and regime change.

25. Coverage is not enough: interval width and reliability

An interval that covers every outcome is not necessarily useful. The trivial interval \([-\infty,+\infty]\) has perfect coverage but offers no planning guidance. We therefore report coverage together with width or interval score. The illustrative intervals here have width \(2q_h=18\) demand units at every horizon. A narrower interval might be more useful if it maintains honest coverage; a wider one may be needed when uncertainty rises. Comparing coverage without width invites a model to win merely by becoming vague.

We should also examine conditional coverage: does the interval miss more often for high-demand weeks, particular seasonal positions, or periods following a sudden jump? A 75% overall proportion can hide dangerous undercoverage during peak demand. With only six examples per horizon in our case, subgroup percentages would be extremely unstable; it is more appropriate to describe the question and collect additional observations before drawing conclusions.

Finally, a calibrated interval assumes that historical error behavior is informative about future errors. Regime changes, promotions, measurement changes, and model recalibration may invalidate that assumption. Under serial dependence, popular exchangeability-based conformal coverage arguments need additional care or altered methods; our nearest-rank empirical intervals are not presented as conformal intervals. The honest report is an observed count with stated limitations.

26. When comparing methods must have a separate final test

Suppose we calculate four baseline scores and select the smallest MAE. Even if each score was computed without direct future leakage, the act of choosing a winner uses that evaluation period to make a modeling decision. If we then quote the winner's performance on the same period as if it were a final untouched test, the estimate can be optimistic. Multiple candidate windows, seasonal periods, transformations, and models increase the selection effect.

A rigorous workflow separates: historical training used to fit methods; validation origins used to choose the candidate and tune design choices; and a later final test segment not consulted during selection. For time series, these partitions must preserve chronology. The final test has a distinct purpose: estimating prospective performance of a choice that has already been fixed. After inspecting final-test results, modifying the model begins a new development cycle and requires fresh independent evidence for an unbiased final claim.

We therefore describe Seasonal Naive as best within our illustrative twelve-pair comparison, not as an established champion. Choosing a method also involves horizon, asymmetric business costs, computation, missing-value behavior and the reliability of its interval forecasts. A manager may prefer a stable, slightly less accurate baseline if it gives safer operational decisions under the relevant loss function.

27. A checklist for temporal leakage

Consider four proposed operations. Case A: compute a moving average centered at week 12 using week 13 to predict week 13. This is immediate leakage because the feature reads its target or another future value. Case B: normalize all 24 weeks before splitting at week 12. Leakage enters through the global mean and standard deviation. Case C: choose the seasonal period after inspecting which period best fits weeks 13–24. Even if later forecasts use valid lags, model selection consumed held-out data. Case D: use a horizon-three calibration error issued at week 15 when predicting at origin 16. Its outcome is not yet observed until week 18; the interval width leaks future information.

For each case ask three questions: What calendar timestamp does the feature or parameter depend on? When would that value have become available in real operations? Does its availability precede the forecast issuance timestamp? It is not enough to compare row numbers: some external economic indicator may be published weeks after the period it describes. Data revisions can also contaminate a backtest if today's corrected history replaces the values available at earlier origins.

The correction is to reconstruct a point-in-time information set for every origin, including publication delays, known future calendars, data revisions and calibration outcomes. A well-designed backtest is a simulation of decisions that could actually have been taken, not just a clever chronological split.

28. Statistical comparison versus practical value

The difference between two MAEs might be economically important or almost irrelevant. Suppose Seasonal Naive has MAE 7.75 and Naive has 8.4167, a gap of about 0.667 demand units per prediction. That is a descriptive gap over twelve constructed decisions. It is not a significance test, and it does not quantify savings because the cost per unit of error was never specified. Furthermore, errors from adjacent forecasts may be dependent, and the methods can fail together during shocks.

A paired comparison would calculate the per-decision difference in losses, such as \(d_j=|e_{j,\mathrm{Naive}}|-|e_{j,\mathrm{SNaive}}|\), and inspect its temporal pattern and uncertainty. With only four origins and three horizons each, we should be cautious about inferential claims. For stronger assessment, collect more genuine out-of-sample origins, choose a method appropriate for dependence, and report sensitivity to season, forecast horizon and window choices.

A recommendation for inventory should specify business consequences: shortage costs, overstock costs, storage capacity, order lead times, price changes and service-level targets. A model can be statistically appealing and operationally inappropriate if it yields biased upper-tail risk or depends on information that will not be available when orders are placed.

29. An end-to-end reproducible evaluation protocol

A complete protocol starts with an explicit question: forecast weekly demand in units, issued at the close of each eligible week, for horizons one, two and three. Next verify that every timestamp and unit is consistent, decide a plausible cycle before evaluating future outcomes, establish a minimum training length, and list the baselines to compare. Generate the chronological origin schedule and maintain one row per origin, horizon and method.

For each origin, freeze data access; fit or update the baseline; issue all required horizons; and store forecasts with their issuance timestamps. Only afterward append the realized outcomes to calculate signed, absolute and squared errors. Compute MAE and RMSE on exactly matched pairs. For MASE, calculate and document the training scale without using held-out outcomes. Produce horizon-wise tables rather than hiding distinctions inside a global average. Record transformations, imputations and hyperparameter choices as versioned objects.

Interval calibration requires a second information audit: historical errors cannot be used until their outcomes are known. Evaluate both interval widths and coverage by horizon on truly later predictions. Finally, before declaring a model ready, examine leakage, unexpected regime shifts, dependence among errors, consequences of model selection and operational costs. The final deliverable should make it possible for someone else to reconstruct a single scored row from original data to final decision.

30. What forecasts can and cannot establish

A low historical MAE does not establish causation, future stationarity, or robust performance under structural change. A temporal split helps prevent one family of leakage but cannot repair a malformed target, hidden stockouts, missing revisions, data collection bias or opportunistic model selection. Similarly, empirical interval coverage is not a guarantee that future consumers or weeks will follow the same behavior. The quality of a forecast depends on data-generating conditions as well as algorithm choice.

We should identify approximations explicitly. Mean and Drift are deterministic forecast recipes. Our MAE/RMSE numbers are exact descriptive calculations to displayed precision, not population parameters. Seasonal period four is imposed synthetically. The intervals use a nearest-rank empirical heuristic, not a calibrated normal-theory distribution nor a distribution-free guarantee under dependence. The reported coverage proportions have tiny denominators and overlapping outcome periods. These facts should appear wherever a reader might otherwise mistake a computational output for a scientific conclusion.

The final exercises below are intentionally independent of the first calculations. They change origins, windows, loss functions, horizons and calibration eligibility. Understanding means explaining why the formulas apply, reproducing key substitutions, distinguishing available from unavailable information, and recognizing when evidence is too weak for a confident decision.

31. Worked exercise 1 — verify a new origin

Problem. At origin 15, with demand known through week 15, calculate the three-step Naive and Seasonal Naive forecasts and their signed errors. The new outcomes are weeks 16–18: 77, 79, 88 units. Do not use any outcome after week 15 to construct a forecast.

Solution. Naive repeats \(y_{15}=87\) at every horizon. Its three errors are \(77-87=-10\), \(79-87=-8\), and \(88-87=1\) units. Seasonal Naive with \(m=4\) uses weeks \(12,13,14\), giving forecasts \(71,69,82\) units. Its errors are \(77-71=6\), \(79-69=10\), and \(88-82=6\) units. All three seasonal reference weeks precede origin 15, so the method respects the information boundary. The average absolute error at this origin is \((10+8+1)/3=19/3\approx6.333\) for Naive versus \((6+10+6)/3=22/3\approx7.333\) for Seasonal Naive.

Interpretation and verification. Naive is better at this particular origin under MAE despite losing to Seasonal Naive across the complete twelve-pair backtest. That is exactly why a single origin is not sufficient for model choice. Reproduce the seasonal lag indices before interpreting the numbers: a perfectly calculated error from an invalid future reference would still be an invalid backtest.

32. Worked exercise 2 — recompute Drift from endpoints

Problem. At origin 21, the known first observation is \(y_1=46\) and the latest observed value is \(y_{21}=84\). Calculate Drift forecasts at horizons one, two, and three, then compare with realized demand \(97,103,94\) in weeks 22–24.

Solution. The slope is \((84-46)/(21-1)=38/20=1.9\) units per week. Therefore \(\widehat y_{22\mid21}=84+1.9=85.9\), \(\widehat y_{23\mid21}=84+3.8=87.8\), and \(\widehat y_{24\mid21}=84+5.7=89.7\). Signed errors are \(97-85.9=11.1\), \(103-87.8=15.2\), \(94-89.7=4.3\). MAE is \((11.1+15.2+4.3)/3=30.6/3=10.2\) units. Each slope term has units of demand per week; multiplying by \(h\) weeks produces demand units, making addition to 84 dimensionally legitimate.

Interpretation. All three forecasts underestimate actual demand. However, one origin does not prove permanent bias. A useful independent check is the origin-21 Naive forecast: it stays at 84 units, yielding larger errors \(13,19,10\). This demonstrates why positive drift can sometimes help, while other origins can reverse the comparison.

33. Worked exercise 3 — compare two candidate MAEs

Problem. A planning team reports mean absolute errors 8.4167 for Naive and 7.7500 for Seasonal Naive over identical twelve scored decisions. Quantify the difference in units and as a percentage of the Naive score. Does that prove an annual saving of a given number of dollars?

Solution. The absolute improvement is \(8.4167-7.7500=0.6667\) demand units per scored forecast. Relative to the Naive benchmark, this is \(100(0.6667/8.4167)\approx7.92\%\). The denominator matters: dividing by Seasonal Naive MAE would answer a different percentage question. Both scores reflect the same small synthetic backtest; no dollar conversion is possible without the business loss associated with excess and insufficient inventory.

Interpretation. An improvement near 7.9% in MAE is potentially useful but is not a universal performance guarantee. The number of future decisions, unit costs, exposure to extremes and dependence among errors are unreported. A complete recommendation needs separate untouched test performance and scenario-specific cost calculations, not merely a percent improvement. A student should never multiply 0.6667 blindly by a future number of weeks and call it profit: absolute forecast errors do not automatically translate linearly into operating cost.

34. Worked exercise 4 — compute an RMSE and explain the square

Problem. One baseline produces errors \(-2,11,16\) units across three horizon scores. Compute MAE and RMSE and explain why they differ. Distinguish MSE units from RMSE units.

Solution. Absolute errors are \(2,11,16\), summing to 29, so \(\operatorname{MAE}=29/3=9.6667\) units. Squared errors are \(4,121,256\), summing to 381; \(\operatorname{MSE}=381/3=127\) units squared. Taking the positive square root gives \(\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{127}\approx11.2694\) units. Notice that squaring 16 yields 256, much greater than 16 itself. This is why a single large miss matters more under RMSE.

Interpretation. RMSE of 11.27 does not mean every prediction misses by roughly eleven units: it is a quadratic summary of three uneven errors. The calculation also does not tell us which type of error was more costly, because signs disappear when squared. If stockout costs dominate excess-stock costs, a business-specific weighted loss could be more informative. To verify, check that RMSE is at least MAE for a common finite error set, as it is here: 11.2694 exceeds 9.6667.

35. Worked exercise 5 — calculate a valid seasonal MASE scale

Problem. The initial training period includes weeks 1–12, with seasonal period four. The eight absolute seasonal differences are \(7,7,11,5,11,6,8,9\) units. A tested method has out-of-sample MAE 8.4167 units. Calculate the seasonal training scale and MASE; interpret without overclaiming.

Solution. The scale is \((7+7+11+5+11+6+8+9)/8=64/8=8\) demand units. The method's MASE is \(8.4167/8\approx1.0521\), dimensionless. The numerator is a test error; the denominator comes exclusively from the initial training observations. If we instead compute the scale using weeks 13–24, future information has entered the reported denominator and the result is not the predeclared scaled metric.

Interpretation. MASE above one says this method's average test absolute error exceeds the particular in-sample seasonal-naive scaling magnitude. It does not necessarily say the method loses to seasonal naive in the same test set; that requires direct comparison of test errors. If the training series were perfectly constant at all four-week lags, the denominator would be zero and the statistic would need to be reported as undefined. Replacing zero by an arbitrary epsilon changes the definition.

36. Worked exercise 6 — explain zero-demand MAPE

Problem. Three stores have actual weekly demands \(100,1,0\) units and absolute forecast errors \(5,5,5\) units. An analyst wants to report a single conventional MAPE. Compute each term where possible, identify the problem, and propose a more defensible summary.

Solution. The first percentage is \(100(5/100)=5\%\). The second is \(100(5/1)=500\%\). The third would be \(100(5/0)\), which is undefined. Thus conventional MAPE over all three outcomes is not a finite, well-defined number. Dropping the zero without reporting the exclusion would change the sample; assigning it an arbitrary 100% would invent a metric definition.

Interpretation. MAE remains computable: \((5+5+5)/3=5\) units. RMSE is also 5 units because all three errors have equal magnitude. A training-scaled MASE can be considered if a nonzero training scale is available. A business comparison might use inventory costs or an explicitly defined aggregate-volume metric, but must state its denominator and treatment of zeros. The lesson is not that percentages are always forbidden; it is that denominators determine what the quantity means and whether it exists at all.

37. Worked exercise 7 — audit sliding and expanding windows

Problem. At forecast origin 15, write the expanding training window and a sliding window of length eight. Identify which observations each method may use and calculate the first and last values of both windows. Explain why using week 16 for a feature would be invalid.

Solution. The expanding window is weeks \(1{:}15\), beginning with 46 and ending with \(y_{15}=87\) units. The length-eight sliding window is weeks \(8{:}15\), beginning with \(y_8=62\) and ending with 87 units. Under expanding Drift, the endpoint slope is \((87-46)/14=41/14\approx2.9286\) units per week. Under sliding Drift, the endpoint slope would be \((87-62)/7=25/7\approx3.5714\) units per week, a different estimator using a different information subset. Both are legitimate if defined before evaluation.

Interpretation. The future \(y_{16}=77\) must not enter either training mean, slope or transformation at origin 15. It is reserved for scoring a horizon-one forecast. A sliding window is not automatically better simply because it uses recent observations. It throws away information and may create unstable parameters; a fair protocol evaluates both strategies on matched future observations, preferably selecting window length during validation rather than the final test.

38. Worked exercise 8 — compute horizon-specific MAE

Problem. Four origin-one errors from Seasonal Naive are \(5,6,7,9\); four horizon-two errors are \(12,10,12,9\); four horizon-three errors are \(7,6,5,5\), all in demand units. Calculate the separate MAEs and the pooled MAE. Why does the pooled number not answer every planning question?

Solution. All errors happen to be positive, so absolute values do not change them. Horizon-one MAE is \((5+6+7+9)/4=27/4=6.75\). Horizon-two MAE is \((12+10+12+9)/4=43/4=10.75\). Horizon-three MAE is \((7+6+5+5)/4=23/4=5.75\). Pooling twelve errors gives \((27+43+23)/12=93/12=7.75\) units. As a cross-check, equally weighting three horizon-specific MAEs gives \((6.75+10.75+5.75)/3=7.75\), because each horizon contributes four cases.

Interpretation. The pooled score hides the fact that horizon two performed worst in these examples. If a manager needs to order inventory exactly two weeks ahead, the relevant MAE is 10.75, not 7.75. The horizon ranking is not a general time-series theorem and might reverse under new data. In an evaluation with unequal numbers of observations per horizon, the pooled score is a count-weighted average, not simply the mean of horizon averages.

39. Worked exercise 9 — reconstruct one prediction interval

Problem. At origin 16 and horizon one, Seasonal Naive uses week-13 demand 69 units. The horizon-one half-width was set to eleven using completed calibration outcomes through week 16. Actual week-17 demand is 79. Compute interval limits, full width, error and coverage indicator.

Solution. Lower bound is 69-11=58 and upper bound 69+11=80 units. Full width is 80-58=22 units. The signed error is 79-69=+10 units, and its absolute value is ten. Since 58<=79<=80, the coverage indicator equals one. The observed value lies one unit below the upper limit. If the observation had been 81, it would fall outside; if it had been exactly 80, it would be counted as covered by our inclusive convention.

Interpretation. One successful interval does not validate a 75% coverage procedure, and a wider interval will tend to cover more outcomes. This calculation is information-valid at origin 16 because the six calibration errors at horizon one have targets no later than week 14 and therefore were already available. The method's performance must be assessed over many later forecast origins, with width and coverage reported together.

40. Worked exercise 10 — count empirical interval coverage

Problem. Six horizon-one absolute errors are 10,6,7,12,5,9 units and the pre-frozen half-width is eleven. At horizons two and three the half-width is nine; errors are 6,7,12,5,9,9 and 7,12,5,9,9,5. Compute coverage by horizon and in total, showing counts and denominators.

Solution. Horizon one covers every error except 12, hence 5/6=83.33%. Horizon two also misses only 12 and covers 5/6=83.33%. Horizon three covers 5/6=83.33%. Pooling gives 15 covered outcomes among 18 scored origin–horizon pairs, or 15/18=83.33%. To verify horizon one, note that 10 is covered because 10<=11; it would be missed if someone mistakenly used the horizon-two half-width nine. The horizon labels therefore materially affect the count.

Interpretation. The widths were fixed using calibration outcomes available at origin 16, before the scored targets in weeks 17–24. But six cases per horizon cannot support a precise population coverage estimate. Dependence across errors and repeated target dates across horizons also matter. State empirical coverage and interval widths, not a guaranteed coverage percentage, and recommend more chronologically later observations.

41. Worked exercise 11 — expose interval calibration leakage

Problem. An analyst wants to issue an interval at origin 16 and horizon three. The proposed calibration sample includes a forecast error generated at origin 15 and horizon three. Is it available? Identify the target date and derive a valid eligibility rule.

Solution. The calibration forecast issued at origin 15 for horizon three targets week \(15+3=18\). Its error requires actual \(y_{18}\), not available at origin 16. Therefore including that error in interval-width estimation is leakage, even if the interval center itself uses only values through week 16. For a calibration error from origin \(r\) and target horizon \(h\), eligibility at current origin \(o\) requires \(r+h\le o\) and actual release of that outcome by issuance time. Thus the suggested error fails since \(18>16\).

Interpretation and transfer. A valid alternative is to compute a current horizon-three calibration quantile from eligible earlier errors only, keeping a documented minimum sample size. Another alternative is to delay issuance until enough calibration outcomes exist. This exercise demonstrates that a chronological Train/Test division alone is insufficient: leakage can enter through hyperparameter selection, transformations, error quantiles or the publication delay of observed outcomes. The correct unit of audit is the entire forecasting pipeline.

42. Worked exercise 12 — independent decision and complete recommendation

Problem. A retailer must choose a weekly three-horizon forecast method. Our backtest ranks Seasonal Naive first on pooled MAE and RMSE, but interval calibration is retrospective and only four origins were used for the point comparison. Write a defensible recommendation, identify what can be adopted now and what must be verified before relying on interval coverage.

Complete solution. First state the evidence: Seasonal Naive has MAE 7.75 and RMSE 8.1394 units across twelve pairs; Naive has MAE 8.4167 and RMSE 10.1201. The observed MAE difference is approximately 0.6667 units per scored forecast. Next name the limitation: the data are synthetic, the four-week cycle was imposed, only four point-evaluation origins were used, and selection on that same backtest may be optimistic. The horizon-two Seasonal Naive MAE is 10.75 units, which matters if inventory must be ordered two weeks ahead.

For initial operational exploration we may retain Seasonal Naive as a transparent candidate baseline, not a certified production champion. Before deployment, reserve later chronological observations untouched by model selection; investigate demand censoring from stockouts, seasonal stability, and business cost asymmetry; issue archived forecasts at genuine origins; and evaluate point losses and horizon-specific coverage with calibration errors available at each origin. Document width alongside coverage, dates and denominators. Do not claim a 75% prospective interval guarantee from the small-sample \(15/18\) count. This final decision links mathematical computation with evidence limits and operational consequences.
