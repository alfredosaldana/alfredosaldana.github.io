1. The forecasting question and the time boundary

A utility needs the electricity demand of its service territory for the next six months. Historical demand has a calendar pattern and changes when temperatures rise. A model that only remembers previous demand may ignore useful outside information; ordinary regression may explain weather but leave correlated forecast errors. The task is to combine both sources while respecting an absolute rule: a forecast made at month 30 cannot use measurements first observed in months 31–36. This rule sounds simple; most serious errors in applied forecasting violate it indirectly through preprocessing, revised data, future temperatures or model selection on the evaluation period.

This guide builds a regression with ARIMA errors. We use the term external predictor for an input whose value is not the target outcome. External does not necessarily mean statistically independent, exogenous in a causal sense, or known ahead of time. Those are separate questions. The distinction matters when a model uses temperature to predict energy demand: a future calendar date is known, but the temperature is not known with certainty. An advance weather forecast may be available, yet its accuracy varies with horizon.

Our evidence is deliberately synthetic, not observed: 36 consecutive monthly demand measurements, measured in abstract gigawatt-hours (GWh), an illustrative average temperature in degrees Fahrenheit, and a preannounced winter scheduling indicator. The first 30 months form the training set. The last six are held out, and every benchmark uses the same forecast origin. The student will inspect all 36 observations, reproduce the predictor transformations, follow the parameter estimates, compute forecasting errors, and distinguish conditional uncertainty from uncertainty in tomorrow's weather. This scope is predictive; none of the fitted associations establishes that changing the temperature or the schedule would cause the estimated change in energy consumption.

2. Prerequisites and a conceptual map

The necessary foundations come from regression interactions and transformations (Guide 53), diagnostic residuals and influence (54), robust uncertainty (55), ARIMA models (69), and seasonal ARIMA (70). You do not need to recall all equations immediately. Review the distinction between a fitted mean and an individual outcome: a regression predicts a systematic part while an error captures deviations. Also review an AR(1) process, in which an error at month t may depend on the error at t−1. A white-noise innovation is the new unpredictable shock after accounting for all specified dependencies.

We proceed in four linked layers. The measurement layer defines units, calendar indexing, data vintage and the forecast origin. The explanatory layer states which predictors could change expected demand and whether their values are known by the origin. The dependence layer models what remains after subtracting the explanatory mean. The decision layer forecasts the outcome and communicates scenarios, error metrics and uncertainty. Separating these layers prevents a common shortcut: fitting a sophisticated ARIMA error model does not make an unavailable predictor observable in the future.

Important terminology: a lag of x at time t is x observed in an earlier period, written x_{t−k}; a lead would be a future value, which generally requires an external forecast. A forecast horizon h is the number of steps after the origin; h=1 and h=6 represent different information sets and different uncertainty. A conditional prediction assumes specific future predictor values. An unconditional prediction integrates over a probability model for those future inputs. Our worked SARIMAX forecast is conditional, and we explicitly explore what is omitted from its nominal prediction interval.

3. A complete synthetic data-generating process

For complete reproducibility, number the months t=1,...,36. Let the recurring monthly reference temperatures be 35, 38, 46, 56, 66, 76, 81, 79, 71, 60, 49 and 39 degrees Fahrenheit. Apply the repeating synthetic offsets 0, +2 and −1 to successive months in a three-month cycle (the same offset pattern repeats every year). Define C_t=max(0,T_t−60), the number of degrees above a 60°F reference. Despite the name, this is a monthly temperature-derived predictor, not a properly integrated daily cooling-degree-day measurement. Define H_t=1 during months 11 and 12 of each annual cycle, zero otherwise; it denotes a calendar schedule established before measurement, not a realized weather shock.

For the teaching data, the construction is Y_t=120+0.9t+1.6C_t+6H_t+u_t, with u_t=0.5u_{t−1}+z_t, starting at u_0=0. The innovations z_t are a fixed supplied sequence, not randomly sampled observations. The entire list appears in the accompanying reproducibility file. A value of C_t=8 therefore contributes 12.8 GWh to the constructed demand before the error term; H_t=1 adds 6 GWh in the construction. These are definitions of a synthetic generator, not claims about real energy elasticities.

Check the first observation: T_1=35°F, C_1=0, H_1=0, u_1=1, and Y_1=120+0.9+0+0+1=121.9 GWh. At t=7, T_7=81°F and C_7=21; the generated systematic component is 120+6.3+33.6=159.9 GWh. With u_7=1.828125, Y_7=161.728125 GWh. Listing observations is not an alternative model: it supplies an audit trail so that every fitted calculation can be traced back to a stated input and unit.

4. Why choosing a monthly time index matters

The time index t is not merely a row number. It defines the horizon, the permissible predictors, and the unit of a trend coefficient. In the construction, 0.9 is GWh per elapsed month, not GWh per year or per degree. If some months were missing and rows were simply numbered consecutively, the coefficient would accidentally describe transitions between surviving records rather than elapsed time. Therefore verify that every consecutive integer corresponds to exactly one consecutive month. The example has 36 months and 35 adjacent transitions, with no missing calendar periods.

A second indexing choice is how to represent seasonality. An indicator for a scheduled event in November and December is known as soon as the calendar is fixed. Temperature during those months is not. Either variable can be useful, but only one is immediately forecastable without another model. An annual pattern in demand may be captured by temperature, calendar indicators, autoregressive seasonal terms, or several of these together; including everything without diagnosis can produce unstable coefficients and confound interpretations.

A third choice is data vintage. Actual weather records and corrected meter totals may be released days or weeks after the nominal month ends. A retrospective spreadsheet may show the final revised series even though the forecaster at month 30 had only preliminary values. A faithful backtest should archive the version available at each origin. In the synthetic example, we declare all training inputs available by the origin, and future temperatures replaced by an explicit scenario. That declaration is an assumption, not a universal operational truth.

5. Availability: known, forecast, and forbidden inputs

Every prospective variable needs an as-of timestamp: when could the forecasting team actually know its value? Distinguish (A) genuinely known future quantities such as month number, planned calendar schedules and a contractually fixed tariff; (B) unknown quantities with legitimate forecasts, such as future temperature or consumer activity; and (C) unknowable at the origin, such as the realized demand in the evaluation month. An observed future weather measurement belongs to category C for a true ex-ante forecast, even though weather is not the target variable.

For our six-month evaluation, H_{31},...,H_{36} and the time indices are already known. At t=30, the last available temperature record is T_{30}. For the forecast scenario, define T^*{30+h}=T{18+h}+0.5°F, h=1,...,6: use each corresponding month from the previous year, plus a predeclared offset. These numbers are 81.5, 81.5, 70.5, 60.5, 51.5 and 38.5°F. They can be calculated at the origin without inspecting the six held-out temperatures. They are not forecasts validated against actual meteorology; they are a transparent illustration of a permissible construction.

The cooling predictors in the evaluation are consequently C^*=21.5, 21.5, 10.5, 0.5, 0 and 0. For the final two months H=1. A reader who substitutes the actual future temperatures 81, 81, 70, 60, 51 and 38°F is conducting a different oracle-conditional calculation. It can be used as a diagnostic sensitivity scenario but must never be mislabeled an operational performance estimate. The distinction persists even if the oracle scenario happens to have better accuracy.

Key mathematical relation
\[T^*_{30+h}=T_{18+h}+0.5,\quad C^*_{30+h}=\max(0,T^*_{30+h}-60)\]

6. Regression for the conditional mean

Define the systematic expected demand as m_t=β_0+β_1t+β_2C_t+β_3H_t. The model then writes Y_t=m_t+u_t. β_0 has units GWh and is the expected component at t=0, C=0 and H=0; because t=0 is outside the observed sample, treating this intercept as an operationally meaningful baseline requires caution. β_1 is GWh per month holding the other modeled variables fixed. β_2 is GWh per °F above the threshold, while the slope below 60°F is zero in the specified hinge form. β_3 is the difference in GWh between scheduled and nonscheduled months conditional on the other regressors.

The conditional language matters. A β_2 estimate does not say that raising temperature one degree causes an exactly equal change in demand. Unmeasured economic conditions, occupancy, changing technology, holiday practices or policy may affect both the predictor and the outcome. Nor is the relation globally linear: the constructed hinge has a kink at 60°F. For example, temperature 58°F and 59°F both give C=0; moving from 70°F to 71°F changes C from 10 to 11 and the modeled mean by β_2 GWh, all else equal.

The same regression may be fitted by OLS as an exploratory starting point. For months 1–30, the independent OLS calculation gives roughly β̂=(120.2684,0.8950,1.6168,6.0336). OLS estimates the mean component, but independent-error standard errors and classical residual diagnostics would be inappropriate if u_t retains temporal dependence. The next stage models that dependence explicitly, instead of attempting to solve it by discarding a correlated residual plot.

Key mathematical relation
\[Y_t=\underbrace{\beta_0+\beta_1t+\beta_2C_t+\beta_3H_t}_{m_t}+u_t\]

7. Regression residuals are not the same as innovations

A regression residual at time t is r_t=Y_t−m̂t, the difference between the observation and the fitted systematic mean. A time-series innovation, usually denoted ε_t, is the new shock remaining after we have also predicted the correlated regression disturbance from its own past. In an AR(1) error model, u_t=φu{t−1}+ε_t; therefore ε_t=u_t−φu_{t−1}. The two quantities answer different questions: r_t asks how far an observation lies from the regression part; ε_t asks what new unexplained information arrived after using temporal history.

A numeric demonstration does not require fitted software. In our synthetic generator, u_1=1 and u_2=−1.5. With φ=0.5, the second innovation equals ε_2=−1.5−0.5(1)=−2. The regression disturbance at t=2 is −1.5, while the innovation is −2; neither should be silently substituted for the other. If a model with ARIMA errors fits properly, we want its standardized innovations to show little remaining predictable dependence. The original regression residuals may remain strongly correlated by design.

A fitted innovation is also an estimate, not identical to the generator's invisible shock. Initial states, parameter uncertainty, model specification and measurement errors influence the filtering. Comparing the innovation residuals to white noise using ACF and Ljung–Box is a diagnostic, not proof that every underlying assumption is true. A model can have uncorrelated standardized innovations and still contain heteroscedasticity, structural breaks or nonlinear dependence. That is why diagnosis has several dimensions rather than a single p-value.

Key mathematical relation
\[u_t=\phi u_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\varepsilon_t=u_t-\phi u_{t-1}\]

8. ARIMA errors and lag-operator notation

The simplest dynamic regression assumes u_t follows AR(1), but the general formulation allows ARIMA(p,d,q) disturbances. Define the lag operator B by Bu_t=u_{t−1}. An AR polynomial φ(B)=1−φ_1B−···−φ_pB^p describes autoregression; an MA polynomial θ(B)=1+θ_1B+···+θ_qB^q describes dependence on innovations. Then φ(B)(1−B)^d u_t=θ(B)ε_t. The orders p, d and q describe the error process, not necessarily the original outcome Y_t by itself.

Substituting the regression disturbance gives φ(B)(1−B)^d[Y_t−β'x_t]=θ(B)ε_t. This identity prevents a serious conceptual mistake: a regression with ARIMA errors is not automatically the same thing as applying a plain ARIMA model to Y_t and adding predictors informally afterward. The lag operator acts on whatever residual series the regression coefficients define; the coefficients and error parameters generally need to be estimated jointly or iteratively.

For d=1, the operator transforms both sides of the deterministic regression relation. The differenced outcome ΔY_t=Y_t−Y_{t−1} depends on differenced covariates Δx_t plus the differenced disturbance. Thus a trend or calendar level effect can change its apparent mathematical form after differencing. If you difference only Y_t but leave the regressors in levels without justifying the model, you may change the scientific question. The guide focuses on a stationary AR(1) error case to keep the full numerical example transparent; seasonal and integrated errors are legitimate extensions with additional identification and initial-state challenges.

Key mathematical relation
\[\phi(B)(1-B)^d\bigl(Y_t-\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x_t\bigr)=\theta(B)\varepsilon_t\]

9. Lagged predictors: useful information or accidental leakage?

A lagged temperature x_{t−1} is known when forecasting one month ahead if that past temperature has already been published. This does not imply it is sufficient for a six-month forecast. For target Y_{t+h}, a proposed term T_{t+h−1} is unavailable whenever h is greater than one unless the forecasting origin already has a separate forecast for it. A column created with a database shift(1) is not automatically safe: safety depends on the target horizon and data-release lag, not the mere existence of a shift operation.

For example, at origin 30, T_{30} may be used to forecast Y_{31}; T_{31} may not be used to forecast Y_{32} at that same origin, because month 31 has not yet happened. If operational weather forecasts of T_{31} are available at the origin, their archived values may be used, but the actual realized T_{31} cannot be substituted during retrospective scoring. A six-step direct regression using t+6 as target requires every predictor to be available at t or forecast by a legitimate procedure.

Lagged demand terms raise another choice. A direct autoregressive regressor Y_{t−1} can overlap with the role of an AR error and create complex dynamic interpretation. This need not be invalid, but blindly adding many past Y's and an elaborate ARIMA residual can duplicate predictive structures, increase variance and complicate causal claims. A clear curriculum starts with the mean equation and the AR error separately, then considers extra lagged predictors only when the information set and a genuine predictive benefit are demonstrated on origin-respecting evaluation.

10. Calendar variables and what makes them safe

A calendar predictor is attractive because its future schedule often is known exactly. Examples include month-of-year indicators, day counts, a planned maintenance outage, the number of working days, a declared holiday or a contractual promotion date. But ask what is truly fixed. An announcement may be revised, a planned event may be canceled, and a schedule that was known only after the forecast origin should not be treated as preannounced. The synthetic indicator H_t is deliberately assumed fixed and known before month 30, including its value for the six evaluation months.

Calendar effects also need careful coding. If all twelve monthly indicators and an intercept are included, the columns sum to the intercept and the design matrix is singular. Use eleven indicators with a reference month or use a constrained encoding. If we add a trend plus several highly collinear seasonal indicators and a temperature variable, coefficients can have wide confidence intervals even when fitted values remain stable. This is a multicollinearity issue, not necessarily a reason to drop the calendar from forecasting. Examine stability, variance inflation, out-of-sample error and interpretation.

A calendar coefficient measures association after the other specified terms; the variable may proxy unmodeled temperature, demand composition or business hours. For the case H_t=1 in November and December, two months share the same dummy value, but they may have different temperatures and lagged errors. Their forecasts are not identical. The future value H=1 is a known input, whereas the future heat measure must be constructed from an information-safe forecast. Keeping these two kinds of predictors in separate columns is an explicit discipline worth carrying into applied code.

11. Spurious regression and time trends

Two growing series often have high sample correlation even if neither helps predict innovations in the other. This can happen when both share trends, persistent stochastic components or calendar patterns. The resulting t-statistics can look compelling while the relationship has no stable predictive meaning. Spurious regression is especially important when integrating ARIMA errors with external predictors: a flexible error model does not by itself repair a misspecified common trend, structural instability, or a wrongly interpreted long-run level relation.

A diagnostic thought experiment is to generate two independent random walks. Each is a cumulative sum of unrelated innovations, yet their level plots can drift together over a finite window. Regressing one level on the other using standard independent-error significance tests can yield misleading evidence. First inspect trend structure, residual stationarity, transformations, the role of differencing, and chronological validation. For a substantive long-run equilibrium claim, cointegration might be relevant, but that is explicitly outside Guide 71's technical scope.

Our synthetic construction includes the known upward trend 0.9t. We include t as a predictor instead of asking temperature to explain every increase in electricity consumption. Holding t out of the model could inflate the apparent relationship between seasonal heat and time, especially if the sample has unequal seasonal coverage. Conversely, including t does not magically identify causality; changing technology, price and population may still be omitted. An honest forecast note should state both model components and the possibility that future conditions break the historical relationships.

12. Selecting a regression error process

There are several reasonable candidates: regression with independent errors, regression with AR(1) errors, regression with ARMA errors, or a differenced ARIMA error if diagnostics and domain knowledge warrant one. Begin with a simple model, inspect the residual series from the regression mean, and estimate a parsimonious dependence structure. Fitting AR(1) to the disturbance says the unexpected deviation tends to persist or alternate according to φ; it does not give an additional change in temperature effect.

The coefficient φ has no energy units; it relates successive disturbances measured in GWh. If |φ|<1, the AR(1) disturbance has a stationary variance under suitable innovation assumptions. Its theoretical one-step conditional expectation is φu_t, and its contribution h steps ahead is φ^hu_t. If φ is negative, the sign of that contribution alternates by forecast horizon. That behavior can occur even when the conditional mean regression shows a smooth positive trend. In the example, a joint Gaussian maximum-likelihood fit yields φ̂≈−0.5752, a result of the fixed synthetic disturbance pattern rather than the positive 0.5 generator coefficient being guaranteed recovered in a short finite sample.

Model selection must also consider complexity. AIC or AICc can compare candidate likelihood specifications fitted to the same outcome and observations, but it is not a certificate of real-world forecasting superiority or predictor availability. A carefully chosen lag order can improve residual decorrelation while simultaneously worsening prediction in a new regime. Hold-out errors, stable coefficients, innovation diagnostics and a plausible availability story should inform the final choice. Refit only using information actually present at the declared forecast origin.

13. Parameter estimates and an interpretation audit

Using only months 1–30, fit Gaussian maximum likelihood with an explicit constant column and an AR(1) disturbance. The fitted conditional-mean coefficients are approximately 120.2476 GWh, 0.8943 GWh per month, 1.6167 GWh per excess °F, and 6.1191 GWh for the scheduled-calendar indicator. The disturbance parameter is φ̂≈−0.5752, while estimated innovation variance is approximately 0.7265 GWh². The likelihood-based AIC from the specified software configuration is about 87.96; this number is not comparable to a model using a different dependent-variable scale or a different sample definition.

It is essential to state the parameterization of a package. In statsmodels, SARIMAX with a constant supplied in exog and trend='n' makes the constant directly appear among the regression coefficients. Another configuration might instead use a trend intercept whose numerical meaning differs because it enters the AR error representation. We document the exact design matrix and software arguments rather than silently treating all printed constants as β_0.

For an interpretation exercise, compare two hypothetical months with equal t and H, but C values 8 and 9. The fitted conditional mean difference is 1.6167 GWh. If we compare different months without holding t fixed, there is also a trend component; if their calendar indicators differ, a third component enters. None of these counterfactual-looking differences is automatically causal. The coefficient is identified from the modeled joint association, and sample-specific validation is required before transferring it to a different utility, technology or climate range.

14. Constructing the six-month ex-ante forecast

At the end of month 30, generate exactly six rows of future regressors. Time indices are 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36. The temperature scenario is 81.5, 81.5, 70.5, 60.5, 51.5, 38.5°F; the associated cooling values are 21.5, 21.5, 10.5, 0.5, 0, 0. The calendar indicator equals 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1. 15. Benchmark models and a fair evaluation

A good dynamic regression must outperform a credible simpler option, not an artificially weak baseline. We evaluate three approaches on exactly the same six months. Seasonal Naive uses Y_{t−12} for the same month of the previous year and needs no future weather. Univariate AR(1) with time trend models demand from its own history without external predictors. Regression with AR(1) errors uses known calendar inputs and the predeclared temperature scenario. All models are estimated or defined at month 30, and none is allowed to learn the withheld outcomes.

The measured fixed-origin MAE values, expressed in GWh, are approximately 10.430 for Seasonal Naive, 12.245 for univariate AR(1) plus trend, and 1.109 for the dynamic regression. The corresponding RMSE values are approximately 10.479, 13.282 and 1.349 GWh. These are six realized absolute or squared errors, not estimates of a universal population ranking. The data were deliberately constructed with strong temperature and schedule effects, so including these predictors has a structural advantage. The ranking could reverse if the scenario inputs became inaccurate, the relationships changed, or a simpler seasonal model generalized better.

MAE is the mean of |Y−Ŷ|; RMSE is the square root of the mean squared error. RMSE penalizes a large missed month more strongly. If the application cares about peak capacity instead of typical demand, neither metric alone specifies the decision loss. Document the scoring unit, aggregation method, comparison period, forecast origin, and available predictors. A model that fits monthly means but misses dangerous extreme demand can be inadequate despite a low MAE. Ask which decision the forecast is supposed to support before choosing the metric.

16. Why fixed-origin and rolling-origin scores differ

A fixed-origin forecast issues all six predictions using only information through t=30. A rolling-origin one-step evaluation issues a new forecast at month 31 after observing month 31, at month 32 after observing 32, and so on. These protocols answer different questions. The latter can incorporate newly available outcomes, actual past temperatures, revised states and possibly refitted coefficients, but only after each relevant month has passed. It is not appropriate to compare rolling one-step errors from one model to fixed-origin six-step errors from another.

For an expanding-window assessment, choose origins 24, 25, ..., 30, train using all information available through each origin, and evaluate the same specified horizons. At origin 24, a horizon-six forecast targets month 30; at origin 30, it targets month 36. Weather scenarios must be reconstructed separately for each origin using historical vintages, not by simply grabbing weather from the finished dataset. If horizons or calibration windows overlap, forecast errors are statistically dependent; an average score does not yield a simple independent-sample confidence interval without additional assumptions.

Practical differences appear when a company issues monthly operating budgets six months ahead but also updates an internal dashboard every month. Both are valid tasks; each deserves its own protocol and benchmark. The fixed-origin example was chosen because it makes the information boundary explicit. A student can implement rolling evaluation later, but the record for every row must include origin date, target date, forecast horizon, model version and weather forecast vintage. Without those fields, reproducibility and leakage audits become much harder.

17. Conditional prediction intervals

A state-space forecast from get_forecast can provide a distribution or interval for future Y conditional on the particular future exogenous matrix supplied. With our fitted Gaussian AR(1) error model, the reported 95% intervals are approximately [180.570,183.917], [181.990,185.789], [164.636,168.556], [149.563,153.517], [155.643,159.607] and [156.599,160.565] GWh. The centers of these intervals are the six forecasts just calculated. Their widths are influenced by the assumed innovation variance and dynamics under the fitted specification.

These intervals are not a guarantee of 95% unconditional coverage in deployment. They treat each input temperature scenario as given, use a particular Gaussian error model, and in many standard outputs do not fully incorporate parameter-estimation uncertainty. They also assume a stable forecasting relationship and no unexpected operational or policy shocks. A realistic energy forecast might need to allow weather uncertainty, weather-model bias, changes in technology, and structural breaks. The nominal level describes a model-based conditional construction, not the empirically observed coverage of six cases.

For the fourth held-out month, the observed value 148.803 GWh lies below the model's fixed-scenario interval [149.563,153.517]. That is not a computational contradiction. An interval can miss observations; it is intended to describe long-run uncertainty under assumptions, not to contain every realized future month. To evaluate calibration, collect many genuinely out-of-time predictions at fixed horizons and count empirical coverage. Six held-out months are too few to validate a nominal 95% coverage rate meaningfully. Furthermore, forecast intervals for different horizons are correlated, so treating six hits or misses as independent would usually be unjustified.

18. The extra variance of an uncertain temperature forecast

Suppose the future temperature scenario is not a point truth but a random variable T^* with conditional variance σ_T² at a given horizon. When the cooling hinge is active and temperature uncertainty does not cross the 60°F threshold, the temperature contribution is approximately β_2(T^*−60). If β_2 is treated as fixed, a first-order variance contribution is β_2²σ_T². At β̂_2≈1.6167 GWh/°F and σ_T=2°F, this adds about (1.6167×2)²≈10.46 GWh² to the uncertainty. This is often far larger than the innovation variance estimate 0.7265 GWh² in the synthetic fitted model.

A simplified illustrative uncertainty calculation could combine an independent response-error component with the predictor term: Var(Y_future|origin)≈Var(dynamic error|origin)+β_2²Var(T_future|origin). If the two errors are correlated, include twice their covariance. If temperature may cross 60°F, the hinge is nonlinear and the linear approximation is inaccurate; generate scenarios through the full max(0,T−60) function or integrate its distribution. When β̂_2 is itself uncertain, parameter covariance contributes still more terms. Do not simply add standard deviations; variance components are the quantities that add under independence.

A practical scenario table might report cool, central and hot forecasts using central temperature minus 2°F, central temperature, and central temperature plus 2°F. These are sensitivity scenarios, not automatically 95% bounds. For a month well above 60°F, the difference in conditional mean between hot and cool scenarios is about 4×1.6167=6.467 GWh. Whether an actual 95% predictive interval should span more or less depends on the distribution of temperature forecasts, dependence structure, parameter uncertainty and operational shifts. Clearly label any interval omitting uncertainty in x.

Key mathematical relation
\[\operatorname{Var}(Y_{\mathrm{future}}\mid\mathcal I)\approx V_{\mathrm{error}}+\beta_2^2V_T\quad\text{(under independence and local linearity)}\]

19. Uncertainty near the cooling threshold

The hinge C=max(0,T−60) creates an important nonlinearity: the expected cooling contribution at a random future temperature generally differs from the contribution evaluated at expected temperature. Mathematically, E[max(0,T−60)] is not usually equal to max(0,E[T]−60). When T is sometimes below and sometimes above 60°F, the mean of the positive excess depends on the whole temperature distribution, not merely its center. This is a direct consequence of the curvature of the positive-part function.

Construct a two-point example: next month's average T equals 58°F with probability one half and 62°F with probability one half. Its mean is 60°F. Inserting the mean into the hinge gives max(0,60−60)=0. But the average actual hinge is 0.5×0+0.5×2=1°F. With β_2≈1.6167 GWh per excess degree, the expected cooling component is about 1.6167 GWh, whereas the plug-in mean-scenario component is zero. This is not a large-sample artifact; it is an exact result under the declared two-point forecast distribution.

One implication is that scenario averages must be combined after applying nonlinear transformations. For each weather scenario, compute C, calculate the dynamic regression forecast, then average forecasts with scenario probabilities. Replacing temperature by its weighted mean before transforming can systematically understate cooling demand near the threshold. Scenario probabilities themselves require validation, and extreme heat can cause operational changes not captured by the fitted piecewise linear relationship. Students should distinguish a mathematical calculation conditional on a scenario from real-world probability calibration.

Key mathematical relation
\[\mathbb E[\max(0,T-60)]\ne\max(0,\mathbb E[T])\ \text{in general}\]

20. Innovation diagnostics and what their p-values mean

After fitting dynamic regression, inspect standardized one-step-ahead innovation residuals. Look for a nonzero mean, residual ACF peaks, changing spread over time, outliers, heavy tails and structural changes. A Ljung–Box test aggregates sample autocorrelations through a declared number of lags. Its null hypothesis is compatible with zero linear autocorrelations over those lags, under its test assumptions. A large p-value is not positive proof of independence or a correct model, and a small p-value is evidence that some chosen structure remains unmodeled—not a unique instruction about which parameter to add.

Lag choice matters. Testing twelve lags with only thirty monthly training observations uses a substantial fraction of the sample; finite-sample approximations and degrees-of-freedom adjustments may be weak. Report the number of tested lags and how fitted ARMA parameters were accounted for. Compare the regression residual ACF with the filtered innovation ACF: the former can show dependence precisely because the model assumes it; the latter is the primary target for approximate white noise.

Prediction diagnostics go further than p-values. Plot predicted versus observed demand in the held-out months, and compare absolute errors by season or thermal regime. The fourth month has a larger conditional forecast miss than its fitted interval anticipates. This may be an ordinary random fluctuation, scenario-input error or model misspecification; a single miss cannot tell which. Document possible explanations and test them using new data and preregistered diagnostics, rather than adjusting the model solely to explain a known test-set error.

21. Time-dependent collinearity and conditional coefficients

Consider three predictors: t, a rising annual temperature series, and a year indicator. Even when none is a perfect linear combination of the others, they may describe similar changes in the sample. The consequence is imprecise individual coefficients, unstable signs, and difficulty interpreting incremental effects. A model can predict adequately because correlated variables carry overlapping signal, yet be unsuitable for assigning separate policy or causal importance to each variable.

Seasonality creates a related issue. The cooling variable already encodes a repeating warm-season pattern. If we add twelve monthly dummies, a Fourier basis, lag-12 demand, temperature and seasonal AR errors, we risk an overly complicated model for thirty training observations. The correct choice is not dictated by one universal formula. Compare a small set of interpretable candidate specifications using the same calendar origins and forecast information. Ask whether each additional component captures variation not already represented by the others.

When a coefficient changes after adding a predictor, the change can reflect adjustment for a common pattern, genuine interaction, multicollinearity, or different estimation weighting under correlated disturbances. It does not by itself imply that the original coefficient was biased by a causal confounder. Students should inspect units, parameter covariance, stability across training windows and forecasts under realistic scenarios. If parameter interpretation is crucial, plan stronger identification and richer data collection rather than relying on a single selected regression.

22. Data leakage audit: four distinct routes

First, direct target leakage occurs when a feature contains a future measured value of Y or a quantity computed from it. Second, predictor leakage occurs when realized future weather replaces the archived weather forecast issued at the origin. Third, preprocessing leakage appears when scaling, imputation, feature selection, seasonal decomposition or hyperparameter tuning is learned using the entire series before splitting it. Fourth, revision leakage occurs when final corrected historical values replace the preliminary versions the forecaster actually possessed.

A practical audit asks, for each input column and transformation: Which raw timestamp did it use? When was that raw value released? When was the transformation fitted? Was it refitted for each forecasting origin? Where is the archived forecast vintage stored? Was the test period consulted to choose the model, an imputation rule or the winner? A procedure can use legal variables and still leak because the computation fitted their normalizing mean on future months.

For this case the temperature scenario T^* uses T from twelve months earlier, which is known at origin 30. The model parameters are estimated on months 1–30 only, and the six reserved values of Y appear solely in the evaluation table after forecast creation. The synthetic recipe defines the full sample for auditing, but our estimation and feature construction deliberately respect the origin. This distinction between knowing the simulation design as authors and using forbidden future observations as forecasters should be explicit in teaching.

23. A full forecasting workflow that can be audited

A defensible production workflow begins before fitting software. Write a forecast specification stating the target variable Y, its units, index frequency, horizons, intended decision, loss metric and expected availability of every feature. Archive the origin and data vintages. Check missing months, duplicate records, daylight or billing-calendar anomalies, revisions, and structural breaks. Document the physical meaning and permissible range of each predictor. State whether calendar schedules are fixed and whether temperature values are measurements, operational forecasts or externally imposed scenarios.

Second, create the temporal partitions before feature engineering. Fit all transformations using historical training rows, and build lagged variables with correct release delays. Fit a small set of mean equations; inspect their residuals. Try a parsimonious ARIMA structure for errors; evaluate one-step innovations and identify obvious defects. Do not let an AIC scan over hundreds of specifications replace domain judgment, particularly with a short sample. Use the same fixed origin and ex-ante weather scenario for all candidate forecasts and compare interpretable baselines. A report of numerical scores should identify the exact window and weighting scheme.

Third, communicate conditional forecasts and scenario sensitivity separately. Give the point estimates, conditional intervals, weather-source assumption and whether parameter and weather uncertainty were propagated. Explain any missed coverage or unstable coefficient without retroactively changing the benchmark. A single successful synthetic backtest is the beginning of model evaluation, not the end. Deployment requires recalibration, data-quality monitoring, drift checks and an explicit owner who can override the system if inputs are unavailable or operating conditions change. A good forecasting report makes these operational uncertainties visible to nontechnical decision makers.

24. How to communicate forecasting scenarios without causal claims

A scenario forecast answers a conditional operational question: If future temperatures follow a specified path, what demand would this fitted model predict? A causal policy question asks what would happen if an intervention changed a variable while other causal mechanisms unfolded. Observational dynamic regression generally does not identify that intervention effect. Temperature is not directly controllable by the utility, and a price intervention could alter consumer behavior, scheduling and error dynamics simultaneously. Predictions conditioned on exogenous-looking columns are not experiments.

A useful executive presentation includes the issued forecast date, what was known, the central weather trajectory, a few physically coherent alternatives, prediction intervals conditioned on each path, and a short explanation of omitted uncertainty. If the weather supplier provides scenario probabilities, combine outcomes appropriately and validate those probabilities. If it provides only point temperatures, label cool and hot cases as sensitivity analyses without attaching arbitrary confidence levels.

For the utility, a forecast can support fuel scheduling, reserve procurement or maintenance coordination. It should not be used alone to claim the calendar event increased demand by exactly 6.119 GWh or to guarantee that the capacity budget will never be exceeded. The model was trained on synthetic data; even a real dataset would require external validation. Academic honesty in the final section is as important as arithmetic: a student's final report should describe what the model learned, what information it used, where its uncertainty comes from and which decisions remain unsupported.

25. Exercise 1: Reconstruct the first observation

Problem. With t=1, T=35°F, H=0, innovation z_1=1 and u_0=0, reconstruct the cooling variable, disturbance and demand. Explain all units.

Complete solution. First calculate C=max(0,35−60)=0°F excess. Next u_1=0.5×0+1=1 GWh. Insert the declared coefficients: Y_1=120 GWh+0.9 GWh+1.6 GWh/°F×0°F+6 GWh×0+1 GWh=121.9 GWh. The product of slope and excess temperature has GWh units; the trend coefficient is GWh per elapsed month, so multiplying by t=1 month returns GWh. The numerical answer follows from a synthetic identity and does not estimate real consumption. If units did not cancel, the calculation would signal a model-definition error.

26. Exercise 2: Separate a disturbance and an innovation

Problem. The synthetic disturbances in months 1 and 2 are 1 and −1.5 GWh. The error recurrence has φ=0.5. Find the second innovation and interpret its difference from u_2.

Complete solution. Start with u_2=φu_1+z_2. Rearrange: z_2=u_2−φu_1=−1.5−0.5(1)=−2 GWh. The conditional predicted disturbance is 0.5 GWh, but the observed disturbance is −1.5; the new shock is the −2 GWh difference. A residual from the regression mean describes the total −1.5 GWh deviation; the innovation describes only what the previous disturbance did not predict. Their equality is not expected in a dependent error model.

27. Exercise 3: Build the ex-ante predictor matrix

Problem. At origin 30, use the previous-year-plus-0.5 rule for months 31–36. Identify the cooling and calendar columns, and say why the realized temperature column is forbidden in the operational backtest.

Complete solution. The temperature vector is (81.5,81.5,70.5,60.5,51.5,38.5)°F, calculated from months 19–24. Apply C=max(0,T−60) entry by entry: (21.5,21.5,10.5,0.5,0,0). The calendar vector is (0,0,0,0,1,1), because target months 35–36 correspond to scheduled winter months. Each future design row is (1,t,C,H); there are six rows and four regression columns, including the constant. Actual temperatures in months 31–36 are unavailable at origin 30, even though they appear in the completed educational dataset. Replacing the scenario would leak future information.

28. Exercise 4: Interpret conditional coefficients

Problem. Using the fitted slope 1.6167 GWh per excess °F, compare C=8 with C=11 while t and H stay equal. Contrast this with a forecast from one month to the next.

Complete solution. At fixed t and H, the fitted mean change is 1.6167(11−8)=4.8501 GWh. If t rises by one month, the trend adds about 0.8943 GWh; if H also changes from zero to one, add about 6.1191 GWh. Thus two actual months generally cannot be compared by multiplying only the temperature difference. Moreover, these are modeled associations at specified covariates; a physical intervention requires stronger causal identification. Check units: temperature slope in GWh/°F times 3°F equals GWh.

29. Exercise 5: Compute a forecast error

Problem. The first six-month fixed-origin prediction is 182.2056 GWh and the actual Y_31 is 181.1203 GWh. Calculate signed error, absolute error and squared error.

Complete solution. The signed error defined as observed minus forecast equals 181.1203−182.2056=−1.0853 GWh. Absolute error is 1.0853 GWh. Squaring the unrounded difference gives approximately 1.178 GWh². The negative sign indicates overforecasting under our convention; another source may define error as forecast minus actual, reversing only its sign. MAE uses absolute error and RMSE uses squared error, so sign convention does not alter those magnitudes. One error cannot establish average predictive superiority.

30. Exercise 6: Reconstruct MAE of the dynamic model

Problem. The absolute six-month forecast errors, rounded, are 1.085, 0.717, 0.456, 2.759, 0.992 and 0.644 GWh. Compute their average and comment on rounding.

Complete solution. Their rounded sum is 1.085+0.717+0.456+2.759+0.992+0.644=6.653 GWh. Divide by six to obtain about 1.1088 GWh. With unrounded model outputs, the independently calculated MAE is approximately 1.10894 GWh. This difference is entirely due to displaying only three decimal places. The unit remains GWh because absolute errors are expressed in that unit and dividing by six is dimensionless. Report numerical reproducibility within the declared tolerance rather than claim equality after aggressive rounding.

31. Exercise 7: Compare a genuine benchmark

Problem. Seasonal Naive has MAE≈10.430 GWh; dynamic regression MAE≈1.109 GWh on the same six months. Calculate the absolute and relative sample improvement, then state what cannot be concluded.

Complete solution. The difference in MAE is 10.430−1.109=9.321 GWh. Relative to Seasonal Naive, (10.430−1.109)/10.430≈0.894, or roughly an 89.4% reduction in this synthetic holdout. Such a percentage can be impressive while still being fragile: the generator deliberately used external temperature and schedule effects, there are only six observations, and weather scenarios might be worse in practice. No universal 89.4% improvement or statistical significance follows without more independent forecast origins and relevant uncertainty analysis.

32. Exercise 8: Propagate temperature uncertainty

Problem. Assume the future temperature prediction has standard deviation 2°F and the fitted cooling slope is 1.6167 GWh/°F. For a month safely above 60°F, calculate the added variance and standard deviation, assuming independence from the dynamic error.

Complete solution. The temperature standard deviation is multiplied by the slope: 1.6167×2=3.2334 GWh. The added variance is 3.2334²≈10.455 GWh². If an independent response-error variance were 0.7265 GWh², the illustrative combined variance would be 11.182 GWh² and standard deviation sqrt(11.182)≈3.344 GWh. One must not add 3.2334 and sqrt(0.7265) as though standard deviations were variances. This simple propagation ignores threshold crossing, parameter covariance, forecast-bias uncertainty and any correlation between weather and demand innovations.

Key mathematical relation
\[V_{T\rightarrow Y}=(1.6167)^2(2)^2\approx10.455\ \mathrm{GWh}^2\]

33. Exercise 9: Nonlinear scenario averaging

Problem. Future T equals 58°F or 62°F with probability 0.5 each. Find expected cooling degrees and compare with cooling degrees computed at expected T.

Complete solution. The expected T is 0.5×58+0.5×62=60°F. Direct substitution into C=max(0,T−60) gives C(60)=0. But the scenario-specific values are C(58)=0 and C(62)=2°F, so E[C]=0.5×0+0.5×2=1°F. The expected modeled cooling demand is 1.6167 GWh, not zero. The operations do not commute because the hinge is nonlinear. The correct scenario approach transforms each scenario first, computes the forecast under each, and then averages according to probabilities. These probabilities are assumed, not validated weather forecasts.

34. Exercise 10: Recognize an illegitimate rolling predictor

Problem. At t=30, a colleague proposes predicting Y_36 using actual T_35, because it is merely lagged one period relative to Y_36. Is the procedure valid? Correct the protocol.

Complete solution. No. The target horizon is six: month 35 has not yet occurred at origin 30. Lagged relative to the target does not mean available relative to the forecast origin. A valid fixed-origin protocol supplies a genuine forecast for T_35 issued by t=30, uses only temperatures through t=30, or designs an alternate model requiring no unavailable weather. A different valid rolling protocol could forecast month 36 at origin 35 after T_35 becomes available; but its horizon is one month and its errors must not be mixed with those of the original six-step experiment.

35. Exercise 11: Differentiate an ARIMA-error regression

Problem. Suppose Y_t=β_0+β_1X_t+u_t and u_t is integrated of order one with an AR(1) difference. Apply Δ=1−B, explaining why a constant in the level equation disappears.

Complete solution. Subtract the previous-period equation: Y_t−Y_{t−1}=(β_0−β_0)+β_1(X_t−X_{t−1})+(u_t−u_{t−1}). Thus ΔY_t=β_1ΔX_t+Δu_t. If (1−φB)Δu_t=ε_t, the differenced regression inherits an AR(1) error. The intercept cancels because the original β_0 is time invariant; a drift in the differenced equation is a separate specification, not an algebraically unchanged copy of β_0. The lesson is why differencing outcomes and covariates coherently matters when interpreting ARIMA-error models.

Key mathematical relation
\[\Delta Y_t=\beta_1\Delta X_t+\Delta u_t\]

36. Exercise 12: Diagnose a suspiciously strong trend fit

Problem. Two independent economic indices increase for ten years; a levels regression has R²=0.95. Can you assert a causal relation? Specify a disciplined diagnostic plan.

Complete solution. No. Shared trend can inflate R² and classical t-statistics even with independent underlying innovations. Examine the plots and measurement definitions, test or otherwise assess nonstationary behavior with appropriate caution, inspect the fitted regression disturbance, compare justified differenced or detrended specifications, and evaluate forecasting on genuinely future dates. If the goal is a long-run level relation, consider cointegration as a separately justified advanced analysis; do not claim it from high R². For causal claims, a credible identification strategy beyond observational correlation would also be required.

37. Exercise 13: Check conditional interval coverage

Problem. The forecast interval for month 34 is [149.563,153.517] GWh but actual demand is 148.803 GWh. Decide whether this contradicts a claimed 95% model-based interval and explain what would constitute a meaningful calibration assessment.

Complete solution. It lies outside because 148.803<149.563, but one miss does not logically refute a nominal 95% interval. Even under an ideal calibrated mechanism, some realizations should fall outside. We would need many forecasts prepared before their targets, separated by horizon and predictor scenario, and then compare empirical coverage to nominal rates with uncertainty accounting for overlapping origins and dependence. We must also distinguish miscoverage caused by weather-scenario error from response-innovation variability. Calling the interval unconditional 95% coverage would overstate what the current calculation does.

38. Exercise 14: Write the final forecast decision memo

Problem. A manager wants to accept the regression AR(1) model because its six-month MAE is lower than both univariate alternatives. Write a decision that addresses predictor availability, scenario uncertainty, backtesting evidence, causal limits and monitoring. Your answer must not promise future superiority.

Complete solution. Decision: Retain the regression-with-AR(1)-errors specification as a promising candidate for supervised operational pilot, not as a definitively superior deployed model. Its synthetic fixed-origin MAE of about 1.109 GWh is well below Seasonal Naive at 10.430 GWh and univariate AR(1) plus trend at 12.245 GWh on the same six observations. The input matrix is reproducible at origin 30 because future temperatures come from a declared prior-year scenario and calendar dates are known. Nevertheless, six outcomes are too few for a stable ranking, and the conditional intervals omit uncertainty in the future weather forecast and possibly parameter estimation. Before deployment, obtain archived real weather forecasts at each historical origin, test many origins and horizons, assess extreme-load costs and interval coverage, monitor innovations and drift, and define a fallback if external inputs fail. Do not describe the thermal or calendar coefficients as proven causal effects. This recommendation is bounded by the explicitly synthetic data and does not validate the method for a real utility.

39. Final synthesis: explain what works, and why

A sound answer to the Guide 71 learning test should reconstruct the complete chain rather than merely report a preferred model. Begin with a forecast origin and target; identify input availability; distinguish the conditional mean regression from the dependent disturbance; identify whether an innovation is new information; estimate only on the training segment; forecast legitimate future regressors; issue fixed-origin predictions; compare with baselines on the same targets; and communicate conditional and unconditional sources of uncertainty separately.

The decisive technical insight is that external predictors are useful only when their future values are available or forecastable, and that the forecast performance of the combined system depends on both the target model and the predictor forecasting procedure. A conditional model can be excellent with observed historical temperatures yet unreliable when future temperature forecasts are biased. Another insight is that a regression coefficient can capture association without establishing an intervention effect, especially in trending or highly seasonal data. Even beautifully whitened ARIMA innovations do not automatically solve this identification problem.

To verify genuine comprehension, a student should now explain in their own words why the held-out weather cannot enter the design matrix at origin 30, reproduce a forecast error, reconstruct an uncertainty scenario, explain why residuals differ from innovations, and defend the final recommendation while admitting its limits. A successful answer requires all five, not merely memorizing the SARIMAX acronym. These lessons provide a foundation for richer forecasting systems while leaving cointegration, VAR and sophisticated joint weather-demand uncertainty models explicitly for advanced study.

40. Model comparison beyond a single favorable score

A ranking based on one six-month block is sensitive to the particular weather pattern, shocks and calendar composition of those months. A more informative plan evaluates multiple past origins without peeking ahead, records a score at each horizon, and compares candidate predictions pairwise for the same target observations. A paired comparison is preferable to comparing means computed over different periods; otherwise the apparent advantage may reflect an easier season or a different mix of hot days. If scores are summarized over overlapping horizons, account for dependent forecast errors rather than applying an independent-observations standard error.

Model complexity deserves a separate audit. An explanatory time trend may extrapolate plausibly for a few months yet become absurd in the long run. A cooling hinge at 60°F can be more interpretable than an unrestricted polynomial but also introduces a threshold that may have been selected using the same data. A seasonal AR component can capture omitted annual dependence, but it might duplicate strong calendar features in a very short series. A good comparison therefore reports sample size, number of fitted coefficients, predictor-vintage rule, weather-scenario method and computational stability in addition to prediction errors.

For our synthetic exercise, the dynamic model's large six-month advantage is evidence of internal consistency with the generator's constructed relationships, not independent confirmation that AR(1) is optimal for all electricity markets. Because the full data-generating mechanism is known to the educational author, it is tempting to optimize around it. In applied data this would correspond to using future knowledge. Keep the model-building rationale separate from the holdout results, and regard a single demonstration as a reproducible lesson, not a validation study.

41. Units, transformations and interpretation under scaling

Suppose the utility reports demand in megawatt-hours instead of gigawatt-hours. Since 1 GWh=1,000 MWh, every demand value and regression coefficient measured in demand units must be multiplied by 1,000. The fitted temperature slope becomes approximately 1,616.7 MWh per excess °F, not 1.6167 MWh/°F. The dimensionless AR parameter φ remains unchanged, while the innovation variance is multiplied by 1,000². Numerical model output may look different while describing exactly the same physical relationship. Tracking units is therefore a mathematical verification tool, not just editorial polish.

For temperature, the choice of Fahrenheit also matters. One degree Celsius equals 1.8 degrees Fahrenheit as a difference. If we define a comparable cooling predictor measured in degrees Celsius above the equivalent threshold, the numerical slope in GWh per °C should be 1.8 times the slope per °F. But the threshold must be converted consistently: 60°F corresponds to about 15.556°C. Changing the slope unit without changing the threshold corrupts the definition of the predictor. Similarly, month number t must represent elapsed calendar months, not arbitrary row positions after filtering missing records.

When reporting a forecast, distinguish levels and changes. An additional 6 GWh of expected monthly consumption differs from a 6% increase: the latter depends on a reference demand denominator. For instance, 6 GWh on a 150 GWh baseline is 4%, not 6%. Such conversions should not hide uncertainty; if the denominator is forecast rather than known, its own uncertainty can enter a ratio. Numerical answers should always state whether a quantity is an observation, a fitted mean, a future prediction or an uncertainty component.

42. Why an apparent external predictor may not be exogenous

A time-series regressor is called external because it is supplied as an additional column. In probability and econometrics, exogeneity has stronger meanings involving conditional expectations, feedback, and the relation between regressors and errors. An operational schedule might respond to demand expectations, making H_t correlated with unobserved anticipated conditions. A retail promotion may be timed because managers already expect high sales. Treating such a schedule as a causal explanatory variable would risk reverse causation or omitted-variable bias.

Temperature is physically outside the utility's control, but even here a reported average may be measured or aggregated differently across regions, and unobserved seasonal variables may correlate with it. An external forecast may also be constructed from the target's history through a forecasting pipeline, creating a less obvious feedback route. For prediction this can still be useful if the features are legitimate at forecast time, but causal interpretation and classical coefficient standard errors require separate assumptions.

A responsible analyst separates three claims. First, availability: the variable can be constructed when a decision is made. Second, predictive relevance: it improves held-out forecast performance under a correctly executed protocol. Third, causal identification: interventions on that variable would have a specified effect. Our lesson directly checks the first two within the synthetic setting, and explicitly does not establish the third. An excellent forecast with an available predictor can be practically valuable without providing a causal coefficient, whereas a well-identified causal study may not automatically give the best multistep forecasts.

43. Reproducibility and the independent numerical check

The complete numerical example is intentionally compatible with ordinary NumPy and statsmodels. Build the 36 monthly temperatures from the twelve-month reference pattern and three offsets. Generate the innovations from the declared fixed sequence and accumulate the AR disturbance recursively. Compute cooling excess and the winter schedule indicator, then calculate Y from the stated generator. Confirm the resulting first row and selected later rows against the HTML table. Save the full generated vectors in a portable CSV with explicit integer month indices.

Fit OLS to the first thirty rows with columns (1,t,C,H), then fit SARIMAX(endog=train_Y, exog=train_X, order=(1,0,0), trend='n') with the same constant present in train_X. Supply six future rows based on time, the scenario temperatures and the scheduled calendar indicator; do not include future observed Y or realized weather. Independently compute fixed-origin errors and benchmark scores from predictions saved before the held-out values are inspected. The accompanying verification script recomputes and asserts the central quantities to numerical tolerance.

Reproducibility does not equal robustness. A script can perfectly reproduce a synthetic model that performs poorly on real data. Conversely, software packages may parameterize intercepts and initial states differently, yielding different printed estimates for related models. Declare package version, exact model arguments, optimizer settings, rounding method, forecast horizon and data-vintage construction. Student-facing code should always follow its explanatory mathematics instead of substituting an opaque command for the reasoning. All computations here are transparent educational checks, not claims about a live utility's forecast service.
