01. Why stationarity is a modeling question, not a visual label

An economic index can rise during several consecutive months because of sustained growth, a seasonal surge, or an accumulation of unpredictable shocks. These possibilities may produce superficially similar lines on a chart but have different implications for forecasts and uncertainty. Guide 63 separated trend, seasonality and remainder. Here we study a different question: do the probability rules producing the series remain stable as the calendar advances? A series whose mean, dispersion and time-dependence structure remain stable is a useful starting point for many time-series models. Calling it stationary is a claim about a stochastic process—not simply a description of a short observed line.

Why should we care? Imagine using the previous year's mean as an estimate of activity next month. If the underlying expectation rises every month, the old average underestimates the next observation. Imagine using a single residual variance to construct forecast intervals. If the spread expands with the level, the same interval width may be too narrow in later months. Finally, if shocks accumulate permanently, a value far above its historical average need not be pulled back toward that average. These failures arise from different mechanisms; no one transformation cures them automatically.

The goal of this guide is diagnostic reasoning followed by reversible arithmetic: inspect what changes over time; distinguish deterministic trends from stochastic trends; consider variance transformations; compute ordinary and seasonal differences; retain the information required to reconstruct the original scale; identify over-differencing; and interpret Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) and Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin (KPSS) tests with their opposing null hypotheses. Every worked dataset in this guide is synthetic, not an official economic statistic. We will not describe a simulated pattern as evidence about an actual economy.

02. Prerequisites, time index, units and learning contract

You need algebra, means and variances from introductory statistics, the idea of a time index from Guide 62, a basic regression slope from Guide 51, and the trend/seasonality distinction from Guide 63. We explain additional concepts—random processes, covariance, unit roots and test specifications—as they appear. No knowledge of ARIMA estimation is assumed; we are preparing the conceptual foundation for it, not fitting a full forecasting system.

Let \(t\) label consecutive months, and let \(Y_t\) be an index of economic activity measured in index points, not dollars and not percentages. Our main example has \(t=1,\ldots,24\). Subscripts are dates: \(Y_{13}\) follows \(Y_{12}\) by one month. Write \(E(Y_t)\) for the population expectation of the random value at time \(t\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)\) for its variance in squared index points, and \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-h})\) for covariance at separation \(h\) months. The observed 24 numbers are one realization of a possible random process, not 24 independent samples from an unchanging distribution.

A difference operator \(\Delta\) means subtracting a previous observation: \(\Delta Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}\). A seasonal operator \(\Delta_{12}\) means subtracting the corresponding month one year earlier: \(\Delta_{12}Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-12}\). A logarithm \(\log Y_t\) means the natural logarithm, only defined as a real value for \(Y_t>0\). When we later mention a lag it is a past observation; a lead is future information. A method that needs a future lead is not automatically available at the historical forecast origin.

At every calculation ask four questions: What is measured? What assumption licenses the operation? Which observations are lost or retained? How do we interpret and reverse the result? These questions make transformation an intelligible decision rather than a blind software command.

03. Stationarity is about distributions across possible histories

A stochastic process is a family of random variables indexed by time. For each calendar position \(t\), many possible values could occur under the assumed data-generating mechanism. An observed series is only one path through that family. Stationarity concerns how the probability structure behaves across possible paths and times. You cannot prove it merely because the observed line fluctuates around a roughly horizontal level for 24 months.

Strict stationarity says that the joint distribution of any finite collection \((Y_{t_1},\ldots,Y_{t_k})\) is unchanged when all times are shifted by the same number of periods, where those times are defined. It is a demanding condition. For many introductory regression and time-series arguments we instead use weak or covariance stationarity. That requires a constant finite mean, constant finite variance and covariance depending only on the lag \(h\), not the calendar date. Weak stationarity does not automatically imply strict stationarity, and strict stationarity does not guarantee finite moments.

For example, a process \(Y_t=50+\varepsilon_t\) with independent, identically distributed zero-mean errors of variance 4 is weakly stationary: \(E(Y_t)=50\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=4\), and covariance is zero for every nonzero lag. If the errors have the same distribution at every date, it is also strictly stationary. This is a model result under stated assumptions, not something established by observing three consecutive values like 49, 51 and 48. A stable mean is not a claim that every observation is equal to the mean.

\[(Y_{t_1},\ldots,Y_{t_k})\overset d=(Y_{t_1+h},\ldots,Y_{t_k+h})\]

04. The three weak-stationarity conditions, one at a time

First, \(E(Y_t)=\mu\) for all \(t\): the process expectation must not drift with the calendar. Here \(\mu\) is a constant in index points. A sequence with \(E(Y_t)=100+2t\) fails this condition even if deviations around that rising line have a stable spread. Second, \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=\sigma^2<\infty\) for all \(t\): dispersion must not expand, contract or become undefined as time changes. Third, \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-h})=\gamma(h)\) must be a function of lag \(h\) only. Two adjacent months in 2027 and two adjacent months in 2037 must have the same covariance under the assumed process, although their realized values differ.

The units help catch mistakes. The mean \(\mu\) is measured in index points; variance \(\sigma^2\) and covariance \(\gamma(h)\) are in squared index points; the autocorrelation \(\rho(h)=\gamma(h)/\gamma(0)\) is unitless when variance is positive. A stationary process may have substantial persistence: an AR(1) process with coefficient 0.8 and stable innovations can be weakly stationary when its other assumptions hold. Thus stationarity is not independence. Independence can simplify a model, but is not required by the definition.

What would violate these conditions? An economy with a deterministic upward mean violates the first. A process whose shocks increase in variance every quarter violates the second. A stable mean and variance can coexist with covariance that changes after a policy change, violating the third. Plot, domain knowledge and diagnostics should investigate all three, not merely whether a line slopes upward.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[E(Y_t)=\mu,\quad\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=\sigma^2,\quad\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-h})=\gamma(h)\]

05. Main case: 24 months of a synthetic activity index

Consider a fictional monthly activity index for a regional service sector. We deliberately construct the 24 observations so students can verify trend, seasonality and disturbance without pretending these components are known in real data. Let the generating equation be \(Y_t=100+2t+S_{m(t)}+R_t\). The deterministic component increases by two index points per month. The twelve seasonal offsets for January through December are \((-6,-3,0,3,6,9,6,3,0,-3,-6,-9)\); they sum to zero. The residual pattern in the first year is \((1,-1,0,2,-2,1,-1,0,2,-2,1,-1)\) and the second-year residual vector is \((-1,-1,0,-2,2,-1,1,0,-2,2,-1,1)\). The residuals are fixed to make reconstruction possible; they are not presented as a statistically estimated stochastic noise process.

For month 1, \(Y_1=100+2(1)-6+1=97\) index points. For month 8, \(Y_8=100+16+3+0=119\). For month 13, \(Y_{13}=100+26-6-1=119\). Those equal observed values at months 8 and 13 do not imply a stable expected level: the two dates have different trend and season components. Indeed, the generating deterministic mean rises from 102 in month 1 to 148 in month 24, before adding seasonality.

The case is intentionally short. Twenty-four points can teach arithmetic, but cannot establish long-run stationarity or the reliability of seasonal patterns in a real economy. Dates are consecutive and have no missing periods; values are in the same units. A separate random-walk example later isolates stochastic-trend reasoning rather than treating these fixed remainders as independently sampled errors.

06. Read the graph without assuming a test result

The observed path exhibits an upward baseline and repeating peaks and troughs. The known deterministic trend \(100+2t\) is included on the diagram solely because this is a teaching construction. In real economic data a researcher cannot identify an exact population trend from a short plot. The plotted series does not resemble a constant-mean process as generated: the mean component changes systematically with calendar position and the recurring seasonal effect changes month-to-month expectations.

Try three verbal descriptions. “The index is higher in the second year” describes the realized data. “The generating conditional mean includes a time trend and seasonality” describes the synthetic model, which we know because we built it. “The economy has a unit root” would be an unsupported statistical conclusion: a deterministic increase and a stochastic accumulation of shocks can both create persistent levels. The correct next step is to specify candidate mechanisms and investigate them, not to assign a unit-root label based on a rising line.

Always inspect measurement changes first. A statistical break might be due to a revised index base, new industries in the survey, a discontinued outlet or a changed seasonal-adjustment method. Differencing a measurement break spreads its impact to nearby changes and may conceal the cause. Transformations are not substitutes for data provenance. The graph is a diagnostic resource, not a proof of all three stationarity conditions.

07. Deterministic trend and trend stationarity

Consider the stochastic model \(Y_t=100+2t+\varepsilon_t\), where the errors \(\varepsilon_t\) form a mean-zero, finite-variance stationary process. This differs from our main constructed path because the errors are now random under explicit assumptions. The expected level is \(E(Y_t)=100+2t\), so the observed \(Y_t\) is not weakly stationary. However, the detrended process \(Z_t=Y_t-(100+2t)=\varepsilon_t\) is stationary under the stated model. We call this a trend-stationary process: a stable stochastic component surrounding a deterministic time function.

Subtracting \(100+2t\) is permitted in our hypothetical known-generating model. In applied work those coefficients must be estimated, often by regression on time, and residual diagnostics must follow. Estimated detrending uses information from the training sample; fitting a trend using the entire time series and then evaluating forecasts in past periods would leak future information into earlier predictions. A forecast should estimate the trend with observations available up to its origin and extrapolate under an explicitly defended assumption.

Detrending and differencing are not identical. First differencing gives \(\Delta Y_t=2+(\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_{t-1})\): the linear slope becomes a constant increment, but the error process changes from \(\varepsilon_t\) to a difference of errors. If the original errors were white noise, differencing them unnecessarily introduces negative lag-one autocorrelation. The operation may achieve constant mean yet distort otherwise simple short-run dynamics. Therefore ask whether a deterministic trend model already describes the process adequately before differencing indiscriminately.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[Y_t=a+bt+\varepsilon_t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Y_t-(a+bt)=\varepsilon_t\]

08. Stochastic trend and the random walk

A random walk follows \(Y_t=Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), where innovations \(\varepsilon_t\) are mutually independent, have mean zero, constant positive variance \(\sigma^2\), and are independent of the starting level. Repeated substitution yields \(Y_t=Y_0+\sum_{j=1}^t\varepsilon_j\). If \(Y_0\) is fixed, the expectation is \(E(Y_t)=Y_0\) but the variance is \(t\sigma^2\). The growing variance alone violates weak stationarity. With drift \(c\), the model becomes \(Y_t=Y_{t-1}+c+\varepsilon_t\) and its expectation becomes \(Y_0+ct\); both mean and variance change.

The name “stochastic trend” means that shocks change the future level permanently under this model. If a positive innovation of 4 index points occurs in month 6, all subsequent levels inherit that increment unless later shocks offset it. Subtracting a fitted straight line does not eliminate the accumulating random component. By contrast, in \(100+2t+\varepsilon_t\) a one-time error does not permanently raise the generating trend in every future month. This is the central distinction between trend-stationarity and a unit-root process.

Differencing the random walk gives \(\Delta Y_t=\varepsilon_t\) (or \(c+\varepsilon_t\) with drift). Under the innovation assumptions, this differenced sequence has stable mean, variance and covariance. The original process is described as integrated of order one, \(I(1)\), if one ordinary difference yields a stationary process while the level is not stationary. Integration order is a statistical model property, not a synonym for “visually upward.”

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[Y_t=Y_0+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\varepsilon_j,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=t\sigma^2\]

09. A small random-walk path calculated in full

Suppose the initial index is \(Y_0=100\). We display an illustrative set of eight innovation values \((2,-1,3,0,-2,1,-3,2)\) index points. Accumulate them by addition. Month 1: \(Y_1=100+2=102\). Month 2: \(Y_2=102-1=101\). Month 3: \(Y_3=101+3=104\). Month 4: \(Y_4=104+0=104\). Month 5: \(Y_5=104-2=102\). Month 6: \(Y_6=102+1=103\). Month 7: \(Y_7=103-3=100\). Month 8: \(Y_8=100+2=102\). These are constructed illustration values, not observed innovations or proof of their independence.

Compute changes: \((102-100,101-102,104-101,104-104,102-104,103-102,100-103,102-100)=(2,-1,3,0,-2,1,-3,2)\). Thus differencing precisely recovers the supplied innovations when we retain \(Y_0\). But recovering these eight values does not demonstrate that they are statistically independent; the underlying random-walk model assumes a distribution of innovations, while this path provides just one realization.

Contrast an alternative level path with the same final value: more volatile increments can cancel. Endpoint equality tells you almost nothing about the variance of the innovations or how uncertainty accumulates over longer horizons. Under the ideal random walk, the variance of the cumulative change over \(h\) months is \(h\sigma^2\), giving a standard deviation \(\sqrt{h}\sigma\). The square-root growth of uncertainty makes distant forecasts less precise, even when the point forecast remains the last observed level.

10. Ordinary first differencing: exact arithmetic and missing observations

Define \(D_t=\Delta Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}\) for \(t=2,\ldots,24\). This is a level change in index points per one-month step, not a percent change. The first transformed observation is not defined because \(Y_0\) was not supplied for the 24-month dataset. Consequently 24 consecutive levels yield 23 first differences. Do not substitute zero for the missing initial change: that would add an invented observation and bias summaries.

In the main case, \(Y_1=97\) and \(Y_2=100\) so \(D_2=100-97=3\). Next \(Y_3=106\), so \(D_3=106-100=6\). The transition from month 12 to 13 uses \(Y_{12}=114\) and \(Y_{13}=119\): \(D_{13}=5\). Notice that the ordinary differences still change with the month. An upward mean trend may be removed, while the seasonal oscillations remain; a single difference is not automatically a complete stationary-series solution.

The sign interpretation is direct: \(D_t>0\) means the index rose, \(D_t<0\) means it fell, and \(D_t=0\) means no change. The operation preserves the index-point dimension; “per month” names the interval over which the difference is measured. Interpreting \(D_2=3\) as 3% is incorrect because a percent needs a base, here \(3/97\times100\approx3.09\%\). A first difference may be useful for changes even when a unit-root model cannot be defensibly established.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\Delta Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}\]

11. Seasonal differencing: what one year removes

For monthly observations with a yearly cycle \(m=12\), define \(G_t=\Delta_{12}Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-12}\), for \(t=13,\ldots,24\). This compares the same calendar month in successive years, so 24 monthly levels provide 12 seasonal differences. Under an additive model with the same seasonal offset each year, the seasonal terms cancel algebraically: \((T_t+S_t+R_t)-(T_{t-12}+S_{t-12}+R_{t-12})=(T_t-T_{t-12})+(R_t-R_{t-12})\) because \(S_t=S_{t-12}\).

At month 13, \(Y_{13}=119\) and \(Y_1=97\), giving \(G_{13}=22\). The known trend change is \(2(13-1)=24\) index points, and the remainder change is \(-1-1=-2\); therefore \(G_{13}=24-2=22\). At month 20, the observed value and its month-8 counterpart can likewise be subtracted: calculate them from the table rather than guessing what a seasonal adjustment “should” yield. The result can be called year-over-year change in index points, not automatically year-over-year growth in percent.

Seasonal differencing removes a stable additive seasonal component; if seasonality's shape changes over years it may not fully cancel. It can also leave trend in the year-over-year change, and with only two annual cycles we cannot responsibly infer long-run dynamics. Avoid treating twelve seasonal differences as twelve independent replicates. Adjacent months may remain dependent after transformation. A visual reduction in peaks is evidence of an arithmetic effect, not proof of statistical stationarity.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\Delta_{12}Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-12}\]

12. Combining seasonal and ordinary differences

Two transformations can be combined: \(H_t=\Delta\Delta_{12}Y_t\). Expanding the operators gives \(H_t=Y_t-Y_{t-12}-Y_{t-1}+Y_{t-13}\). Both operators are linear and commute when dates exist: taking first differences and then seasonal differences produces the same numbers as taking seasonal differences and then first differences. A 24-month dataset loses 13 initial positions, leaving \(24-13=11\) combined differences for \(t=14,\ldots,24\).

For our constructed index, \(H_{14}=(Y_{14}-Y_2)-(Y_{13}-Y_1)\). From the data, \(Y_2=100\), \(Y_{14}=124\), so \(Y_{14}-Y_2=24\); \(Y_{13}-Y_1=22\). Therefore \(H_{14}=24-22=2\). Alternatively calculate \((Y_{14}-Y_{13})-(Y_2-Y_1)=(124-119)-(100-97)=5-3=2\). Two routes agree and independently verify the algebra.

This transformed quantity is a change in the year-over-year change. Its interpretation is more remote from the original index than either first or seasonal differences alone. The deterministic linear trend and fixed seasonality cancel in our generating rule, leaving a combination of four remainder values. Additional differencing increases sampling noise when it is unnecessary. Always investigate whether the single seasonal difference already gives a satisfactory model before applying another ordinary difference.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\Delta\Delta_{12}Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}-Y_{t-12}+Y_{t-13}\]

13. Invert first differences exactly: keep the anchor

A common teaching failure is to compute differences and never explain how to return to interpretable levels. If the initial level \(Y_1\) is known, then \(Y_t=Y_1+\sum_{j=2}^t D_j\) for \(t\ge2\). For the first three main-case observations, start with \(Y_1=97\), add \(D_2=3\) to get \(Y_2=100\), and add \(D_3=6\) to get \(Y_3=106\). The arithmetic telescopes: \((Y_2-Y_1)+(Y_3-Y_2)=Y_3-Y_1\) because the intermediate \(Y_2\) cancels.

Why retain the anchor? The difference sequence alone does not specify the absolute level. Adding 500 to every \(Y_t\) leaves all first differences unchanged. If you lost \(Y_1\) there are infinitely many possible level reconstructions, each differing by an additive constant. In forecasting, begin from the latest observed level at forecast origin \(T\) and accumulate predicted changes: \(\widehat Y_{T+h}=Y_T+\sum_{j=1}^h\widehat D_{T+j}\). Each future difference must be generated using information available when the forecast is made.

If the model forecasts a constant next change of 2 points and the last observed level is 140, the successive level forecasts are 142, 144 and 146, not 142 for every horizon. Uncertainty in predicted changes also accumulates; summing point forecasts does not by itself provide forecast intervals. The correct reverse transformation depends on what was differenced and the reference levels retained.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[Y_t=Y_1+\sum_{j=2}^{t}\Delta Y_j\]

14. Invert seasonal differences: twelve distinct anchors

Seasonal differences use \(G_t=Y_t-Y_{t-12}\), so the inverse is \(Y_t=Y_{t-12}+G_t\). To reconstruct a full monthly series after seasonal differencing, retain the twelve initial monthly levels, not just the first one. Each calendar-month chain has its own anchor: January to January, February to February, and so on. In our main case January year two is \(Y_{13}=Y_1+G_{13}=97+22=119\).

For February, \(Y_{14}=Y_2+G_{14}=100+24=124\). A forecast for January of a third year would require the forecasted January year-over-year change plus the latest observed January level, not the latest observed December level. Mixing those anchors produces a numerical value but violates the seasonal operation being inverted. This is a common implementation error when working with differenced model outputs.

For a combined first-and-seasonal difference, inversion requires intermediate changes and a coherent set of historical anchors. One can first recover the seasonal difference series using its initial value, then recover the levels using the preceding twelve observations. A production pipeline must store the last needed level observations and every operation in its original order. Without the anchors, a transformation that looked convenient during analysis becomes impossible to interpret in index-point units.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[Y_t=Y_{t-12}+\Delta_{12}Y_t\]

15. A second difference and its meaning

An ordinary second difference is \(\Delta^2Y_t=\Delta Y_t-\Delta Y_{t-1}=Y_t-2Y_{t-1}+Y_{t-2}\). A first difference measures the change in level; a second difference measures the change in that change. With monthly index data, both are still in index points, but the latter concerns how monthly increments accelerate or decelerate. For a pure linear trend \(Y_t=a+bt\), the first difference is \(b\) and the second difference is zero exactly. This algebra explains why two differences can eliminate a deterministic linear level pattern, but it does not mean two differences are statistically desirable.

Consider levels \((100,105,111,118)\): first differences are \((5,6,7)\) and second differences are \((1,1)\). The series grows faster by one index point per month in this small deterministic illustration. For levels \((100,105,110,115)\), the first differences are \((5,5,5)\) and the second differences are \((0,0)\). The two examples demonstrate arithmetic, not data-generating randomness. We should never identify an ARIMA integration order solely by seeing one or two differences become numerically small.

Each additional difference loses another boundary value and can amplify measurement noise. If the original observations are already stationary with a stable expectation, first differencing typically makes the dependence structure less simple. The practical default is the least differencing justified by the assumed mechanism and diagnostics, not “difference until the plot looks flat.”

16. Why over-differencing induces negative autocorrelation

Suppose \(\varepsilon_t\) is independent white noise with mean zero and variance \(\sigma^2\). This process is already stationary, so there is no trend to remove. Imagine nevertheless constructing \(W_t=\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Its variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(W_t)=\sigma^2+\sigma^2=2\sigma^2\) because the two independent terms have zero covariance. For adjacent changes, expand \(\operatorname{Cov}(W_t,W_{t-1})=\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_{t-1},\varepsilon_{t-1}-\varepsilon_{t-2})=-\sigma^2\). All other cross-products involve independent innovations.

Consequently the lag-one correlation is \(\rho_W(1)=-\sigma^2/(2\sigma^2)=-1/2\). We created an apparent negative one-lag pattern by differencing noise that required no differencing. A sample may not display exactly \(-0.5\) because this is a theoretical process result, not an identity for each finite dataset. But it demonstrates why unnecessary transformations can generate misleading patterns and reduce forecast efficiency.

Over-differencing can also complicate reversal of a model and inflate uncertainty when forecasts are accumulated back to levels. Recognizing it involves triangulating model assumptions, a plot of differences, residual diagnostics and perhaps behavior of sample autocorrelation. It should not be diagnosed with a single automatic threshold. In the following guide, ACF and PACF will provide additional tools for characterizing the remaining dependence.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\rho_{\Delta\varepsilon}(1)=\frac{-\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2}=-\frac12\]

17. The logarithm as a variance-scale transformation

Sometimes economic variability increases as the activity level increases. An index fluctuating around 100 might have changes of 2 points, whereas at 1,000 it might have changes of 20. If relative variability is approximately stable, a logarithm can help. Define \(X_t=\log(Y_t)\) for strictly positive \(Y_t\). For a multiplicative process \(Y_t=L_tM_t\) with positive components, \(\log Y_t=\log L_t+\log M_t\) changes multiplication into addition. This algebra can make growth and seasonality easier to represent, but it is not a universal guarantee of constant variance.

The exact log change is \(\Delta\log Y_t=\log Y_t-\log Y_{t-1}=\log(Y_t/Y_{t-1})\). It is unitless because the ratio cancels the index-point units. Consider activity rising from 100 to 105: ordinary change is 5 points; exact log change is \(\log(1.05)\approx0.048790\), often expressed as 4.8790 log-percentage points. The ordinary percentage change is \((105-100)/100=5\%\). The two are close for a small change but not identical.

For a 50% increase, \(\log(1.5)\approx0.405465\), which is not 0.50. Explain the approximation before using “log difference is percent growth.” The natural logarithm is undefined for zero or negative activity values. Adding an arbitrary constant to force positivity changes the meaning and sensitivity of every ratio; it must be documented and justified, not silently applied.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\Delta\log Y_t=\log\left(\frac{Y_t}{Y_{t-1}}\right)\]

18. Log-differences, exact percentage changes and inversion

If \(g_t=\log(Y_t)-\log(Y_{t-1})\), the exact gross growth factor is \(\exp(g_t)=Y_t/Y_{t-1}\). Thus the exact simple percentage growth rate is \(100[\exp(g_t)-1]\%\). For \(g_t=0.048790\), \(\exp(g_t)\approx1.05\) and simple growth is 5%. If a reader merely multiplies \(0.048790\) by 100, they obtain the approximate 4.879%, a useful approximation only when its size and error are acceptable.

Reconstruction requires an anchor and exponentiation. Given \(Y_0=100\) and next log changes \(\log(1.05)\) then \(\log(0.98)\), the successive levels are \(Y_1=100(1.05)=105\) and \(Y_2=105(0.98)=102.9\). Equivalently \(Y_2=100\exp(g_1+g_2)=102.9\). Simply adding \(0.048790\) index points to 100 is dimensionally wrong: the log change is not a level change. If future log changes are forecast, exponentiating their expected sum generally produces a median-like level forecast under lognormal assumptions, not necessarily the mean; a mean forecast needs a distribution-sensitive bias correction.

Do not assume log-transforming fixes unit roots. A positive random walk on the original scale is not generally turned into a stationary level process by taking logs; the stochastic mechanism matters. The log transformation addresses scale and multiplicative structure, while differencing addresses certain forms of time variation. One can use either, both, or neither depending on the economic meaning and diagnostic evidence.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\%\mathrm{growth}=100\bigl(e^{\Delta\log Y_t}-1\bigr)\]

19. Log versus level: compare units and decisions

Consider two firms whose production indices move from 100 to 110 and from 1,000 to 1,010. Both increase by 10 index points, so on the original level scale the absolute increment is identical. Their simple growth rates differ: \(10/100=10\%\) versus \(10/1000=1\%\). Their log changes are \(\log(1.1)\approx0.095310\) and \(\log(1.01)\approx0.009950\). If the business question is “how many index points changed?” use level differences. If it is “what proportional change occurred?” log or percentage changes may be more meaningful.

Now compare a drop from 110 back to 100. The original rise of 10 points is reversed by a fall of 10 points, but the percentage rise and decline are not symmetrical: +10% from 100, then \(-10/110\approx-9.09\%\) from 110. Log changes are additive and symmetric over reciprocal moves: \(\log(110/100)+\log(100/110)=0\). This makes them useful for chained growth analysis. It does not make them universally more accurate or causally informative.

A logarithmic chart can look more visually stable just because it compresses large values. Before claiming variance stabilization, compare the distribution of errors across different levels, consider reporting units, and examine residual diagnostics. If the business needs forecasts in original index points, evaluate forecast error after correct back-transformation rather than on a transformed scale alone.

20. Structural breaks are not ordinary seasonality

Suppose the index's methodology changes in month 13, adding 15 points to all later reported values without changing actual economic activity. Then the plotted series would show a permanent jump. First differencing turns the break into an unusually large change at the transition, but the statistical transformation does not restore comparability of the two regimes. If the units, coverage or base year changed, the proper task is source reconciliation or a documented statistical adjustment, not blindly differencing.

Economic structural breaks can also be genuine: a pandemic, policy regime or factory closure may shift mean, volatility or persistence. A process stationary within one regime can fail stationarity across the whole sample. Unit-root tests can have distorted performance in the presence of unmodeled breaks; an apparent failure to reject a unit root may reflect low power or misspecification. Researchers should inspect event dates and consider suitable break-aware methods when the research question demands them.

Do not invent event explanations merely because an unusual observation coincides with a familiar historical date. Record observed measurement changes separately from hypothesized economic mechanisms. For our synthetic 24-month case there is no documented break; the exact generating rule is disclosed. A hypothetical break is therefore a separate teaching counterexample, not an extra event hidden in the data table.

21. ADF: a unit root is the null hypothesis

The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test examines a unit-root null in a specified time-series regression. A common representation is \(\Delta Y_t=\alpha+\beta t+\gamma Y_{t-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\Delta Y_{t-j}+u_t\), where the intercept \(\alpha\), deterministic trend \(\beta t\) and lagged changes may be included according to the test specification. The unit-root null is \(H_0:\gamma=0\) (equivalently a lag-one level coefficient of one in the corresponding autoregressive expression). The alternative depends on which deterministic terms were included.

“Augmented” means lagged differences are added to address serial correlation in the error. The number of lags \(p\) matters: too few may leave correlated residuals, while too many consume degrees of freedom and reduce power. The statistic has special nonstandard reference distributions under the unit-root null; using an ordinary regression t critical value is generally incorrect. A p-value below a chosen \(\alpha\) supplies evidence against the specified unit-root null, not proof that the series is strictly or weakly stationary under every possible complication.

A high p-value is even more often misread. Failure to reject \(H_0\) does not prove the series contains a unit root: a short sample, breaks, different deterministic terms, or a near-unit-root stationary process may produce low power. Record whether the implementation used a constant only (c), constant plus trend (ct), or another option and how the lag length was chosen. Two analysts can obtain different results from the same data because they tested different deterministic specifications.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\Delta Y_t=\alpha+\beta t+\gamma Y_{t-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\Delta Y_{t-j}+u_t\]

22. KPSS: stationarity is the null hypothesis

The KPSS test reverses the inferential direction. In a level-stationarity specification, \(H_0\) is that the series is stationary around a constant; the alternative is nonstationarity. With a trend-stationarity specification, \(H_0\) is stationarity around a deterministic trend. These are different null models, so always identify the variant. KPSS uses a partial-sum statistic and a long-run variance estimate, which makes bandwidth selection and serial dependence important.

A small KPSS p-value suggests rejecting the chosen stationarity null. A large p-value means only that the data have not supplied sufficient evidence to reject it; it does not establish stationarity beyond doubt. The test can be sensitive to structural breaks, changing variance, short samples and lag/bandwidth choices. The p-value is conditional on its setup and reference approximation, not a direct probability that the stationarity null is true.

The contrast with ADF can be productive when interpreted carefully: ADF asks whether the data strongly contradict a unit-root null, while KPSS asks whether they strongly contradict a stationarity null. They are not logical complements with an exhaustive answer for every time-series process. ADF's alternative and KPSS's null also need compatible deterministic specifications before comparisons can be meaningful. Jointly using the two tests can illuminate uncertainty, but cannot replace plots, economic knowledge, break diagnostics and sensible transformation decisions.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[H_0^{\mathrm{KPSS}}:\text{level or trend stationarity}\]

23. ADF and KPSS decision matrix, properly qualified

Consider four possible illustrative outcomes at a predeclared 5% significance threshold. First, ADF rejects the unit-root null while KPSS does not reject level stationarity. Those findings are broadly compatible with stationary behavior under the chosen specifications, but are not proof. Second, ADF does not reject while KPSS rejects: findings are compatible with nonstationarity or a unit-root-like process. Third, both reject: contradictory or misspecified assumptions may be present, perhaps a break, nonlinear behavior, heteroscedasticity, different deterministic terms or size distortions. Fourth, neither rejects: low power or an ambiguous near-boundary process may explain the indecision.

Here is a hypothetical example, not results computed from our 24 synthetic observations: ADF p=0.03 under a constant-plus-trend specification, and KPSS trend-stationarity p=0.20. At the chosen threshold ADF rejects its unit-root null and KPSS fails to reject trend stationarity. That is compatible with a trend-stationary interpretation; it is not a calibrated posterior probability that the model is correct. Change the deterministic specification or shorten the sample and conclusions can change.

We deliberately do not manufacture an actual ADF/KPSS result for the tiny 24-month case: the purpose is to teach hypotheses and responsible interpretation, and a single software output would be easily misused as certification. Advanced study may compare alternative lag selections, seasonal-unit-root methods and break-aware tests, with their assumptions. This guide limits formal testing to intuition, formulations and evidence interpretation, as specified by the curriculum.

24. How to diagnose transformations without leaking the future

A responsible workflow begins with the measurement definition and timestamps: regular monthly frequency, missing values, revisions and index rebasing. Plot levels, inspect mean/variance changes and recurring calendar structure. Next write candidate mechanisms: deterministic trend with stationary residuals, stochastic trend from accumulated shocks, seasonality, proportional variance or a structural break. Match operations to mechanisms and interpretation: detrend when a defensible deterministic mean model is appropriate; difference for a plausible integrated trend; apply a seasonal difference for persistent seasonal structure; log-transform when strictly positive values and proportional variability justify it.

At a historical forecast origin \(T\), only observations through \(T\) may inform the transformation parameters and model fitting. A global trend estimated from future months leaks information. A centered smoother may also depend on future observations. An ordinary first or seasonal difference uses past levels and is operationally causal in time, but choosing its order after inspecting the entire held-out future segment leaks model-selection information. Plan rolling-origin validation with each transformation fixed or re-estimated using training history only.

Then inspect transformed mean, variance and autocorrelation; consider ADF/KPSS as limited corroboration rather than a switch that mechanically selects \(d=0,1,2\). Assess whether additional differencing produces spurious negative dependence or loses too many observations. Document the inverse transformation, anchors and any log-scale correction before fitting. Finally evaluate forecasts back on the business scale and explicitly name uncertainty about regime changes. A beautifully flat transformed series is not necessarily the most useful forecasting input.

25. Common misunderstandings worth actively correcting

A trendless graph is not automatically stationary: variance or lag covariance may change while the mean remains flat. A statistically insignificant time-slope coefficient is not proof that a process is stationary, and a deterministic slope does not imply a stochastic unit root. Conversely, a random walk without drift can wander upward for many periods even though its expected level is constant; its variance nevertheless grows over time. “Stationary” also does not mean “independent”; a persistent but stable autoregressive process can satisfy weak stationarity.

Taking a difference is not the same as calculating a percent change, and neither operation necessarily makes the process stationary. Taking logs is not defined for zero or negative levels, and ordinary exponentiation of log forecasts is not automatically an unbiased level-mean forecast. One ordinary difference needs one anchor for reconstruction, while a monthly seasonal difference requires twelve historical anchors. Excessive differencing can create negative lag-one dependence even from independent white noise, as we derived.

Finally, ADF and KPSS do not give interchangeable p-values. ADF's null is a unit root; KPSS's null is a form of stationarity, and both depend on deterministic specifications and assumptions. Failing to reject either null proves neither. The practical skill is to produce a defensible narrative: what was observed, what process was posited, what transformation was applied, what evidence remains, and how the numbers can be recovered and interpreted.

26. Worked case synthesis: three transformations, one original scale

Return to the monthly index. Month 1 is 97, month 2 is 100, month 3 is 106, month 13 is 119 and month 14 is 124. The first differences are \(D_2=3\), \(D_3=6\) and \(D_{14}=124-119=5\). The seasonal differences are \(G_{13}=119-97=22\) and \(G_{14}=124-100=24\). The combined difference is \(H_{14}=24-22=2\). These three transformed values answer three different questions: monthly change, change from the same month last year, and change in the year-over-year change.

Reconstruct month 14 in two independent ways. First use the previous month and a monthly difference: \(Y_{14}=Y_{13}+D_{14}=119+5=124\). Second use the prior-year February and a seasonal difference: \(Y_{14}=Y_2+G_{14}=100+24=124\). From the combined difference alone, we cannot reconstruct an absolute value: we first need prior seasonal changes and original seasonal anchors. This is why the transformation log belongs alongside model outputs, not only within exploratory analysis.

For proportional change, the January year-two/January year-one ratio is \(119/97\approx1.226804\), so the exact ordinary year-over-year growth is about 22.68%. The log year-over-year change is \(\log(119/97)\approx0.2044\), about 20.44 log-percentage points. These differ because a log change is not a percentage change for a large movement. Neither calculation establishes stationarity or causal growth; both describe an arithmetic contrast in a deliberately constructed series.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\Delta\Delta_{12}Y_{14}=(124-100)-(119-97)=2\]

27. A diagnostic calculation: rolling mean and rolling variance

A graph conveys temporal order but may hide gradual change in the mean or dispersion. A rolling statistic provides another descriptive view. For a four-month window ending at time \(t\), define \(\overline Y_{t,4}=(Y_t+Y_{t-1}+Y_{t-2}+Y_{t-3})/4\). This is a trailing mean, calculated with information available by month \(t\); it is not a centered mean that reads the future. In our main case, the first four observations are 97, 100, 106 and 113, so \(\overline Y_{4,4}=(97+100+106+113)/4=416/4=104\) index points. The second four-month window uses observations 100, 106, 113 and 114, giving \(433/4=108.25\). The local mean increased, but the change can reflect deterministic trend, seasonal movement or disturbance.

To quantify dispersion, calculate a sample variance for the first window: \(s^2=[(97-104)^2+(100-104)^2+(106-104)^2+(113-104)^2]/(4-1)\). The squared deviations are 49, 16, 4 and 81, totaling 150; divide by 3 to obtain \(s^2=50\) squared index points, and \(s=\sqrt{50}\approx7.071\) index points. This statistic describes dispersion of four realized values around their local sample mean, not automatically the process's innovation variance. In a window containing a trend, variation due to the trend contributes to the calculated spread. Small windows also produce noisy estimates, particularly with seasonal data.

Repeat the calculation with longer windows or after removing a justified deterministic pattern. Compare results across time while noting that overlapping windows share observations and are not independent. A rolling plot cannot prove stationarity, and window-size selection can change its appearance. Its value is in highlighting questions: does the local level rise; does the residual spread expand; does an unexplained break appear? If later forecasting is the objective, every rolling statistic used as an input must be calculated using data available at that forecast origin.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\bar Y_{4,4}=\frac{97+100+106+113}{4}=104\]

28. Conditional forecasts reveal why the two trends matter

A useful thought experiment compares two mechanisms that are close in the last observed month. Under a deterministic-trend model \(Y_t=a+bt+\varepsilon_t\) with mean-zero stationary errors, the conditional expected level at future date \(T+h\) may follow \(a+b(T+h)\) if the error dynamics are adequately specified. In the particularly simple independent-error case, yesterday's positive error does not change that deterministic future mean. Under a random walk with drift \(Y_t=Y_{t-1}+c+\varepsilon_t\), the level forecast instead starts from the latest observed actual value: \(E(Y_{T+h}\mid\mathcal F_T)=Y_T+hc\) under independent zero-mean innovations. Thus the effect of a positive shock persists in the latter.

Choose a deliberately simple numerical comparison. Suppose the deterministic model's trend at month 24 is 148 index points, with slope 2 points per month, and the actual last value is 156 because of an eight-point shock. Under independent temporary errors, its next-month expected level is \(148+2=150\), not 158. Under a random walk with drift 2 and current level 156, its next expected level is \(156+2=158\). Both models can have the same estimated slope or drift; their conditional initial reference differs because they assign different permanence to shocks. The figures are a new hypothetical teaching comparison, not a forecast for our main dataset or evidence that either model is correct.

At two steps, expectations become 152 and 160 respectively under these simplified mechanisms. Their uncertainty also differs: independent stationary errors around a deterministic trend need not accumulate forecast variance linearly with the horizon, while independent random-walk innovations contribute \(h\sigma^2\) in variance over \(h\) steps. Parameter estimation uncertainty and error dependence add complications omitted from this transparent comparison. This is why stationarity matters operationally: it determines which historical information supports future predictions and how uncertainty is propagated, not merely which graph looks smoother.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[E(Y_{T+h}\mid\mathcal F_T)=Y_T+hc\quad\text{(random walk with drift)}\]

29. Sensitivity and an auditable transformation decision

Suppose a researcher considers four candidates: no transformation, deterministic detrending, first differencing, and log-first-differencing. Rather than announcing one winner from a single p-value, define a reproducible evaluation. First specify the economic target: the next month's index level with error measured in index points. Second set chronological training and evaluation windows and a rolling forecast origin. Third fit any trend or scale parameters on the training portion only. Fourth make forecasts in the transformed space when relevant, invert them using the correct anchor and mathematical operation, and compare prediction errors on the original level scale. Finally inspect residual dependence, changing variance, structural stability and model complexity.

This evaluation does not imply selecting whichever model happens to produce the smallest error on one short holdout and declaring its probabilistic assumptions true. A convenient error score is mean absolute error, \(\operatorname{MAE}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n|Y_i-\widehat Y_i|\), where \(n\) counts genuine out-of-sample forecasts; the result is expressed in index points. For a hypothetical three-month holdout with actual levels \((120,123,121)\) and forecasts \((119,125,120)\), absolute errors are \((1,2,1)\) and \(\operatorname{MAE}=(1+2+1)/3=4/3\approx1.333\) index points. Those invented numbers demonstrate the metric and do not compare the four candidates empirically.

Keep an audit trail showing whether missing data were imputed, why logs were legal, the differencing orders \(d\) and \(D\), any seasonal period \(m\), the ADF/KPSS deterministic specifications, and the initial levels retained for inversion. A small change to those choices can change the apparent forecast ranking. If a method is selected after inspecting held-out observations repeatedly, the evaluation is no longer a clean out-of-sample test. Responsible statistical communication includes this limitation rather than declaring a series stationary merely because a candidate's residual plot appears approximately horizontal.

KEY EQUATION · notation explained in the text
\[\mathrm{MAE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|Y_i-\widehat Y_i\right|\]

30. Worked exercise 1 — identify a weak-stationarity violation

Problem. A process satisfies \(E(Y_t)=40+3t\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=9\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-h})=9(0.5)^{|h|}\). Identify which condition fails. Solution. The expected level changes from 43 at \(t=1\) to 46 at \(t=2\) and continues rising by three points each period. Thus the constant-mean condition fails. The variance is the same finite 9 at all times. Covariance depends on \(|h|\), not on \(t\), so the third condition is satisfied as written. Therefore the level process is not weakly stationary. Subtracting the known mean \(40+3t\) would leave a mean-zero process satisfying the listed moment conditions; this is a hypothetical trend-stationary example, not proof about an observed dataset.

31. Worked exercise 2 — distinguish a variance change

Problem. Suppose \(E(Y_t)=20\) for all \(t\), independent observations have variance \(1\) for \(t\le6\) and variance \(16\) for \(t>6\). Is the process weakly stationary? Solution. The mean condition holds, and independence gives zero lagged covariance for distinct dates. But variance depends on the date: standard deviation grows from \(\sqrt1=1\) to \(\sqrt{16}=4\) index points. Therefore weak stationarity fails. A flat mean plot would miss this failure. A logarithm may sometimes stabilize proportional variance when values are positive, but no such proportional mechanism was specified here; automatically logging is not justified. The first response should be to investigate why the variance regime changed.

32. Worked exercise 3 — compute first and second differences

Problem. The complete illustrative levels are \((50,53,57,62,68)\) index points. Compute both types of differences, counts and interpretation. Solution. Subtract adjacent levels: first differences are \((3,4,5,6)\), so five original points give four changes. Subtract adjacent changes: second differences are \((1,1,1)\), giving three values. Each first difference represents an increment between consecutive dates; each second difference means the increment rose by one point. Starting from the anchor 50, recover levels as \(50+3=53\), then \(53+4=57\), \(57+5=62\), and \(62+6=68\). No assumption of independence or stationarity follows from the arithmetic.

33. Worked exercise 4 — seasonal differencing with the correct anchor

Problem. Suppose the January and February index levels in year one are 95 and 101; those in year two are 118 and 129. Compute seasonal differences at period 12 and reconstruct. Solution. January: \(G_{13}=118-95=23\). February: \(G_{14}=129-101=28\). Their difference is \(H_{14}=28-23=5\), the change between consecutive year-over-year changes. Reconstruct January with \(95+23=118\) and February with \(101+28=129\). Using February's change 28 with January's anchor 95 would yield 123, a mathematically possible sum but the wrong reconstruction. Seasonal inversion requires retaining a separately aligned anchor for each month.

34. Worked exercise 5 — random walk accumulation

Problem. Start at 200 and observe four illustrative random-walk shocks \((+4,-2,-3,+6)\). Calculate levels and the differenced series. Solution. The levels are 204, 202, 199 and 205, since \(200+4=204\), \(204-2=202\), \(202-3=199\), and \(199+6=205\). Their differences relative to each previous level are exactly \((4,-2,-3,6)\). The net four-step change is \(4-2-3+6=5\), agreeing with \(205-200=5\). Under independent innovations with variance \(\sigma^2\), theoretical forecast-error variance after four steps would be \(4\sigma^2\), but the four displayed numbers do not estimate or verify those assumptions on their own.

35. Worked exercise 6 — detrending versus differencing

Problem. Let \(Y_t=30+5t+\varepsilon_t\), where \(\varepsilon_t\) is mean-zero stationary white noise of variance 4. What happens under detrending and differencing? Solution. Subtract \(30+5t\), obtaining \(Z_t=\varepsilon_t\), with mean zero, variance 4 and zero nonzero-lag covariance by assumption. First differencing instead gives \(D_t=5+\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_{t-1}\). The mean is 5, the variance is \(4+4=8\), and lag-one covariance is \(-4\), so lag-one autocorrelation is \(-4/8=-0.5\). Both transformed sequences have constant moment structure, but differencing introduces avoidable negative dependence. This is why choosing an operation involves the modeled mechanism, not just flattening the plotted series.

36. Worked exercise 7 — approximate and exact growth

Problem. Activity rises from 80 to 88. Calculate point change, simple percent growth and log change. Solution. Level difference is \(88-80=8\) index points. Relative growth is \(8/80=0.10=10\%\). Natural-log change is \(\log(88)-\log(80)=\log(1.1)\approx0.095310\), which is about 9.531 log-percentage points. To reverse, \(80\exp(0.095310)\approx88\). The log figure is not an exact “9.531% growth”; exact simple growth is \(100(\exp(0.095310)-1)\approx10\%\). If activity were zero, the log change would be undefined.

37. Worked exercise 8 — detect over-differencing

Problem. Suppose errors are independent white noise with variance 25. An analyst first-differences them. Derive the variance and lag-one autocorrelation of the result. Solution. For \(W_t=\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_{t-1}\), independent components imply variance \(25+25=50\). Adjacent differences share the error \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\) with opposite signs, making their covariance \(-25\). The lag-one autocorrelation is \(-25/50=-0.5\). This value is a property of the modeled process, not an observed sample statistic. Since the original white noise was already stationary, the operation is unnecessary and introduces a new negative dependence structure.

38. Worked exercise 9 — interpret ADF without reversing its null

Problem. A properly specified ADF test reports a hypothetical p-value of 0.018 at a declared 5% threshold. What can be concluded? Solution. ADF has a unit-root null, so \(0.018<0.05\) leads us to reject that null within the chosen deterministic and lag specification. We may say the data are inconsistent with that specific unit-root model at the selected threshold. We cannot announce strict stationarity, independence or correct forecasting behavior. We must inspect the alternative specification, structural breaks, residual dynamics and changes in variance. A p-value is not the probability the null is true.

39. Worked exercise 10 — interpret KPSS with correct variant

Problem. A hypothetical KPSS level-stationarity test gives p=0.02 at 5%. An analyst concludes, “the series is stationary because the test is significant.” Find the error. Solution. The KPSS null in this specification is level stationarity, so \(0.02<0.05\) rejects that null. The stated conclusion reverses the hypothesis. The result suggests evidence against level stationarity within this test's assumptions; it does not prove a unit root or identify a transformation. We next examine plots, possible deterministic trend, variance changes, structural breaks and whether a trend-stationarity KPSS variant is more relevant.

40. Worked exercise 11 — combined differencing and data loss

Problem. A monthly series has \(T=36\) consecutive observations and season length 12. How many values remain after one ordinary difference, one seasonal difference, and both? Solution. Ordinary differencing requires one previous month and leaves \(36-1=35\) changes. Seasonal differencing needs a twelve-month reference and leaves \(36-12=24\) changes. Applying both requires level values through \(t-13\), so it leaves \(36-13=23\) observations. A second ordinary difference after seasonal differencing would lose another point and leave 22. These are arithmetic counts conditional on a complete regular index; missing months must be resolved separately rather than silently treated as valid lags.

41. Worked exercise 12 — rebuild a two-step log forecast

Problem. Last observed index is 120. Forecasted next two log changes are 0.02 and -0.01. Reconstruct level forecasts and interpret. Solution. At horizon one, \(\widehat Y_{T+1}=120e^{0.02}\approx122.4242\). At horizon two, accumulate log changes: \(\widehat Y_{T+2}=120e^{0.02-0.01}=120e^{0.01}\approx121.2060\). The second point forecast is lower than the first because the second predicted log change is negative, but still above the original 120. These are exponentiated point predictions on the log scale. If they represent conditional log means under a nonzero predictive variance, they do not automatically equal conditional means on the original scale.

42. Worked exercise 13 — choose a transformation with a reason

Problem. Two positive index series grow over time. In series A the residual standard deviation is about 2 points at level 100 and 20 points at level 1,000. In series B the residual standard deviation stays close to 2 points at both levels. Which is the stronger candidate for a log transform, and why? Solution. A is a stronger candidate because the ratio of standard deviation to level is roughly \(2/100=2\%\) and \(20/1000=2\%\), suggesting proportional variability. Taking logs may stabilize variance under an appropriate multiplicative-error model. B already has roughly constant absolute spread, so logging might create artificial scale dependence. Neither transformation guarantees stationarity or independence. One must inspect residuals, positivity, possible breaks and meaningful reporting units after fitting.

43. Worked exercise 14 — a transfer case with a structural break

Problem. A statistical agency rebases an activity index at month 25, making its later reported levels 20 points higher even if actual activity was unchanged. An analyst differences the 48-month concatenated series and announces a historic boom. Diagnose and propose a remedy. Solution. The difference at month 25 includes the artificial 20-point rebasing discontinuity. It is not a genuine 20-point economic shock. The analyst must investigate official methodological documentation, convert both segments to a comparable base if a valid bridge exists, or model/report the break explicitly. A test result after naive differencing can be distorted by this unhandled change. The remedy is measurement reconciliation before deciding stationarity or a transformation. Without a credible bridge, analysis should disclose the comparability limitation rather than inventing values.

44. Worked exercise 15 — fully integrate the monthly case

Problem. From the disclosed 24-month dataset, calculate (i) the month-14 first difference, (ii) the month-14 seasonal difference, (iii) the month-14 combined difference, and (iv) the exact year-over-year percentage for month 13. Then explain what cannot be concluded. Solution. Month 13 equals 119, month 14 equals 124, month 2 equals 100, and month 1 equals 97. First difference: \(124-119=5\) points. Seasonal difference: \(124-100=24\) points. Combined: \((124-100)-(119-97)=24-22=2\) points. Month-13 year-over-year growth: \(100(119/97-1)\approx22.6804\%\). Both inverse checks succeed: \(119+5=124\) and \(100+24=124\). These operations do not prove weak stationarity, a unit root, or causal economic growth. The observations were constructed with deterministic trend and seasonal pattern; a real series requires measurement audit and model-based diagnostics.

45. Advanced synthesis — what would change under a different process?

Suppose the same observed sequence were modeled as a deterministic-trend process with stationary errors, or as a random walk with drift and appropriately chosen innovations. A short finite path may be compatible with both explanations. Under the first, an unexpected shock is transient relative to the stated deterministic mean; under the second, it enters the accumulated level and affects future conditional expectations. Therefore the forecast of a level many months ahead, the width of forecast intervals and the usefulness of detrending differ substantially. The choice is not resolved by simply choosing the method that makes the past graph look smoothest.

A good analyst states the null and alternative of any formal test, records the deterministic terms and lag choices, and triangulates them with data provenance and structural-break possibilities. When fitting regressions after differencing, remember that coefficients now refer to changes rather than level relationships. When interpreting a logged difference, report whether the output is in log points or converted to exact percent changes. A transformation alters the target variable and hence the question being answered; it should never happen invisibly between analysis and presentation.

The transferable habit is to write down the forward and inverse maps before running a procedure. For ordinary differences, store one starting level. For seasonal differences, store twelve matching monthly anchors. For log transforms, preserve positivity assumptions and exponentiation with the necessary distribution correction for mean forecasts. For a combination of operations, document the order and all boundary observations. A process may look stationary after transformation yet still fail a useful forecasting model because of nonlinear dependence, changing variance or breaks.

46. Final learning assessment and responsible conclusion

Before treating this guide as mastered, try to answer without looking at the formulas: Why can a random walk without drift be nonstationary even though its expected level does not change? Why is a process with constant variance but a rising deterministic mean nonstationary in levels? What is removed by year-over-year differencing when the seasonal shape is exactly repeated? How many initial levels must be stored to invert that operation? Why does differentiating white noise introduce a lag-one correlation of \(-0.5\)? Which null belongs to ADF, and which null belongs to KPSS? What does failure to reject either null actually mean?

The worked answers distributed through the guide provide the mechanisms: a random walk's variance grows with time; a deterministic trend changes the mean; seasonal offsets cancel when they match; monthly seasonal inversion needs twelve anchors; over-differencing creates a shared innovation with opposite signs; ADF uses a unit-root null and KPSS a stationarity null. Neither non-rejection certifies a universal model property. If you can reconstruct the numeric cases from the original data, explain every operation and recognize these limits, you have the required foundation for Guide 65 on autocovariance, autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation.

The case values and innovation examples are synthetic and chosen only for instruction. No human-participant learning assessment was conducted. The academic references for the mathematical definitions and procedures are provided below; the data-generating formulas and teaching calculations remain explicitly distinguishable from externally cited results. The main practical lesson is that a stationarity judgment is a reasoned conditional model assessment, while differencing and back-transformation are exact arithmetic that you should always be able to reproduce.
