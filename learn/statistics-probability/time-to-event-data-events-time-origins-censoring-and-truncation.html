1. The question: how long until an event?

A reliability engineer follows industrial compressors to learn how long they remain in service before their first mechanical failure. The familiar spreadsheet question, “What was the outcome?”, is not enough. A compressor may fail after two months, another after nine, and another may still be operating when the study ends. Reporting all three as if their failure times were known would make up an observation for the third compressor. Dropping the still-operating compressor would discard evidence that it survived at least until the last inspection. Time-to-event data preserve both the duration observed and the reason observation stopped.

The central question is not merely which devices failed; it is which device was at risk, from what time, until which observed endpoint, and whether that endpoint truly was the event. This distinction supports later survival curves and hazard models, but we deliberately start before estimation. We first make the data structure understandable. Every formula below is tied to a timeline, a declared unit, and a physically meaningful event definition. The case is entirely synthetic teaching data, not observations from any manufacturer or an empirical claim about equipment quality.

2. Prerequisites and essential vocabulary

The essential prerequisites are a basic idea of a random variable, a probability between zero and one, and the distinction between a population and a sample (Guides 04, 07 and 10). If those topics are unfamiliar, here is the usable bridge: a random variable is a numerical outcome that can differ from one unit to another; the population distribution describes hypothetical repetitions under stated conditions; the observed sample contains only the units actually enrolled. A duration is measured in a physical unit such as days, months, machine-hours, or years. We never combine machine-hours and calendar months without a justified conversion and a common origin.

A subject is the unit followed; here it is one compressor, not one daily measurement. An event is a predeclared transition, here first mechanical failure. Time zero is the origin from which the duration is measured, such as installation, enrollment or start of treatment. Follow-up is the observation window. Censoring means the actual event time is not exactly observed but certain bounds are known. Truncation is a selection rule: some units never enter the data because their event-time relationship to an entry condition makes them unobservable. These terms are not synonyms for an empty spreadsheet cell. We will keep their meanings separate throughout the guide.

3. Define the event before recording the clock

Suppose the organization records emergency shutdown, scheduled maintenance and mechanical failure as different codes. If our event is first mechanical failure, scheduled maintenance is not automatically a failure. An emergency shutdown caused by a sensor alarm may also be different from a failed compressor. A precise event definition includes a confirmation rule, a date or time-stamp, a severity threshold if relevant, and rules for repeated events. We use the first confirmed mechanical failure. Later failures after repair are outside the single-event question posed here.

Changing the event definition halfway through the analysis changes which endpoints count. For example, if a compressor experiences a preventive replacement at month 7, it has left service without the target failure. Whether that is censoring or a competing event depends on the scientific question and the physical possibility of observing the target afterward. Merely labeling it “censored” because it is not the desired event can be wrong: a replacement may permanently prevent the original compressor from failing in service. Clear definitions before data collection reduce selective relabeling after results are seen.

4. Time zero, calendar time and time on study

Imagine compressor A was installed on 1 January and failed on 1 March, while compressor G was installed on 1 March and failed on 1 September. Their calendar dates are different, yet each duration must be measured from a meaningful origin. In a study of age since installation, G's failure is measured as approximately six months since its installation. In a study with a common observation start on 1 January, G is not under observation until March. These are two different clocks: equipment age and calendar time since the study began. Do not move their zeros silently.

This guide fixes one common study clock measured in months since the monitoring program opened, with entry times recorded for equipment joining later. That makes delayed entry easy to visualize. If we were instead modeling age since installation, we would need each installation date and a consistent entry age. A correct dataset declares the time scale and the date conversion rule, including how partial months or operating hours are treated. Time origins determine who is eligible for a risk set; an incorrect origin can turn two months of true exposure into several months of imaginary risk.

5. The latent failure time and the censoring time

Let T denote the nonnegative time to first failure on the chosen clock, and let C be the time at which observation would stop for a reason other than the target failure. For ordinary right-censored follow-up beginning at time zero, the record is \(Y=\min(T,C)\) and \(\delta=\mathbf 1(T\le C)\). Thus Y is the observed duration and δ equals one only when the failure was actually observed. This notation prevents the common mistake of calling Y “the failure time” for everyone. For censored units, Y is instead a lower bound on T.

Why the minimum? We learn whichever happens first: failure or the end of observation. If failure occurs at month 5 but tracking would otherwise end at month 12, Y=5 and δ=1. If tracking ends at month 7 while the device remains operational, Y=7 and δ=0; we know T>7 under the declared convention, not T=7. For a simultaneous reported event and censoring time, our formula assigns the observed event priority, but a real project must establish which timestamp and event ordering are reliable. This simplified formula applies to right censoring; left and interval censoring require different records.

7. Construct each time–event record

Start with A: entry 0, exit 2, δ=1. It belongs to the monitoring cohort from month 0 until its mechanical failure at month 2. Its observed follow-up length is 2−0=2 months. B: entry 0, exit 3, δ=0. Its record does not mean it failed at month 3. It means it was seen functioning until month 3 and then observation ended; its true target failure, if it eventually happens, lies beyond what the project observed. C: entry 0, exit 5, δ=1, contributes five months until failure. G: entry 2.5, exit 6, δ=1, contributes 3.5 monitored months; it was not in the observed cohort at month 1.

Now compare I (entry 4.5, exit 11, δ=1) and J (entry 5.5, exit 12, δ=0). I has 6.5 observed months ending in the event. J has 6.5 observed months ending administratively, with no observed failure. These two follow-up lengths happen to match; their scientific meaning does not. The two-column pair (Y,δ) can encode the endpoint and its status, but with delayed entry the dataset must also preserve L. A reproducible record is therefore \((L_i,Y_i,\delta_i)\), plus identifiers and documented reasons for censoring. Discarding L would misrepresent how long each unit was eligible to be counted.

8. A follow-up diagram as a data audit

A timeline is not decoration. Each horizontal line begins at enrollment, ends at the observed exit, and uses a dot for confirmed failure or a hollow mark for censoring. The part of the common clock before enrollment is left blank. This visual answers three operational questions simultaneously: when a unit could enter the risk set, when it leaves, and why it leaves. In the companion diagram, A and C show early failures, whereas J and L reach the administrative end without failure. G and K visibly begin later and therefore cannot be included in early risk sets.

When checking a real database, compare the plotted segment against the raw timestamp fields. An exit preceding entry is not a short survival time; it is a data defect or a mismatched clock. A unit with a positive event indicator but no verified event code requires reconciliation. An ordinary absent measurement inside a monitored period must not be converted to censoring if the failure time is known from another reliable source. Likewise a late enrollment date is not permission to manufacture prior event-free follow-up. The diagram makes these mistakes easier to spot than a table of durations alone.

9. Right censoring is partial information

Right censoring is the central operational case in this guide. When a unit is observed without failure up to month 8 and then monitoring stops, the information is \(T>8\) under the convention that it was still event-free through the observation endpoint. It is not “failure at 8,” “failure never,” or “missing entirely.” A censored unit has a known lower bound on its event time. That lower bound matters when describing which units were still at risk before time 8.

There are several reasons for censoring. Administrative censoring occurs when observation ends on a prespecified study closing date. Loss to follow-up occurs when records cease earlier and the unit's subsequent event status cannot be established. These may have different assumptions: the first often reflects a known calendar rule, whereas the second may be related to the health or reliability of the unit. The analyst should store a reason code instead of flattening both into an unexplained δ=0. Different follow-up lengths are not inherently bias; selective loss related to impending failure is a more serious issue.

10. Risk sets: who was actually under observation?

At an event time t, define the observed risk set \(R(t)=\{i:L_i<t\le Y_i\}\). Here L is entry and Y is exit on the same common time clock. The strict inequality at entry reflects our dataset: every late entry lies strictly between integer-valued event times, avoiding ambiguous simultaneous enrollment and failure. The weak inequality at exit means the subject that fails at t is still counted just before its observed failure. A censored unit present at time t is similarly at risk immediately before it leaves. This is a bookkeeping definition, not yet a survival estimator.

At month 2, only A–F have entered, and all six have exits at or after 2: \(R(2)=\{A,B,C,D,E,F\}\), so n(2)=6 and one event occurs. At month 5, A has failed and B was censored at 3; meanwhile G, H and I have enrolled. The risk set is \(R(5)=\{C,D,E,F,G,H,I\}\), hence n(5)=7 and one event occurs. J only enters at 5.5, so it is correctly excluded at 5. By retaining a complete history of entry and exit, these counts can be computed without guessing any hidden failure time.

11. Risk-set accounting at every failure

Repeat the condition \(L_i<t\le Y_i\) at every observed failure time. At month 6, C has already failed; D, E and F remain, G fails at that instant, H and I remain and J has entered. Thus \(R(6)=\{D,E,F,G,H,I,J\}\) and n(6)=7. At month 9, D was censored at 7, H at 8, and late entrant L has arrived: \(R(9)=\{E,F,I,J,K,L\}\), n(9)=6. At month 10, \(R(10)=\{F,I,J,K,L\}\), n(10)=5; K fails while F is censored at the same integer month. At month 11, \(R(11)=\{I,J,L\}\), n(11)=3. The ordered event risk-set sizes are therefore 6, 7, 7, 6, 5, 3, each with one observed event.

A rising risk-set count is possible even after failures, because more units may enter than leave. This is an important consequence of Delayed Entry and a reason not to assume all curves must have risk-set counts declining from the first time point. The risk sets above describe the monitored cohort on the common study-time clock; they do not automatically represent an unbiased population of compressor lifetimes. That further claim requires a defensible sampling and entry process. Also, when a censoring and an event share a rounded timestamp, the true ordering must be documented rather than inferred from the rounded month labels alone.

12. Why deleting censored rows distorts the question

Take the six observed failure times from our complete dataset: 2, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 11 months. Their arithmetic mean is \((2+5+6+9+10+11)/6=43/6\approx7.17\) months. It is correct to call this the mean of observed failure endpoints among those that failed during this particular monitoring window. It is not a valid estimate of the mean lifetime of all enrolled devices: the six censored devices may fail later, and delayed entry changes who could have been observed at early times. Calling 7.17 the cohort mean lifetime substitutes a selected subset for the population at risk.

We can also calculate the mean of the twelve observed exit timestamps, but that number combines real failures and arbitrary administrative endpoints, so it has no simple interpretation as a lifetime mean. A cleaner descriptive report states “12 enrolled; 6 observed first failures; 6 right-censored” and describes how follow-up lengths differ. More advanced estimators will use partial information rather than filling in fictitious failure times. The practical principle is straightforward: censoring changes the type of information, not the existence of the subject. Any method that discards censored units without justification is answering a different, selected-sample question.

13. Independent censoring is a conditional assumption

Many standard survival procedures need an assumption relating the censoring mechanism to the event mechanism. Informally, after conditioning on the variables actually used for adjustment, the fact that a unit is censored should not reveal an additional systematic difference in its remaining failure time. A common notation under suitable conditions is \(T\perp C\mid X\), where X denotes measured relevant covariates. This is a modeling assumption, not a statement that all censoring is harmless and not something that a failure-indicator column can prove.

If a sensor network disconnects randomly because of scheduled IT maintenance, independent censoring might be plausible, subject to checking other dependencies. If machines often disappear from the database because they exhibit alarming vibrations and are sent elsewhere for emergency replacement, disappearance is informative about failure risk. Conditioning on measured vibration severity may help, but unmeasured indicators can still violate the assumption. Similarly, administrative censoring can become outcome-related when everyone is enrolled at different calendar dates during a period of changing technology. Ask: given the observed data, can a unit's exit without failure convey information about its unobserved failure risk? This question should appear in the study protocol and final limitations.

14. Left censoring: the event occurred before detection

Suppose a compressor is inspected for the first time at month 4 and is already broken, but no installation log or previous inspection tells us its exact failure date. We learn \(T\le4\), not that it failed precisely at 4. This is left censoring. If we incorrectly code it as an exact event at month 4, we shift an unknown earlier failure to the inspection date. If we instead mark it right-censored at 4, we assert the opposite inequality, namely that the device survived through that date. Both shortcuts reverse or destroy the information actually observed.

A suitable record stores the upper bound on event time and an indicator identifying left censoring. It may also need a meaningful lower bound (such as installation at month 0). This situation is common when equipment is only occasionally inspected, but not every failure noticed at a late visit is left-censored. If a maintenance log records the exact failure timestamp even though the engineer discovered it later, the event time may be known. The relevant question is what is known about occurrence time, not simply when the researcher read the report. We introduce the data representation here; specialized estimators for mixed censoring belong to later or more advanced study.

15. Interval censoring: the event lies between visits

Suppose the unit is working at month 3 and is found failed at month 7, with no intervening inspection or electronic event log. We know \(3<T\le7\), but the exact T is unknown. This is interval censoring. A dataset that records only “failure month 7” treats detection as occurrence and may systematically exaggerate failure times. A dataset that only records “unknown date” discards the informative bounds provided by the inspection history. The right record retains the last known event-free time and the first known failed time.

If inspections occur monthly, the interval may be narrow; if they occur annually, the bounds may be very wide. The width is part of the measurement process, not a property of the equipment itself. An accurate study therefore reports inspection cadence and whether it differs by machine condition. In interval censoring, using a midpoint such as (3+7)/2=5 months is at best an explicit approximation with assumptions, never an observed exact event time. The midpoint is not automatically unbiased if failures cluster near inspections or the interval lengths depend on risk. We can construct and interpret the bounds without teaching the specialized likelihood in this introductory guide.

16. Truncation changes who enters the sample

Censoring concerns incomplete information for a unit that appears in the dataset. Truncation concerns a rule under which certain units are absent from the dataset altogether. Suppose a registry begins only with compressors that are still in service when a new monitoring system is installed. A compressor that failed before installation of that monitoring system may have no row. The observed registry systematically overrepresents units that survived long enough to become eligible. That is a selection mechanism rather than a record of right censoring. We cannot reconstruct the missing machines merely from the observed rows.

Left truncation appears when a unit is observable only if its event time exceeds an entry threshold. If the time origin is installation age, a compressor first recruited at age 4 years must have survived to at least age 4 to appear. Its earlier at-risk history cannot be treated as if it were sampled under observation. Later entry means the equipment begins contributing to the observed risk set at its entry age, conditional on being event-free at that point. This is also called Delayed Entry. Right truncation is a different mechanism in which only units experiencing events before a cutoff might enter a sample. Our central worked case focuses on left truncation and late entry.

17. Delayed entry and risk-set correction

Look again at compressor K: it enters the program at month 6.5 and fails at month 10. Without its entry time, a naive analysis might count it as at risk during months 2, 5 and 6, despite the fact that it was not enrolled. That wrongly increases early risk-set denominators. With entry correctly recorded, K is included at months 9 and 10, but not before 6.5. Equipment L enters at 8.5 and is censored at 12; it can contribute to risk sets at 9, 10 and 11 without being a failure. Those are ordinary, scientifically meaningful observations, provided entry and sampling mechanisms are adequately understood.

The simple inclusion rule \(L_i<t\le Y_i\) is a compact encoding of this logic. For models on an age time scale, however, L and Y would both need to be expressed as age since installation, not as calendar time since our program opened. The same calendar table can answer a different scientific question only after reconstructing that common age scale. Delayed entry corrections fix the accounting of observed risk; they do not magically correct every selection bias. If machines are recruited because they already appear unusually durable or because high-risk machines are excluded, additional assumptions are needed for population inference.

18. Immortal-time errors and false exposure

Consider a maintenance program that classifies a compressor as “treated” if it receives a preventive upgrade at month 6. To enter the treated group under this retrospective definition, the compressor must remain in service until month 6. If the analyst then starts comparing the treated and untreated groups from installation without accounting for upgrade timing, the first six months for eventual recipients become guaranteed failure-free by construction. That period is sometimes called immortal time with respect to the defining event: machines failing early cannot be treated later.

This problem is not solved merely by changing the color of a chart or adding another covariate to an ordinary baseline table. The exposure needs a time-aware definition or the design needs a common, defensible landmark with explicit eligibility. In our dataset, the parallel warning is that “survived long enough to enter monitoring” is a prerequisite for joining the late-entry cohort. The information before entry is not observed prospective risk time. Students should be able to ask whether a grouping variable depends on future survival. Whenever it does, the time origin, eligibility and attribution of risk time must be reconsidered before interpreting a comparison.

19. Competing events: an introductory boundary

What happens if a compressor is permanently removed for a nonmechanical reason before its first mechanical failure? Suppose it is destroyed in an external fire or replaced as part of a plant closure. The original compressor cannot subsequently experience a first in-service mechanical failure under the same operational definition. This is a competing event: it changes the possible occurrence of the target event, rather than simply hiding a future event time. Recording such an occurrence as ordinary independent right censoring can distort estimates of actual failure incidence.

For an introductory dataset, create separate status labels: mechanical failure (target), still functioning at study end (administrative censoring), lost contact without known failure, and permanently removed due to another cause (candidate competing event). If we later simplify to a binary δ, we must explicitly state what happens to each alternative endpoint and which estimand the analysis targets. The dataset of twelve compressors was constructed with only observed target failures and ordinary censoring to keep the core calculation transparent. Competitor modeling, cause-specific hazards and cumulative incidence are advanced extensions, not interchangeable shortcuts in this introductory lesson.

20. Missing readings versus missing event status

A database may have no vibration reading for compressor E on a Tuesday while the equipment continues to be followed, and its eventual failure at month 9 is securely logged. That missing sensor value is a missing covariate measurement, not censoring of the event time. Conversely, if all monitoring ends and there is no later reliable confirmation, the event time is right-censored at the last verified event-free instant. The distinction depends on the information process and the event definition, not on the presence of a generic null value.

A second subtlety is missing event status at a visit. If a technician could not inspect a unit, but at a subsequent visit confirms it failed sometime in between, the event may be interval-censored. If no later inspection occurs, the last confirmed operating time gives a right-censoring bound. In both situations, inventing a point event at the first missing field is unjustified. Data collection should therefore store timestamps of the last confirmed functioning state, first confirmed event, last contact, registry closure and replacement. This is much more useful than a single ambiguous column named “status” with no operational dictionary.

21. Units of analysis and repeated measurements

Each compressor contributes one primary event-time record in this guide. If the monitoring system collects 100 vibration readings per compressor, there may be 1,200 sensor rows, but still only twelve experimental or observational units for the first-failure question. Treating all readings as independent compressor lifetimes would multiply the apparent sample size without creating additional independent failures. This is pseudoreplication. The repeated readings can be useful as time-varying predictors in advanced models, but their dependence must be handled instead of ignored.

Equipment nested within the same plant may share operating conditions, maintenance policies and power supply. Even twelve different compressor IDs might not be statistically independent if a common plant-wide event affects all of them. Our arithmetic risk sets do not require independent units to list who is present, but inferential uncertainty later may require clustered, shared-frailty or other dependence-aware methods. Define the target population and observational unit before counting sample size. A report should distinguish number of plants, number of compressors, total monitored months and number of target events. These are four different quantities answering different questions.

22. Descriptive counts and observed exposure

The twelve rows contain six events and six right-censored exits. Counting all row-wise monitored durations \(Y_i-L_i\) yields a total exposure of 64 compressor-months. This is a directly checkable descriptive quantity: each compressor contributes only after its recorded entry and until its exit. The arithmetic is 2+3+5+7+9+10+3.5+4.5+6.5+6.5+3.5+3.5 = 64 compressor-months. The explicitly displayed sum is an independent check that prevents a plausible but false reported total.

A descriptive observed-event rate can be reported as \(6/64=0.09375\) observed target failures per monitored compressor-month under the chosen counting convention. Do not turn this into “9.375% probability of failure each month” without stronger constant-rate and population assumptions. It is a crude quotient of counts by observed time, not yet a survival probability, a hazard estimate, or a causal effect. Unlike a percent, its dimension is events per compressor-month. The denominator excludes unmonitored pre-entry time and unobserved post-censoring time, which prevents making a long-looking dataset by inventing exposure outside the actual monitoring window.

23. A construction protocol from raw logs

To build these data from an industrial database, first define a unique compressor ID and versioned event dictionary. Next establish the study clock and the precision of timestamps. For every unit, retrieve the eligibility or entry timestamp, the first confirmed target event if any, the last verified working timestamp, the withdrawal or loss-to-follow-up timestamp if relevant and the administrative closure timestamp. Then apply a predeclared rule to choose the earliest applicable observed endpoint. Record both the endpoint timestamp and an indicator of why observation ended. Finally transform all timestamps to the same units and derive the corresponding entry and exit durations.

A row-level audit should require that entry precedes exit, the event flag agrees with the underlying event log, the event occurs inside the eligible observation window and reasons for censoring are traceable. A cohort-level audit checks how many records were screened, excluded, entered late, observed to fail and right-censored. The denominator of any flow diagram must match the raw eligibility criteria. For conflicting records, flag uncertainty; do not silently prefer the timestamp that supports the hypothesis. Raw records, transformation scripts and version identifiers are essential for reproducibility even when the final lesson is presented as an HTML page rather than a programming lab.

24. What the dataset does not yet identify

We can count observed events and construct risk sets. We cannot yet claim the overall population's mean failure time, probability of surviving 12 months, or the effect of preventive maintenance. Those require additional estimands, assumptions and estimation methods. Even an apparently complete six-event table is insufficient for such claims when the remaining subjects are right-censored and some enrolled late. The order of learning matters: first understand the recorded bounds and sampling process; only then choose a survival function, hazard or regression model.

The same caution applies across disciplines. In medicine, time zero might be treatment initiation; death or relapse may be an event; switching treatment might be a competing event or a protocol deviation. In customer analytics, time zero might be account opening, event might be cancellation, and data export date might create administrative censoring. Identical columns can be mathematically structured but scientifically different because risk, eligibility and event meaning differ. The transferable skill is to translate domain facts into a defensible observation mechanism, not to assign 0 and 1 mechanically to any status code. Our final exercises will test precisely that transfer.

25. Design decisions that change the meaning of survival

A team can construct technically valid triples (L,Y,δ) and still study the wrong quantity. Consider four alternative origins for an industrial system: first factory assembly, installation at a customer site, first day powered on, and entry into a warranty program. These clocks answer different questions. Assembly-to-failure includes storage time; installation-to-failure includes periods the machine may be idle; powered-on operating hours represent exposure to actual operation; warranty-entry-to-failure is conditional on remaining in service until warranty entry. None is universally correct. We must choose the origin that corresponds to the scientific or operational decision, then maintain it for every unit in the analysis.

The event must match the origin. A warranty study might focus on a claim rather than physical failure, because reporting requirements can change the elapsed time between failure and claim. A clinical study may distinguish death from first relapse, because both can occur on different trajectories. An online platform may define account closure separately from last login: prolonged inactivity is not the same as a recorded cancellation. These are not just domain details; changing the definition alters the event indicator, timing and risk-set membership. Before extracting data, write a short protocol specifying (a) population and eligibility, (b) start of observation, (c) event confirmation, (d) endpoint when no event is observed, (e) entry timing and (f) the target comparison or descriptive estimand.

For our compressors, the common monitoring clock is a didactic convenience and not a hidden assertion that a month since program start equals a month of machine age. All calculations are valid as bookkeeping on this declared clock. To infer on physical durability since installation, the dataset would need installation timestamps and a scientifically appropriate age-based time scale. An honest boundary between “can construct the dataset” and “can estimate a population reliability function” protects students from overinterpreting precise arithmetic.

26. Risk sets as a reproducible algorithm

A risk set can be constructed using a deterministic procedure rather than visual guesswork. First sort unique verified target event times. For each event time t, visit every enrolled record and evaluate two statements: “Was this unit already eligible and observed immediately before t?” and “Had it not yet left observation before t?” Under our stated convention these are simply \(L_i<t\) and \(Y_i\ge t\). Include the unit if both statements are true. Then count included IDs and count observed events occurring at that same t. Store the full ID list, not only the final count, because a list makes discrepancies auditable.

For example, at t=9, the entry rule admits A–L except units that arrived after 9 (there are none); the exit rule removes A, B, C, D, G and H because each exit is earlier than 9. What remains is exactly E, F, I, J, K and L. This produces n(9)=6, while the event count is d(9)=1 because only E fails then. If a script returned 7, we could inspect the reported IDs and identify which earlier exit had been mishandled. Counting only unit numbers is weaker than reproducing membership.

The procedure also requires a policy for ties. In our synthetic dataset, no entry occurs exactly at an event time, so a strict entry inequality avoids ambiguity. Real data often have timestamp precision only to a day. If one device enrolls and another fails on the same recorded day, their true order may not be known. The study should define whether entry occurs at beginning of day, whether failure is recorded at end of day and how event–censor ties are processed; these decisions must be applied consistently, tested in edge cases and reported in QA.

27. A sensitivity comparison that exposes selection

Consider two naive summaries based on the same dataset. The first reports the average of the six observed failures as 7.17 months. The second assigns every right-censored compressor the time it was last seen and averages all twelve exit times. The second sum is 2+3+5+7+9+10+6+8+11+12+10+12 = 95, so the mean recorded exit time is \(95/12\approx7.92\) months on the common program clock. This apparent increase from 7.17 to 7.92 does not prove greater durability: the second number averages a mixture of event times and monitoring cutoffs, and delayed entries mean it is not even the average monitored duration. It is simply a different, administrative summary.

The average observed follow-up length, by contrast, is \(64/12\approx5.33\) monitored months per enrolled unit. It uses \(Y-L\) and is a coherent statement about data collection rather than lifetime. These three averages—7.17, 7.92 and 5.33—are all arithmetically reproducible yet answer three different questions. The distinction demonstrates why one cannot choose a plausible statistic and decide only afterward what to call it. The estimand and observation mechanism must be declared before calculating.

A useful sensitivity question asks what happens if censored units are disproportionately fragile. The same recorded endpoints could arise whether their unknown failures happen soon after censoring or much later. The available dataset cannot distinguish those possibilities without assumptions or additional follow-up. Rather than fabricating exact values for them, a responsible analysis documents censoring causes, explores plausible mechanisms and proceeds to methods appropriate for partial time-to-event information. No amount of decimal precision in 7.17 or 7.92 resolves the underlying identification problem.

28. Independent validation and an audit trail

A strong verification procedure checks the same central result in two different ways. For the observed exposure, one calculation sums twelve individual durations; another can reconstruct how many compressors are under observation on successive portions of the clock and add the resulting person-time rectangles. Both must equal 64 compressor-months when the same open/closed endpoint convention is applied. For the risk sets, manual inclusion checks at 2, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 11 should agree with a small independent algorithm. A failure in either comparison is a reason to inspect source rows, not to choose whichever number looks more convenient.

Verification is also conceptual. Before release, ask whether every δ=1 has a confirmed target event, whether any δ=0 actually hides a known event, whether left-censored or interval-censored records were forced into an inappropriate right-censoring schema, and whether late entrants were incorrectly treated as present since time zero. Inspect the first, middle and final pedagogical sections separately: the student should still be able to reproduce the last exercise rather than encountering a shallow answer key.

Finally separate technical testing from reader understanding. Passing an HTML validator or recomputing 64 does not establish that an introductory student knows why censored rows remain in the dataset. The Student Understanding Test asks whether a beginner can follow the argument, reproduce the calculation and explain its reason in their own words. Editorial review may support that judgment, but it is not an experiment with real learners. A transparent QA report identifies what was directly tested and what remains unverified, especially external MathJax rendering and the final deployed website paths.

29. Exercise 1: Interpret a right-censored observation

Question. Compressor B entered at month 0 and its last verified operating observation is at month 3, without recorded target failure. Write (L,Y,δ), the observed follow-up duration and the inequality for its unknown failure time. Explain why recording an event at 3 is incorrect.

Solution. Its record is \((0,3,0)\). Observed duration is \(3-0=3\) compressor-months. Because the compressor was observed functioning through the endpoint, \(T_B>3\) under our endpoint convention. δ=0 says no confirmed mechanical failure was observed, not that failure never happens. Coding δ=1 would invent the event at month 3; deleting the row would discard three months of evidence. A correct interpretation communicates both what we know (a lower bound) and what remains unknown (the actual future time).

30. Exercise 2: Calculate follow-up after delayed entry

Question. Compressor K enters at month 6.5 and fails at 10. How many months are directly followed? Should it count at the failure times 5, 6, 9 and 10?

Solution. The observed record is \((6.5,10,1)\). Subtract entry from exit: \(10-6.5=3.5\) months under observation. The risk rule is \(6.5<t\le10\), so K is absent at 5 and 6 but present at 9 and 10. At 10 it is still in the risk set immediately before its failure. Counting it at 5 would add a unit that had not joined the monitored cohort. This is the operational reason entry time must be saved separately from time since the original study opening.

31. Exercise 3: Rebuild the risk set at month six

Question. Use all twelve rows to reconstruct the monitored risk set at month 6. Show inclusions and exclusions rather than giving a single count.

Solution. The condition is \(L_i<6\le Y_i\). A is out because failure occurred at 2; B is out because observation stopped at 3; C is out after failure at 5. D and E remain, F remains, G enters at 2.5 and fails now, H enters at 3.5, I at 4.5, and J at 5.5. K enters at 6.5 and L at 8.5, so neither qualifies. The set is \(\{D,E,F,G,H,I,J\}\), exactly seven units, with one observed failure at this time. The result agrees with the dataset-derived risk-set table.

32. Exercise 4: Check the monitored exposure total

Question. Compute total observed compressor-months and explain why summing exit timestamps instead would be wrong.

Solution. Sum each \(Y_i-L_i\): for A–F, \(2+3+5+7+9+10=36\); for G–L, \(3.5+4.5+6.5+6.5+3.5+3.5=28\); total 64 compressor-months. Summing exit timestamps would implicitly add unmonitored pre-entry months for G–L, overestimating observed exposure. This exposure is not automatically the sum of true lifetimes: six units have unknown failure times after censoring, and some machines existed before enrolling. The 64 figure is a directly observed monitoring quantity, not a population durability parameter.

33. Exercise 5: Identify left versus interval censoring

Question. Unit P is first seen broken at month 4, with no earlier reliable inspection. Unit Q is functioning at month 3 and found broken at month 7. Classify the records and write the bounds.

Solution. P has left censoring: \(T_P\le4\), assuming an origin before 4. The engineer knows an upper bound but not the exact event time. Q has interval censoring: \(3<T_Q\le7\). The engineer knows both lower and upper bounds. Recording P and Q as failures at 4 and 7 confuses discovery with occurrence; right-censoring either one would assert survival past a bound contradicted by the detected failure. The appropriate columns for such data must preserve bounds and censoring type, not force the pair (Y,δ) designed for ordinary right censoring.

34. Exercise 6: Distinguish truncation and censoring

Question. A registry includes only machines functioning on its opening day. Machine R failed before that day and has no row; S entered on that day and is last observed working three months later. Which mechanism applies to each?

Solution. R is excluded by the sampling or eligibility mechanism: it is left-truncated relative to the registry and has no observed endpoint in this dataset. S is included and contributes three event-free months before observation stops, so its endpoint is right-censored if no target event was seen. These can occur within the same registry but describe different problems. Adding a censored row for R with an invented duration would manufacture a subject and information that the registry does not possess. Simply counting only the machines surviving to registry entry also changes the population represented.

35. Exercise 7: Assess the censoring assumption

Question. Compare a prescheduled database shutdown at month 12 with machines disappearing because high vibration triggers emergency transfer. Is independent censoring equally plausible?

Solution. A common predetermined closure is often more compatible with independent censoring, although calendar and cohort effects still require thought. Emergency transfers caused by warning signs can reveal elevated impending failure risk, making the censoring informative. Even if both rows receive δ=0, their mechanism differs. A correct study retains censoring reasons, measured warning indicators, and dates. We cannot verify conditional independence just by inspecting the failure column. The substantive question is whether censoring predicts unobserved failure beyond recorded covariates. If it does, ordinary survival methods relying on independent censoring may be biased.

36. Exercise 8: Spot immortal-time bias

Question. An analyst calls a machine “upgraded” if it ever received a preventive retrofit in the first six months and then compares failure rates from day 0. Identify the flaw and give a defensible redesign.

Solution. A machine classified as upgraded must survive until its actual retrofit date; early failures cannot later receive it. Assigning all its earlier failure-free time to the upgraded group creates immortal-time bias. One approach is to define upgrade as a time-varying exposure, counting pre-upgrade time in the not-yet-upgraded state. Another is a justified landmark comparison among units still eligible at month 6, while acknowledging its changed target population. The choice depends on the causal question; neither option turns a nonrandom maintenance decision into a randomized trial. The lesson is to align grouping and eligibility with when the information became true.

37. Exercise 9: Calculate a descriptive observed-event rate

Question. There are six confirmed failures and 64 observed compressor-months. Compute the event-count-to-exposure ratio with units. Is it a monthly risk probability?

Solution. Divide events by exposure: \(6/64=0.09375\) events per compressor-month. The units are essential because the denominator measures time rather than number of compressors. This descriptive rate is not the statement that every device has 9.375% chance of failure in the next month. Converting a rate into a probability would require a model of event intensity, population comparability and time dependence, and the current data include delayed entry and censoring. A correct report labels it a crude rate of observed events in monitored exposure, and reserves more advanced survival or hazard inference for later guides.

38. Exercise 10: Handle an apparent competing event

Question. Compressor V is permanently destroyed in a fire at month 4 without mechanical failure. A technician labels it ordinary right-censored at month 4. What needs clarification?

Solution. The engineer must define the target estimand. If the question is the real-world probability of a first mechanical failure while the original compressor remains in service, permanent destruction prevents later occurrence of that event and is a competing event, not simply an unobserved future failure. If the estimand is a hypothetical failure-time process in the absence of the fire, censoring may be one component of a hypothetical analysis but requires strong, explicit assumptions. Preserve a separate cause code and time. A binary event indicator alone cannot safely express both mechanisms. Avoid treating competing events as independent ordinary censoring by default.

39. Exercise 11: Transfer to a clinical follow-up cohort

Question. Twelve patients join a clinic study on different dates. The target is first documented relapse after beginning therapy. Some relapse, some are last seen relapse-free, and others died without relapse. Describe the minimum data schema and the methodological warnings.

Solution. Define time zero as therapy initiation, not automatically clinic registration; preserve treatment start, clinic entry, last verified relapse-free date, first verified relapse, withdrawal date, death date and record closure. Convert all dates to the same time-from-therapy scale. For a patient enrolled after treatment began, record delayed entry and check eligibility without manufacturing pre-entry follow-up. Relapse-confirmed patients have δ=1 with observed event time; relapse-free patients whose records end may be right-censored at their last confirmed visit. Death without relapse may be a competing event depending on the estimand. If relapse is discovered only between visits, retain interval bounds. The data dictionary must distinguish unknown status from missing laboratory values. An honest report states the population, exclusions, selection mechanism and censoring assumptions.

40. Exercise 12: Final understanding test and independent construction

Question. A client subscription begins on day 0. A record starts at day 10, shows activity until day 35 and closes without observed cancellation. Another client cancels on day 12 after enrolling at day 5. An external transaction record proves a third client's cancellation happened between days 15 and 20. Build the correct observation descriptions; explain one danger of omitting entry or censoring type.

Solution. Client one has \((L,Y,\delta)=(10,35,0)\), so 25 directly monitored days and \(T>35\) relative to the common clock if confirmed active through 35. The second has \((5,12,1)\) and 7 monitored days ending in cancellation. The third has interval-censored time satisfying \(15<T\le20\), with its enrollment timing recorded separately; it cannot be assigned δ=1 at day 20 as an exact cancellation unless a reliable event timestamp exists. Omitting entry would add unobserved early risk time for the first two clients. Treating the third as exact at 20 would replace an interval with invented precision. The transferable reasoning is to specify event, origin, eligibility, observed bounds, censoring/truncation mechanism, and units before choosing an estimator. That is the conceptual foundation this guide was designed to build.
