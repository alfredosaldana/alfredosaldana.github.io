01. Why decomposition is worth learning

A time series is not merely a column of numbers. Its order is information: the same 24 sales figures rearranged into a different month order would suggest a different business history. In Guide 62 we learned to identify a time index, frequency, missing periods and information available at a forecast origin. Here we ask a new question: what different patterns are combined inside the observed series? A store may grow steadily, experience a predictable August surge, react to a temporary promotion, and still exhibit measurement noise. A single line hides these mechanisms. Decomposition aims to make them distinguishable without pretending that the components are directly observed.

We will distinguish trend, a long-run change in level; seasonality, a pattern associated with a recurring calendar position; cycle, a broader fluctuation without a necessarily fixed 12-month calendar; and remainder, whatever the chosen model does not explain. These are descriptive terms, not guarantees about their causes. A gradual increase could reflect more customers, rising prices, or a change in how revenue is recorded. A December peak could reflect holidays, an accounting practice or both. Separating shapes does not identify causal mechanisms.

The practical objective is to answer questions that raw sales obscure. Is business growing after allowing for typical calendar peaks? Is the August increase unusually strong or simply normal? Are residual anomalies concentrated around policy changes? Does one need to modify the data before comparing adjacent months? Throughout the guide we will make the components reconstruct the observed values, and also explain why perfect reconstruction by itself does not prove the decomposition is meaningful.

02. Prerequisites and the meaning of each symbol

The essential prerequisite is Guide 62: a time index, equally spaced monthly observations, a forecast origin and awareness of missing timestamps. We will review the arithmetic mean, fractions and logarithms before relying on them. We assume an introductory command of algebra and Calculus I; no previous time-series model is required. Let \(t\) denote a month index, \(t=1,\ldots,24\), and let \(Y_t\) denote observed monthly sales, measured in thousands of items sold per month. The index increases by one for each successive month. Thus \(Y_1=91\) means 91 thousand items during January of the first year, not 91 dollars or 91 thousand items at an instant.

Write \(T_t\) for a trend or trend-cycle level, \(S_t\) for seasonal contribution, and \(R_t\) for the unexplained remainder. All three are in thousands of items for an additive representation. A hat, as in \(\widehat T_t\), means an estimate built from observations; it is not guaranteed to equal the true latent pattern. The seasonal period \(m=12\) means that the position within a year repeats every 12 monthly timestamps. It does not mean the observed sales are identical in two Januaries: the level and remainder may change.

Before decomposition, verify the dataset is monthly, has 24 ordered distinct timestamps, contains no missing month and uses comparable business definitions. If a month covers different store locations or an altered counting rule, that structural change requires discussion. For our teaching case all months are complete and comparable, and the dataset is explicitly synthetic. A two-year sample supports arithmetic exercises but is too short to establish that an apparent seasonality will persist in the real world.

03. The observed series: all 24 months

Consider a fictional retailer. The synthetic observations below are deliberately constructed so their underlying pieces can be checked independently. They are not retail observations from a government agency or commercial company. Both years use the same month positions, and all quantities are thousands of items sold within the stated month. The table includes the observed \(Y_t\) and, for instructional verification, the generating trend \(T_t\), the seasonal term \(S_t\), and the remainder \(R_t\). In real applications those latter three columns would be unknown and must be estimated.

The generating rule is \(T_t=100+2t\); the monthly seasonal offsets in January-to-December order are \((-12,-8,-4,0,4,8,12,16,8,0,-8,-16)\), with a sum of zero. The first-year remainder vector is \((1,-1,0,2,-2,1,-1,0,2,-2,1,-1)\); in year two the signs reverse. Thus every observed row is obtained using \(Y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t\). A positive seasonal value means more units than the modeled trend level in that month; a negative one means fewer. This is an accounting decomposition, not a claim that changing the month itself causes the exact offset.

Notice how much information the raw totals conceal. Year one sums to 1,356 thousand units, while year two sums to 1,644 thousand, a 288-thousand-unit increase. The monthly overall average is \(3,000/24=125\) thousand units. August rises from 132 to 156, but those two values combine increasing trend, a shared seasonal boost and no remainder in this particular constructed month. If we report only the 24-unit August change, we have not yet stated how much should be attributed to trend versus seasonality.

04. What the line plot does and does not show

A first plot places time along the horizontal axis and monthly sales along the vertical axis; the line is not a collection of independent randomly drawn points. Look for an upward baseline, repeating late-summer high points and recurring low points near the end or start of each year. The pattern is visually suggestive, but a visual impression alone does not establish its period or statistical reliability. A single exceptional promotion could produce a false seasonal-looking peak, and a change in store footprint could imitate trend.

A useful discipline is to describe what is visible before invoking a method: sales in the second year tend to exceed their counterparts from the first year, and a roughly 12-month shape repeats. Only then ask which numerical representation could separate those features. A line chart is also a measurement audit: gaps, irregular spacing, level discontinuities and isolated spikes should be questioned before applying an automatic decomposition. In this example none of the 24 months is missing; in an actual dataset a blank month must not silently be assigned sales of zero.

05. Trend is not the same as a forecast

A trend summarizes a sustained movement in the level of a series over the interval examined. For our constructed dataset the known trend is \(T_t=100+2t\), so the level increases by two thousand units per month of the time index. At \(t=1\), \(T_1=102\); at \(t=13\), \(T_{13}=126\); at \(t=24\), \(T_{24}=148\). The change from January year one to January year two is \(126-102=24\) thousand units, entirely consistent with 12 monthly increments of two thousand.

That known formula is an unusual luxury: in observed business data trend must be inferred and depends on the chosen smoothness and sample. A smooth trend curve need not be linear; it might flatten, accelerate or turn. 'Trend' here often means trend-cycle, because a smoother cannot necessarily distinguish a slow economic cycle from a long-term change. The statistical decomposition will not tell us whether growth will continue after month 24. Extrapolating the line to month 36 would require a forecasting assumption not justified by mere reconstruction of the past.

The slope's units matter. The difference \(T_{24}-T_1=46\) thousand items describes change between month indices, not a 46% increase. A percentage uses a denominator, such as \((148-102)/102\approx45.10\%\) for the generating trend's relative growth across these endpoints. Percent changes in the observed \(Y_t\) may be very different because the months have different seasonal positions. Always name the starting and ending months before interpreting a level difference.

06. Seasonality, cycles and calendar effects

Seasonality is a recurring association with a known position in a calendar or operational cycle. With monthly data, a yearly seasonal pattern has \(m=12\) positions. In our example August is associated with \(S_8=+16\) thousand units; December with \(S_{12}=-16\). The difference between these seasonal contributions is 32 thousand items. This describes a modeled contrast between months at the same trend level, not a randomized comparison of two identical business conditions.

A cycle is conceptually different: a multiyear boom and recession may fluctuate around a long-run level without repeating on an exact 12-month schedule. A single series may contain business cycles, a repeating calendar season, and one-off shocks simultaneously. With two years of monthly observations, assigning an 18-month wave to a 'cycle' would be highly uncertain. A trend smoother may absorb much of it into the trend-cycle component.

Calendar effects require particular care. Some months have 28, 29, 30 or 31 days, and a business with a weekday/weekend imbalance can show monthly totals differing merely because of trading-day counts. Leap years, movable holidays and promotions can shift demand relative to fixed calendar months. Monthly seasonality alone does not automatically correct those effects. Before interpreting a retail peak, consider both total units and units per trading day, but do not substitute a rate for a total without explaining the new denominator and business question.

07. An additive accounting identity

The additive decomposition writes \(Y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t\). It is an identity once the three components are defined appropriately; the statistical challenge is estimating components that have a useful interpretation. The terms have matching units, because adding a percentage to thousands of items would be dimensionally invalid. For month 8 our table gives \(T_8=116\), \(S_8=16\), \(R_8=0\), so \(Y_8=116+16+0=132\) thousand items. For month 17, \(T_{17}=134\), \(S_{17}=4\), \(R_{17}=2\), hence \(Y_{17}=140\).

For month 1 the reconstruction is \(102-12+1=91\), while for month 13 it is \(126-12-1=113\). These examples help distinguish a seasonal effect from the observed value: two Januaries share the same constructed seasonal contribution but not the same total. An unusually high remainder is also not necessarily an error. It can be genuine demand that is not captured by the chosen trend-seasonal representation.

The model is not uniquely determined by the observations without constraints. If we add 5 to every trend estimate and subtract 5 from each seasonal estimate, reconstruction remains exactly the same. A common additive identification convention forces the seasonal effects over a complete period to average zero. Our twelve seasonal offsets sum to zero, establishing a convenient scale for separating average level from recurring deviations. This convention helps interpretation but does not make the components automatically true.

\[Y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t\]

08. Multiplicative decomposition and scale

When peak-to-trough changes grow approximately in proportion to the series level, a multiplicative form may be more appropriate: \(Y_t=T_t\times S_t\times R_t\). Here \(S_t\) and \(R_t\) are dimensionless factors, while \(T_t\) has units of sales. A seasonal index 1.20 means 20% above the reference trend, while 0.80 means 20% below it, assuming the remainder factor is one. This interpretation differs sharply from adding 20 thousand units.

Consider an independent four-quarter illustration of units sold: reference trend levels \((100,110,120,130)\) thousand items and seasonal factors \((0.8,1.0,1.2,1.0)\). If the remainder factor is one, observed values are \((80,110,144,130)\). In the first quarter the seasonal reduction is \(100(0.8-1)=-20\) thousand; if the trend instead equaled 200, the same seasonal factor would imply \(200(0.8-1)=-40\) thousand. Thus the amplitude scales with the level. Four factors average one arithmetically in this illustration, a conventional normalization; other decompositions may normalize differently.

The multiplicative representation requires strictly positive values when taking logarithms, and a zero monthly count creates a serious modeling issue rather than an invitation to silently take \(\log 0\). Compare additive and proportional alternatives by looking at how the seasonal amplitude changes over time, considering domain meaning and checking the residual pattern. Our main 24-month example was constructed additively: the August contribution remains +16 at both trend levels. That is a reason to prefer additive units for this particular case.

KEY EQUATION · terms and assumptions explained
\[Y_t=T_t\times S_t\times R_t\]

09. Logarithms turn products into sums

For positive sales \(Y_t\), the logarithm obeys \(\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\). Consequently, a multiplicative decomposition \(Y_t=T_tS_tR_t\) becomes \(\log Y_t=\log T_t+\log S_t+\log R_t\). This is why log transformations can help visualize stable percentage seasonal changes as approximately stable differences on the log scale. The logarithm is natural log unless otherwise stated. No logs are used for the exact additive teaching dataset, because logging it would change the scale and the component interpretation.

A small numerical example makes the distinction tangible. Suppose a baseline is 100 thousand items and a seasonal factor is 1.20. Then \(Y=120\) thousand and \(\log Y-\log 100=\log(1.20)\approx0.18232\). The log difference is about 0.1823, while the exact relative increase is \(e^{0.18232}-1=0.20\), or 20%. Saying the increase is 18.23% is a first-order approximation, not the exact calculation. When seasonal effects are large, that approximation matters.

Back-transforming a mean on the log scale needs caution: exponentiating an estimated average log sales does not generally equal the arithmetic average of sales, because the exponential function is nonlinear. For our level-by-level decomposition, exponentiating a predicted log value produces a positive quantity interpretable as a geometric-scale prediction, not automatically an unbiased level mean. These nuances are not reasons to avoid logs; they are reasons to name what is estimated.

KEY EQUATION · terms and assumptions explained
\[\log Y_t=\log T_t+\log S_t+\log R_t\]

10. The moving-average idea from first principles

An arithmetic moving average smooths rapid changes by averaging neighboring observations. A trailing three-month average at month 3 uses months 1–3: \((91+95+102)/3=96\) thousand items, and at month 4 it uses months 2–4: \((95+102+110)/3\approx102.333\). The window moves one month forward, so neighboring averages share observations. Smoothing reduces some local variation, but it does not reveal a unique underlying trend or prove that the residuals are random.

A centered three-month average for month 2 instead uses months 1–3 and equals 96. Its position is different: it assigns the average to the middle month. A centered window at month 23 would need observations around month 23, some of which would be unavailable if the forecast origin were month 22. This distinction will become critical in retrospective forecast evaluation. The centered calculation is valid descriptive analysis when those later months are genuinely available, but not a valid real-time feature at an earlier origin.

Window width determines what is smoothed away. Three months is usually too short to remove a 12-month retail cycle, because seasonal peaks remain influential. A 12-month average gives each month position one representation and tends to cancel stable annual offsets. But an even-length moving average is initially located between months, so careful centering is needed. A longer window smooths more strongly and responds more slowly to sudden changes; it can also erase meaningful turning points.

11. An even period: why 2 × 12 centering is necessary

For monthly annual seasonality, the classical method uses the centered \(2\times12\) moving average. First calculate consecutive averages of 12 observations, located at half-integer times such as 6.5 and 7.5. The first 12 months total 1,356, so their mean is \(1,356/12=113\), located at \(t=6.5\). Months 2 through 13 total \(1,378\), so their average is \(1,378/12=114.83333\), located at \(t=7.5\). Averaging those two values gives the centered trend estimate at integer month 7:

\(\widehat T_7^{\mathrm{CMA}}=(113+114.83333)/2=113.91667\) thousand items. Our known generating trend at month 7 is 114. The -0.08333 difference reflects the remainder structure and the fact that these are estimated components. It is not a spreadsheet malfunction. If the remainder happened to cancel perfectly across both adjacent windows, the centered average would match the generating trend more closely.

Equivalently, a \(2\times12\) centered moving average is a weighted 13-month average with weights \(1/24\) at the two endpoints and \(1/12\) for each of the eleven internal observations. The total weight is \(2(1/24)+11(1/12)=1\). The half-weights are not a cosmetic convention: they position a symmetrical 12-month smoothing operation at an actual calendar month. This operation cannot supply values at the first six or last six months without additional assumptions or endpoint methods.

\[\widehat T_7=\tfrac12\left(\tfrac{1356}{12}+\tfrac{1378}{12}\right)=113.9167\]

12. Seasonal indices from detrended observations

After estimating \(\widehat T_t\) for the interior months, classical additive decomposition forms detrended deviations \(D_t=Y_t-\widehat T_t\). Values from the same calendar month are then averaged to estimate its seasonal contribution. In a long series, there will be several observed Januaries, several Februaries and so forth. In only two years with a 12-month centered smoother, some calendar months have very few usable detrended values. This edge limitation is a fundamental reason our example is better for understanding arithmetic than for claiming precise classical seasonal estimates.

We can verify the intended seasonal offsets independently because the synthetic generating trend is known. January of year one: \(91-102=-11\). January of year two: \(113-126=-13\). Their average is \((-11-13)/2=-12\), exactly the January seasonal effect. August: \(132-116=16\) and \(156-140=16\), giving 16. Across each month, the year-two remainder negates its year-one counterpart, so the average detrended offset recovers each generating seasonal contribution. This is a teaching oracle check, not a procedure available when the true trend is unknown.

The twelve recovered monthly values sum to zero, so no seasonal recentering is necessary. In actual classical decomposition we would subtract the mean of the twelve preliminary seasonal values to make their sum zero. If that mean were 0.5 thousand units, each raw index would be reduced by 0.5. Explain the adjustment: otherwise average seasonal effects would shift the estimated level, obscuring the trend-versus-seasonality distinction. The order is estimate trend, detrend, average by calendar position, normalize, and compute the remainder.

KEY EQUATION · terms and assumptions explained
\[\widehat S_{\mathrm{Jan}}=\frac{(91-102)+(113-126)}{2}=-12\]

13. Reconstruction and residual arithmetic

Once components are estimated, calculate the remainder as \(\widehat R_t=Y_t-\widehat T_t-\widehat S_t\). It records what remains under this chosen representation. With our known constructed components, at month 4 the remainder is \(110-108-0=2\), and at month 16 it is \(130-132-0=-2\). Reconstruction checks both signs: \(108+0+2=110\) and \(132+0-2=130\). At month 22, \(146-144-0=2\) and reconstructing produces 146.

The table's remainder values are intentionally small, but that design does not imply that residuals from real data will be independent, normally distributed or free of patterns. One should examine serial dependence, unusual peaks, level shifts and changes in variability. If residuals still display a clear annual rhythm, the seasonal component may be inadequate; if residuals drift upward, the trend estimate may be too rigid. A highly flexible decomposition can make residuals small by absorbing meaningful shocks, so a small in-sample residual is not a stand-alone quality criterion.

Do not confuse an estimated residual with a causal unexplained shock. For example, a temporary discount might raise actual demand, but a centered smoother could allocate part of that increase to trend, part to seasonal variation and part to remainder. The allocation depends on the method, bandwidth and sample endpoints. Even when each row reconstructs perfectly, uncertainty about the component assignments remains.

KEY EQUATION · terms and assumptions explained
\[\widehat R_t=Y_t-\widehat T_t-\widehat S_t\]

14. Seasonal plots and seasonal subseries

A seasonal plot overlays the 12-month progression for each year, allowing direct comparison of January with January and August with August. A seasonal-subseries plot groups values by month and shows their year-to-year variability. The first makes the annual shape visible; the second makes month-specific differences and dispersion easier to inspect. Because year two's trend is higher, raw overlay lines are vertically separated even when the seasonal contributions are identical. Detrending or comparing deviations from the annual level can clarify that distinction.

In the observed case August values are 132 and 156, a difference of 24; December values are 107 and 133, a difference of 26 because the remainder changes sign. A raw seasonal-subseries plot cannot by itself conclude that August's seasonal effect is +16; it mixes level growth and seasonality. A reader should ask whether the plot uses raw units, detrended differences, ratios to a trend or log-transformed values. Each answers a different question.

With only two annual repetitions, month-specific variability is barely identifiable. A large spread between two February observations might be trend, measurement change or an event rather than seasonal instability. A robust presentation pairs a plot with data provenance, the precise calendar aggregation and a warning about the short sample. Visualizations are evidence for questions, not replacements for those questions.

15. Distinguishing trend-cycle and seasonal components

In practice a trend smoother cannot uniquely decide whether a slow, roughly two-year wave is an economic cycle or a shift in long-run level. Thus many time-series tools label the smooth component trend-cycle. In our 24-month classroom series, the underlying trend is linear by construction. That is unusually simple; a real retail series may have an abrupt supply interruption, a recovery and a later plateau. A smoother with a long window may depict that history as one gently curved line, hiding important transitions.

Seasonality is more plausibly tied to calendar position if similar peaks appear over several years under consistent measurement. Nevertheless, repeated December spikes do not by themselves prove that December caused them. Advertising budgets, holidays and opening days may coincide with December. For statistical interpretation, distinguish a seasonal description from a causal explanation and distinguish a calendar index from a manipulable treatment.

It can help to ask a counterfactual diagnostic question: if the store were twice as large, would the August increment plausibly stay 16 thousand items or double in size? A constant absolute increment suggests additive units; a roughly constant percent premium suggests multiplicative structure. But without observations spanning different levels, the answer is often weakly identified. State uncertainty instead of forcing a confident model choice.

16. What STL means and what it actually estimates

STL stands for Seasonal-Trend decomposition using LOESS. LOESS (locally estimated scatterplot smoothing) fits local weighted regressions to trace smooth patterns without assuming the entire trend is a straight line. STL repeatedly separates seasonal structure from trend structure using smoothers and a low-pass step. Its output is conventionally \(Y_t=\widehat T_t+\widehat S_t+\widehat R_t\) on the scale to which STL is applied. The components are estimates, not additional observed columns.

STL is attractive when seasonal shape changes gradually rather than repeating exactly. A yearly August premium might grow as the business evolves; a fixed classical monthly index cannot capture that evolution, whereas an appropriately chosen STL smoother may. The user must still specify the seasonal period; for regularly spaced monthly observations with an annual cycle it is 12. Without at least enough repeated periods, seasonal estimation is unstable. STL does not automatically solve a missing-timestamp problem or know why a holiday shifted.

Three smoothing controls are especially important: the seasonal window, trend window and low-pass window. They govern how flexible the estimated pieces can be. Short windows can follow quick fluctuations but risk fitting noise; long windows suppress noise but may miss real shifts. A robust option can reduce the influence of isolated outliers on smooth component estimates, though it does not decide whether an outlier represents an error. Official documentation describes these controls; none guarantees a causal, future-proof decomposition.

17. A transparent STL verification, not a black box

For our synthetic series, a reader can reproduce an STL fit in a numerical package using the complete table, period=12, seasonal=13, and a documented choice of trend window. Such an implementation is useful to check shapes and component reconstruction, but we should not promise that STL will recover our generating \(T_t\) or \(S_t\) exactly. LOESS is a smoothing procedure with finite-sample behavior, edge estimates and settings-dependent allocations. Its output may differ from the classroom oracle and from classical moving averages.

To verify the identity, take one month and add the three reported STL components: \(\widehat T_t+\widehat S_t+\widehat R_t\) should return \(Y_t\) within numerical precision, because the remainder is defined accordingly. To verify adequacy, ask harder questions: are seasonal values stable enough to be interpreted; are residuals systematically related to month; and do different reasonable smoothing windows materially alter the findings? Reconstructing is necessary but insufficient for a useful decomposition.

It is also crucial to document the available data. A full-sample STL fit over 24 months may use information from months after an earlier historical date. Its retrospective trend at month 12 is not what an analyst could have estimated at the close of month 12. If the purpose is describing the entire completed history, that is acceptable. If the purpose is simulating a real forecast issued at month 12, it is leakage. The correct test uses only information available at that origin.

18. Decomposition graphics and reconstruction

A component figure should contain a time series panel for observed sales, one for the estimated trend, one for seasonality and one for remainder, with an explicit common time index. On our oracle illustration the constructed trend rises from 102 to 148, the seasonal terms range from -16 to +16 and the remainder remains between -2 and +2. These ranges answer concrete questions: which component accounts for most change in baseline, which months are consistently above baseline and how much remains unexplained under the generating rule.

The axes require care. If all panels use a single vertical scale, the small remainder may become almost invisible; if each uses a different scale, a tiny remainder might appear as large as the trend movement. A caption should state that the panels have different ranges and that the graphical heights are not directly comparable. Plot units remain thousands of items for all additive components. The reconstruction is local: at any month sum the three values at the same time index; do not sum a January seasonal effect with the August trend.

A comparison chart may also show seasonally adjusted sales \(A_t=Y_t-\widehat S_t\). In our oracle case August month 8 becomes \(132-16=116\), while August month 20 becomes \(156-16=140\). The 24-unit difference is then attributable to trend and remainder under the construction. Seasonal adjustment is useful for adjacent-month comparisons, but the adjusted values are not counterfactual sales that would necessarily occur if seasons could be removed.

\[Y_{20}=140+16+0=156\quad\Rightarrow\quad A_{20}=156-16=140\]

19. Scale-sensitive model choice with actual numbers

Suppose two years of a fictional online store show winter sales of 80 and 160 thousand items, and summer sales of 120 and 240 thousand items. The summer-to-winter absolute differences are 40 and 80 thousand, while the relative ratios are \(120/80=1.5\) and \(240/160=1.5\). These observations are more consistent with a proportional seasonal pattern than a fixed +40-thousand effect. This is evidence about scale, not proof of a multiplicative data-generation process.

Contrast a second store with winter sales of 80 and 160 thousand and summer sales of 120 and 200 thousand. The absolute changes remain 40 thousand in each year, while the ratios decline from 1.5 to 1.25. Additive seasonality may be more interpretable there. In both cases the comparison is descriptive and imperfect because we have not separately estimated a within-year trend or controlled for other changes. A third year or product-level information could change the assessment.

Be careful with zeros and negatives. A multiplicative seasonal factor cannot be applied transparently to a series containing negative net revenue. Adding a constant before logging shifts interpretation, and logging plus one is not neutral for small counts. If the business metric can be zero, one might model counts directly or use an alternative transformation with explicit justification. The main lesson is to choose a representation that respects domain, units, positivity and amplitude.

20. Calendar adjustments before decomposition

Monthly totals depend on the number and composition of trading days. A hypothetical month with 100 thousand items over 20 trading days averages 5 thousand per trading day; another with 110 thousand over 22 trading days also averages 5 thousand per trading day. The second has a larger total but not a larger observed daily rate. Decomposing the totals without checking exposure may assign some trading-day difference to seasonal effects.

Calendar correction has several forms. One can model counts with an exposure variable, compute appropriately defined rates, or include known calendar regressors when evaluating a forecasting system. Each alters what the outcome means. A day-normalized rate and a monthly total are not interchangeable, and dividing by calendar days when stores close on Sundays might itself create bias. Record dates, holidays, trading days and the target business quantity before adjusting.

STL alone does not automatically account for movable holidays or day-of-week composition. A holiday falling in late March one year and early April another can create apparent month-to-month irregularity, even if the underlying holiday effect is consistent. Calendar effects are therefore a diagnostic issue parallel to seasonal decomposition rather than something to declare solved by decomposing a monthly curve.

21. Leakage: descriptive smoothing versus real-time analysis

Imagine an analyst issues a forecast immediately after month 12. The only observations available are \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_{12}\). A centered \(2\times12\) moving average at month 12 would require future months, so it cannot be a feature available at the forecast date. Likewise, a trend reconstructed later from all 24 months can incorporate demand shocks that had not yet occurred. Using such a component as an input to a historical forecast would make performance seem better than real deployment permits.

Define the forecast-origin information set \(\mathcal F_{12}\) as everything genuinely known after month 12. A valid predictor for month 13 must be a function of \(\mathcal F_{12}\), not of \(Y_{13}\) or later. A trailing average from months 1–12 is legal; the centered average at month 12 is not. A seasonal model fitted only to months 1–12 is legal in principle but has just one annual cycle and therefore a very fragile seasonal estimate. Legal use does not mean high quality.

This distinguishes two tasks. Retrospective description: after all 24 months are observed, use both-sided smoothing to explain the historical shape, labeling it as retrospective. Historical forecast evaluation: at each rolling origin, fit or update the decomposition using only the training history available then, produce predictions and compare to held-out future outcomes. One cannot fix leakage simply by hiding future labels while keeping precomputed full-sample components.

KEY EQUATION · terms and assumptions explained
\[\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}=f_h(\mathcal F_t)\]

22. Why the method does not create future seasonal knowledge

It is tempting to take seasonal values estimated from the full dataset and extend them forward as if they were known future constants. That may be a forecasting assumption, but it is not a mathematical implication of decomposition. An estimated August effect of +16 thousand could weaken, strengthen or disappear after a policy change. The same caution applies to extrapolating a smoothed trend: smoothing past observations and forecasting future observations are distinct activities.

A sound forecasting workflow defines a horizon and compares candidate methods against simple baselines that would have been available at the time. For monthly data, a seasonal-naive baseline predicts next August from last August; this does not require sophisticated decomposition, though it relies on past seasonal stability. A decomposition-based forecast can be useful, but evaluating it requires repeated origin-based tests and a documented way to project the trend and seasonal components. Those forecasting methods are treated more fully in later guides.

No result in this guide licenses a claim that an apparent upward trend must continue. Separating trend and seasonality makes analysis clearer; it does not eliminate economic shocks, model uncertainty or data quality problems.

23. Revisions, endpoints and stability

A centered smoother revises earlier estimates when new data arrive. At month 18 the analyst has more future neighbors around month 12 than at month 12, and by month 24 may obtain a different smoothed estimate. This is called an endpoint or revision problem, not necessarily an implementation error. If a report compares 'trend estimates as originally published' to 'trend estimates revised later', it should retain the relevant data vintages and computation settings.

Short samples intensify boundary sensitivity. Our data contain only 24 months, so a 12-month seasonal smoother consumes a large share of available observations. At the start and end of the sample the classical centered 12-month trend cannot be calculated by the symmetric formula; software may omit it, extrapolate, or use a different smoother. Those choices affect the apparent turn at the endpoint. If the business asks whether growth slowed this month, endpoint uncertainty deserves prominent disclosure.

A useful sensitivity analysis recomputes a decomposition using a reasonable alternative window and compares the conclusions, not just the curves. If the calendar-adjusted August effect changes sign under modest smoothing variations, a confident business recommendation is not justified. Keep the distinction between a stable descriptive pattern and one that depends heavily on modeling choices.

24. A reproducible method-selection workflow

Start with the time index: verify frequency, units, gaps, duplicates and measurement changes. Then visualize raw values by time and by seasonal position. Ask whether the seasonal period is operationally plausible and whether there are enough repeated cycles. Consider additive versus multiplicative scale by checking whether the size of peaks tracks the baseline. Do not let a software default choose the business meaning.

Next select an explicit method—classical seasonal indices, centered moving averages or STL—and record period and smoothing choices. Compute components, reconstruct several observed values, inspect remainder and investigate large anomalies. Check endpoint behavior, missing periods and calendar exposure. If results will be used for historical forecasting, restart the procedure within each training window rather than reusing full-sample components. Finally write a decision narrative in ordinary units: what is growing, what repeats, what remains uncertain and what could change the interpretation.

The workflow answers an applied question. Suppose the shop asks whether August's high sales require emergency inventory expansion. An August value 156 in the second year looks large, but its seasonally adjusted value is 140, which equals the known trend level at month 20. Under the constructed model, it is not an unexpectedly positive shock. That does not make 156 units irrelevant for inventory: the actual seasonal peak still affects capacity planning. Different decisions legitimately emphasize different components.

\[F_S=\max\!\left(0,1-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(R_t)}{\operatorname{Var}(S_t+R_t)}\right)\]

25. Seasonal strength is not the same as statistical significance

A decomposition may report a numerically large seasonal panel, but 'large' depends on the units and on the remaining variation. A simple descriptive summary compares the remainder variance with the variance of seasonal plus remainder. One common strength measure is \(F_S=\max\{0,1-\operatorname{Var}(R_t)/\operatorname{Var}(S_t+R_t)\}\). It is bounded between zero and one by construction and is descriptive, not a p-value. With a perfectly stable seasonal shape and tiny remainders, the ratio of variances is small and strength approaches one. With a weak seasonal pattern and large residual fluctuations, the ratio is larger. This interpretation presumes that the components were computed coherently.

Our generating seasonal effects are as large as 16 thousand while the residuals range only from -2 to +2, suggesting a large descriptive seasonal signal. However, the 24 months are constructed to have those components. Reporting a tiny p-value from them as evidence that real retailers experience the same seasonality would be meaningless. A strength metric also depends on the decomposition method and smoothing choice: if the smoother assigns more variation to the seasonal component, it mechanically changes both numerator and denominator. A strong fitted seasonality can coexist with poor future predictions after a market change.

A business audience may need an effect stated in plain units rather than one strength score. For example, the August seasonal offset is 16 thousand units and the December offset is -16, so the modeled seasonal contrast is 32 thousand. For operations, that difference is directly actionable; the abstract variance ratio may be less informative. Describe both size of the pattern and reliability of the evidence, without confusing them.

26. Why an exact decomposition is not unique

Suppose someone proposes \(Y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t\). For any time-varying sequence \(c_t\), the definitions \(T'_t=T_t+c_t\), \(S'_t=S_t-c_t\) and \(R'_t=R_t\) reconstruct the same observations exactly. Therefore perfect fit cannot establish a unique economic trend or seasonal cause. To define an interpretable decomposition, methods impose structure: trend must be sufficiently smooth, seasonal behavior repeats or varies slowly with month, and remainder records what the first two components do not absorb.

The familiar constraint that monthly additive seasonal indices sum to zero removes one constant ambiguity, but does not eliminate every possible tradeoff between a flexible seasonal curve and a flexible trend curve. STL controls that tradeoff using explicit smoothing choices. Classical centered moving averages impose another type of structure: they strongly attenuate stable annual seasonality in the trend estimate. Those assumptions are methodological choices, not discoveries produced by the observed totals.

Try a concrete check. For month 8, \(116+16+0=132\). A different representation \(121+11+0=132\) reconstructs the same observation, but calling the shifted value 121 the long-run level is not justified unless the new representation is coherent across all months. This is why local row-level verification and global pattern checks must be combined. Arithmetic establishes consistency; smoothness, repeatability, domain context and out-of-sample behavior establish whether the representation is worth using.

27. Missing months, interpolation and false seasonal patterns

In this example all 24 monthly observations exist. If February of year two were missing, the correct initial data table would carry a missing marker and preserve its timestamp. Dropping that row and reindexing subsequent observations would mistakenly assign March to February's seasonal position, corrupting every month label afterward. Filling the gap with zero would instead create an artificial winter trough and might inflate estimated seasonal amplitude. Neither operation is innocuous.

Interpolation may be reasonable for a documented measurement outage, but a value estimated from both neighboring months may use future information. For example, a linearly interpolated February using January and March year two cannot be known at the close of January year two. It may be defensible for reconstructing a completed historical descriptive plot, but invalid as a feature for a January-origin forecast. The imputed value should be identified as estimated and accompanied by sensitivity checks using other plausible methods.

Seasonal adjustment itself can become circular if missing values are filled using seasonality learned from all months, then the same filled values are cited as evidence for that seasonality. A robust workflow first records which values were observed, estimates missing values only when necessary under stated assumptions, and reports whether substantive conclusions change. Data provenance is as important as the equation on a highlighted card.

28. When a trend line and seasonal index disagree

Consider a sudden store closure that reduces observed sales by 30 thousand in an otherwise ordinary September. A strongly smoothed trend may treat most of that decline as a residual, whereas a flexible trend may partially absorb it as a local change of level. An algorithm that allows seasonality to change rapidly could also distort September's seasonal effect. None of these allocations independently identifies the closure. Without event metadata, the decomposition is underdetermined.

A practical audit records known store openings, closures, price changes and recording conventions beside the plot. Then compare decompositions with a long and a short trend window. If the September shock disappears from the residual under the shorter window but the implied growth narrative becomes unstable, explain both effects. Choose smoothness based on the question: isolating unusual events may favor a stable baseline, while tracking abrupt operational changes may require a more responsive trend.

Do not optimize a smoothing window only to make the graph attractive or residuals small. The right outcome is a transparent description that survives reasonable perturbations and does not conceal known interventions. When the underlying business process changes fundamentally, segmenting the history or modeling the event explicitly may be more honest than forcing one unchanged seasonal pattern across the entire dataset.

29. Turning decomposition into clear statistical communication

A concise managerial explanation can follow five parts. First, name the population, timestamps and units: '24 monthly synthetic retail sales totals, in thousands of items.' Second, state the method and period: 'additive decomposition, annual period 12, trend estimated using a specified smoother.' Third, give reconstructible numbers: 'in month 20, 140 trend plus 16 seasonal and zero residual equals 156 observed.' Fourth, distinguish description from projection: 'the calculation explains this observed month but does not establish next August's sales.' Fifth, state critical limitations: two annual repetitions, possible calendar effects, dependence and endpoint revisions.

The word 'adjusted' deserves special care. Seasonally adjusted month-20 sales of 140 are not a new observed transaction total. They are a derived quantity formed by subtracting a modeled effect from 156. If accounting staff need reconciled invoices, they should use 156; if analysts compare underlying levels across differently seasonal months, 140 may be useful under the model. Reporting both reduces the chance that a derived series will replace the original operational measure.

Finally, visual presentation should distinguish estimated and observed information. Use solid markers for observed sales, a separate legend for smooth components, and an explanatory caption for residuals. On a mobile device the graph can scale without sacrificing legibility, while tables may scroll horizontally within their own frame. The purpose of visual design is to support the student's ability to check a number, not merely to make the page colorful.

30. Exercise 1 — Reconstruct a monthly observation

Problem. In month 19, the synthetic store's trend is 138, seasonal component 12 and remainder 1, all measured in thousands of units. Find observed sales and explain what 'seasonal +12' means. Check against the source table.

Complete solution. The additive identity is \(Y_{19}=T_{19}+S_{19}+R_{19}\). Substituting each value gives \(138+12+1=151\) thousand items. The table lists 151, so reconstruction agrees. The seasonal contribution indicates a recurring modeled July increase of 12 thousand over the trend level at this month. It does not mean sales increased by 12% or prove that July itself causally generated 12 thousand units. If we overlooked the remainder, we would predict 150 rather than observe 151.

31. Exercise 2 — Calculate and locate a moving average

Problem. Compute the trailing three-month average at month 4 and a centered three-month average at month 4 from the complete observations. Explain why the two numbers belong to different information sets.

Complete solution. The trailing value uses months 2–4, \(Y_2=95\), \(Y_3=102\), \(Y_4=110\), giving \((95+102+110)/3=307/3\approx102.333\). The centered value uses months 3–5: \(Y_3=102\), \(Y_4=110\), \(Y_5=112\), giving \(324/3=108\). Both have units of thousands of items per month averaged over three months. The centered result needs month 5, which would be unknown immediately after month 4; the trailing result is available at that origin. Neither is 'better' without specifying whether the task is retrospective smoothing or real-time prediction.

32. Exercise 3 — Verify 2 × 12 centering

Problem. Find the centered 12-month smoothing estimate assigned to month 7. Include the two intermediary moving averages, their locations and a comparison to the generating trend.

Complete solution. Months 1–12 sum to 1,356, so their 12-month mean is 113 at center 6.5. Months 2–13 sum to 1,378, so their mean is 114.83333 at center 7.5. The symmetric average \((113+114.83333)/2=113.91667\) is assigned to month 7. The generating trend is \(T_7=100+2(7)=114\), yielding an estimation error of \(-0.08333\). This small difference can be traced to constructed remainder terms. The result is not a legal month-7 real-time feature because months 8–13 contribute to it.

33. Exercise 4 — Estimate a seasonal index with an oracle trend

Problem. Use both Januaries and the known trend formula to estimate January's additive seasonal effect, assuming opposite remainders average to zero. State why this is a verification rather than an ordinary estimation procedure.

Complete solution. January year one has \(Y_1=91\) and \(T_1=102\), giving a detrended deviation \(-11\). January year two has \(Y_{13}=113\) and \(T_{13}=126\), giving \(-13\). Average: \((-11-13)/2=-12\) thousand items. The true teaching seasonal contribution is -12, and the equality follows because the remainders +1 and -1 cancel. In practice the generating trend is not available, so replacing it with an estimated trend introduces uncertainty and possibly different seasonal estimates.

34. Exercise 5 — Additive versus proportional amplitudes

Problem. Two hypothetical years have winter/summer levels \((80,120)\) and \((160,240)\) thousand items. Calculate additive differences and ratios. Which seasonal description is more stable and what remains unidentified?

Complete solution. Year one has \(120-80=40\) thousand and \(120/80=1.5\). Year two has \(240-160=80\) thousand and \(240/160=1.5\). The ratio remains constant whereas the absolute increase doubles, so a proportional seasonal model is the more stable description among these two simple alternatives. It does not prove that the ratio was caused by calendar season. Within-year trend and other demand changes remain unmeasured; additional data and context are needed.

35. Exercise 6 — Work with logarithms correctly

Problem. A multiplicative seasonal factor is 1.25. Calculate its natural-log contribution and recover the exact percentage effect. Explain why treating log points as percent can be inaccurate.

Complete solution. The log effect is \(\log(1.25)\approx0.22314355\). Exponentiate to recover \(e^{0.22314355}=1.25\), so the original-scale effect is \((1.25-1)100=25\%\). Multiplying the log difference by 100 gives 22.314%, a first-order approximation that understates the exact 25% by about 2.686 percentage points. It is a log-point change, not an exact percentage change; the error grows with effect size.

36. Exercise 7 — Diagnose the seasonal-adjusted series

Problem. Calculate seasonally adjusted sales for month 8 and month 20 using the known seasonal component. Interpret their difference and explain whether adjustment removes all variation.

Complete solution. The August seasonal contribution is 16 in both years. Therefore \(A_8=Y_8-S_8=132-16=116\) and \(A_{20}=156-16=140\), a difference of 24 thousand items. The generating trend rises by \(T_{20}-T_8=140-116=24\), and both August remainders equal zero, so the adjusted difference happens to match trend growth exactly. In other months a remainder remains; seasonal adjustment removes the specified seasonal term, not anomalies, measurement errors or all uncertainty.

37. Exercise 8 — Identify a leakage mechanism

Problem. A researcher forecasts month 13 at the close of month 12, but supplies a centered moving-average estimate at month 12 computed afterward using months 7–18. Is the backtest valid? Propose a correction.

Complete solution. The backtest is invalid: the moving average incorporates \(Y_{13},\ldots,Y_{18}\), outcomes that would not have existed in the information set at month 12. The result is a feature with future leakage, even if the forecast model never directly sees a future target column. Rebuild features from months 1–12 only, such as a trailing 12-month mean, and refit any decomposition within the historical training window. Repeat this procedure at each subsequent forecast origin. Report any limited-seasonal-history problem honestly.

38. Exercise 9 — Read a residual, not a cause

Problem. An STL decomposition reports observed sales 150, trend 136 and seasonal 10 at a month. Compute the remainder. Can you conclude the extra items were generated by a promotion?

Complete solution. Rearranging gives \(\widehat R=150-136-10=4\) thousand items. Reconstruction \(136+10+4=150\) verifies arithmetic. The remainder is positive relative to this estimated decomposition, but it does not identify a promotion as the causal source. Stock availability, pricing, weather, recording errors or random variation could contribute. A causal answer would need stronger design, covariates or experimental evidence, not just decomposition.

39. Exercise 10 — Evaluate calendar exposure

Problem. One month sold 100 thousand units over 20 trading days and the following sold 110 thousand over 22 trading days. Compare totals and daily rates, and explain the consequence for seasonality.

Complete solution. Monthly totals differ by \(110-100=10\) thousand or 10% relative to 100. Average trading-day rates are \(100/20=5\) thousand/day and \(110/22=5\) thousand/day. The rate does not change. If the target is monthly total inventory demand, the 10-thousand difference remains important. If the target is per-trading-day productivity, the apparent increase disappears. A seasonal decomposition of raw totals may partly reflect trading-day composition, so the question and denominator must be documented.

40. Exercise 11 — Investigate edge uncertainty

Problem. A 24-month series is decomposed with a symmetric 13-point weighted smoother. How many observations can receive a fully symmetric result without extrapolation, and why should the end-of-sample trend be treated cautiously?

Complete solution. A centered 13-point window requires six neighbors on each side. Therefore only months 7 through 18, inclusive, can receive fully symmetric estimates: \(18-7+1=12\) interior months. Months 1–6 and 19–24 lack enough observations on one side. Software may return missing trends, use asymmetrical rules or extrapolate; those choices make endpoint estimates more sensitive. A chart displaying a smooth trend at month 24 without describing the endpoint method can imply unjustified certainty.

41. Exercise 12 — Full transfer: make a decision and disclose limits

Problem. You are advising the fictional retailer at the end of month 24. Year two August sales were 156 thousand units, trend 140, seasonal contribution 16 and remainder zero. The director asks whether August demand is an unexplained surge and whether the company should base next August's staffing on the projected trend. Deliver a complete recommendation.

Complete solution. First reconstruct: \(140+16+0=156\), so August's observed high value is fully accounted for by trend plus expected seasonality under the constructed model. Seasonally adjusted sales equal \(156-16=140\). Therefore it is incorrect to describe this August as a positive remainder shock. Second, capacity decisions must still respect the actual 156-thousand peak; removing seasonal variation for analysis does not remove summer demand operationally. Third, projecting next August's trend requires a separate forecast and validation, not merely taking the slope 2 forever. Use only information available at each forecast origin; compare seasonal-naive and more elaborate forecasts with rolling evaluation. Fourth, check calendar exposure and new store openings before trusting the seasonal effect. Finally disclose that our evidence is a 24-month synthetic example with fixed seasonal offsets, and cannot certify what a real retailer will sell. The recommendation is to prepare for recurrence while treating next year's level and amplitude as uncertain and monitoring new evidence.

42. Exercise 13 — Averages can conceal a calendar problem

Problem. A retailer sold 96 thousand units in a 24-trading-day month and 90 thousand in a 20-trading-day month. Compute daily rates. Explain why a seasonal decomposition of monthly totals alone could suggest a misleading slowdown.

Complete solution. The first daily rate is \(96/24=4\) thousand units per trading day. The second is \(90/20=4.5\) thousand per trading day. Monthly total sales fell by six thousand, yet daily selling rate increased by 0.5 thousand, or \(0.5/4=12.5\%\). A seasonal analysis of totals may attribute a lower monthly outcome to seasonal weakness even though the available sales days were fewer and per-day demand rose. The business must decide whether it is analyzing total units needed for inventory or demand intensity per day before choosing the metric.

43. Exercise 14 — A decomposition sensitivity audit

Problem. For the same October observation \(Y=146\), decomposition A estimates trend 144 and season 0, while decomposition B estimates trend 142 and season 3. Calculate both remainders and explain why one is not automatically superior.

Complete solution. Under A, remainder \(146-144-0=2\) thousand. Under B, remainder \(146-142-3=1\) thousand. Each reconstructs 146 exactly. B has the smaller residual for this month, but this does not prove that B is better overall: it may have absorbed a transient fluctuation into its seasonal estimate or distorted neighboring months. Compare component smoothness, stability across calendar repetitions, other residuals and the intended use. The ability to reproduce a row is a necessary arithmetic check, not model selection by itself.

44. Exercise 15 — Forecast origin and a tempting feature

Problem. A researcher evaluates a January year-two forecast issued after December year one. A seasonal index for January is computed using January observations from both years. Why is this a problem? Give a permissible alternative and one caution.

Complete solution. The index uses January year-two data, which is the very future period the forecast is intended to predict. It leaks outcome information into the predictor and invalidates the simulated history. An alternative is to estimate seasonality solely from the first twelve months and apply it to January year two, or use the observed January year-one value as a seasonal-naive baseline. With only one annual cycle, those estimates are fragile, and the evaluation should report that limitation rather than claim reliable seasonal learning.

45. Exercise 16 — Interpret a multiplicative remainder

Problem. A multiplicative decomposition reports observed sales 132 thousand, trend 110 thousand and seasonal factor 1.20. Find the remainder factor and interpret it. Contrast this with an additive remainder of zero.

Complete solution. The multiplicative identity gives \(R=Y/(TS)=132/(110\cdot1.20)=132/132=1\). The factor one means no remaining proportional deviation after the chosen trend and seasonality terms. Under an additive representation, if trend 110 and seasonal contribution 22 thousand, the remainder would be \(132-110-22=0\). Both describe no remainder at this observation, but the seasonal quantities live on different scales: factor 1.20 versus +22 thousand. We cannot substitute the number 1.20 as an additive component or 22 as a multiplicative index.

46. Student understanding test and final synthesis

A first-time student should now be able to reproduce one row from components, calculate a trailing mean, explain why the even 12-month average needs centering, estimate a seasonal index using a known trend for verification, identify when a multiplicative description has more stable proportional amplitude, and distinguish retrospective smoothing from features usable at a forecast origin. These are separate capabilities: correct arithmetic alone is not understanding if the reason for choosing the operation is unexplained.

To check your understanding, explain aloud why \(Y_{20}=156\) does not mean a 156-thousand-unit trend; why the seasonal adjustment is \(140\); why an estimated trend from full-sample STL cannot simply be used in a month-12 backtest; and why a strong holiday pattern does not prove holiday causation. If any statement is unclear, revisit the indicated worked example and reproduce the numbers without looking at the final answer. This is an editorial self-check, not evidence of a trial with actual students.

The principle for the next guide is equally important: decomposition helps us describe the pieces of a time series, whereas stationarity and differencing ask how its statistical properties change across time. The concepts are related but not interchangeable. Here the essential achievement is disciplined interpretation: separate patterns, verify reconstruction, preserve units and prevent future information from entering the past.
