1. The question behind survival functions

A maintenance manager can ask three different questions about a compressor: What fraction is expected to remain functional after four months? How rapidly do failures occur among compressors still working at that age? How much event intensity has accumulated by four months? These are not three labels for the same quantity. They are three different descriptions of a distribution of time until failure. This guide connects them with algebra, derivatives, integrals, and observable decisions. The event is the first mechanically confirmed failure after a new compressor enters operation; the clock is months of operating age. A repair followed by another failure would require a recurrent-event model, which is beyond our present contract.

A small teaching model will make the calculations checkable. Assume that comparable new compressors have independent failure times governed by an Exponential distribution with rate \(\lambda=0.20\) per month. This is a synthetic parametric assumption, not a value measured at a factory. In a real study one would first define the population, assess censoring and selection, and estimate or justify the failure-time model. We will deliberately keep model probability separate from empirical evidence. A numerical answer such as 0.45 means a model probability for the specified event and period, not an observed percentage of actual equipment.

Our route follows the logical dependency of the concepts. Begin with the cumulative probability of failure, then obtain survival and density. Only afterward introduce the conditional instantaneous hazard, integrate it into cumulative hazard, and use those relationships to calculate conditional probabilities and summary lifetimes. Finally we test the interpretation against an increasing-hazard model and against incomplete follow-up. This order matters: the same symbol can otherwise be mistaken for a count, rate, probability, or guarantee.

2. Prerequisites, notation and units

You need the probability ideas from Guide 11, continuous densities and integrals recalled in Guide 18, and the event/censoring distinctions of Guide 72. A derivative measures a local rate of change; an integral accumulates a rate over an interval. For a continuous nonnegative random variable \(T\), a single exact time has probability zero, while an interval can have positive probability. We use \(t\) for a particular nonnegative elapsed time and \(u\) for a dummy integration variable. A parameter is a feature of a population model; an estimator from censored data is a separate statistical object.

The five functions are \(F(t)=P(T\le t)\), the cumulative distribution function (CDF); \(f(t)\), its probability density function (PDF); \(S(t)=P(T>t)\), survival; \(h(t)\), the instantaneous conditional event rate; and \(H(t)\), cumulative hazard. For a continuous distribution, \(P(T=t)=0\), so strict versus non-strict inequalities at one fixed time do not change probabilities. We use a conventional capital \(H\) for cumulative hazard of the event time, not for a censoring-time distribution; other textbooks sometimes use different letters.

Keep units visible. Time \(t\) is in months, \(F\) and \(S\) are unitless probabilities, \(f\) and \(h\) have units of inverse months, and \(H\) is dimensionless. For example, an instantaneous hazard of \(0.20;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\) cannot be compared directly with a 20% probability until an interval and an appropriate conversion have been specified. The density \(f(t)\) likewise is not the probability of failing at the exact instant \(t\): integrating density over a nonzero interval gives a probability. Every later calculation will be checked against these units.

3. CDF and survival are complements

The event \(T\le t\) means the first failure has happened by age \(t\). The event \(T>t\) means the first failure has not happened by that age. These events are mutually exclusive and exhaust the possibilities, hence \(F(t)+S(t)=1\). This is a probability identity, not a special assumption about exponential lifetimes. It holds for any nonnegative lifetime distribution; what differs between models is the shape of the curves.

For our Exponential model, the waiting-time CDF is \(F(t)=1-e^{-0.20t}\) for \(t\ge0\). Consequently \(S(t)=e^{-0.20t}\). At \(t=4\) months, \(S(4)=e^{-0.8}\approx0.449329\) and \(F(4)=1-0.449329=0.550671\). The two results add to 1, a direct verification. Among a hypothetical large population following this assumed distribution, about 44.93% remain event-free beyond month four and about 55.07% experience first failure by month four. These values do not describe any actual fleet.

A survival function is nonincreasing, lies between zero and one, and for an initially event-free population typically starts at \(S(0)=1\). More generally, \(S(t)\) can have jumps (discrete event times) or a positive limiting value if some items never experience the specified event. Those cases require care when using density-based derivatives. This guide derives calculus formulas for continuous, absolutely continuous survival laws, indicating where the assumptions enter.

KEY EQUATION
\[F(t)=P(T\le t),\qquad S(t)=1-F(t)\]

4. From the CDF to the PDF

For an absolutely continuous lifetime, \(F(t)=\int_0^t f(u),du\) and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives \(F'(t)=f(t)\) where the derivative exists. Since \(S=1-F\), differentiation produces \(S'(t)=-f(t)\). That minus sign is meaningful: survival decreases because probability mass moves into the already-failed category as the time horizon advances. A density is nonnegative and integrates to one when the event eventually happens with probability one.

Differentiate the Exponential CDF carefully: the derivative of the constant 1 is zero, while the derivative of \(-e^{-0.20t}\) is \(-(-0.20)e^{-0.20t}\). Therefore \(f(t)=0.20e^{-0.20t};\mathrm{month}^{-1}\). At month four the density is \(0.20(0.449329)=0.089866;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\). The density's vertical value is not an 8.99% point probability. Over the interval \((4,5]\), probability equals \(F(5)-F(4)=e^{-0.8}-e^{-1}\approx0.081450\). Multiplying the density at four by one month would give only a rough local approximation, not the exact one-month probability.

The integral also checks normalization: \(\int_0^\infty0.20e^{-0.20t}dt=[-e^{-0.20t}]_0^\infty=1\). The endpoint \(e^{-0.20t}\) approaches zero, so the integral contributes 0 minus -1. If this limit did not vanish, we would need to ask whether some probability is concentrated at infinite survival. That is not our Exponential model.

KEY EQUATION
\[f(t)=\frac{dF(t)}{dt}=-\frac{dS(t)}{dt}\]

5. Derive the hazard from a conditional probability

Take a compressor that is known to remain functional at age \(t\). Ask for the chance that its first failure occurs during the following small interval of length \(\Delta t>0\). The required probability is \(P(t<T\le t+\Delta t\mid T>t)\). By the conditional-probability rule, its numerator is \(F(t+\Delta t)-F(t)\) and its denominator is \(S(t)\), assuming \(S(t)>0\). To obtain a rate rather than an interval probability, divide by \(\Delta t\) and let that interval shrink to zero.

This gives \(h(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow0}{P(t<T\le t+\Delta t\mid T>t)/\Delta t}=f(t)/S(t)\). The limit uses the derivative \(F'(t)=f(t)\). Hazard thus has inverse-time units. It is not the unconditional density and is not the cumulative probability of failure. The denominator explicitly limits attention to those not yet failed: if many compressors have already failed, the conditional rate among survivors can remain unchanged even though the absolute number of failures falls.

For the Exponential case, substitute \(f(t)=0.20e^{-0.20t}\) and \(S(t)=e^{-0.20t}\). The common exponential factor cancels, giving \(h(t)=0.20;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\) at every \(t\ge0\). The result explains the model's constant conditional intensity. This is not a claim that 20% of survivors fail in every one-month interval: the exact fraction is \(1-e^{-0.20}\approx0.18127\). The difference is a core Student Understanding Test for this topic.

KEY EQUATION
\[h(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow0}\frac{P(t<T\le t+\Delta t\mid T>t)}{\Delta t}=\frac{f(t)}{S(t)}\]

6. Why a hazard rate can exceed one

A rate is a probability per unit of time in a vanishing interval, not a probability with a fixed upper bound of one. Suppose another modeled component has constant hazard \(h(t)=2.0\) failures per unit year while still operational. Saying “hazard equals 2” is mathematically valid and does not assert a 200% probability of failure. The chance of failure over the next quarter year, conditional on survival, is \(1-e^{-2(0.25)}=1-e^{-0.5}\approx0.39347\). Over the next full year it is \(1-e^{-2}\approx0.86466\), both legitimate probabilities.

A useful dimensional check converts the rate into months: \(2.0;\mathrm{year}^{-1}\) equals \(2/12\approx0.16667;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\). The probability over three months must remain unchanged when the same physical duration is used: \(1-e^{-(2/12)3}=1-e^{-0.5}\). Treating a yearly rate of 2 as a monthly rate of 2 would change the scientific model and dramatically exaggerate predicted failures. The variable and the rate must use compatible units before multiplying them.

The local approximation \(P(t<T\le t+\Delta t\mid T>t)\approx h(t)\Delta t\) only holds for sufficiently short intervals and a smooth hazard. If \(h(t)\Delta t\) exceeds one, that is evidence the local approximation is being used outside its useful range, not evidence that probability laws fail. Exact interval probabilities rely on differences of cumulative hazard, derived next.

7. Derive cumulative hazard with Calculus I

Define \(H(t)=\int_0^t h(u),du\). The integrand has inverse-month units and \(du\) has month units, so \(H(t)\) is dimensionless. It is cumulative intensity, not a cumulative probability. When \(h(u)\ge0\), cumulative hazard cannot decrease. Starting from \(h(t)=f(t)/S(t)\) and \(f(t)=-S'(t)\) gives \(h(t)=-S'(t)/S(t)\). Recall the chain rule: the derivative of \(\log S(t)\) is \(S'(t)/S(t)\) when \(S(t)>0\). Therefore \(h(t)=-d\log S(t)/dt\).

Integrate both sides from 0 to \(t\): \(\int_0^t h(u)du=-[\log S(t)-\log S(0)]\). For a new unit known to be event-free at the origin, \(S(0)=1\), hence \(\log S(0)=0\). We obtain the exact identity \(H(t)=-\log S(t)\) and rearrange to \(S(t)=e^{-H(t)}\). This derivation assumes a continuous absolutely continuous lifetime and positive survival over the time range. It is a proof under stated conditions, not a numerical approximation.

Apply it to constant hazard: \(H(t)=\int_0^t0.20du=0.20t\). At four months, \(H(4)=0.8\) and \(S(4)=e^{-0.8}=0.449329\). Notice that 0.8 is not an 80% probability. The failure probability is \(1-e^{-0.8}=0.550671\). At ten months \(H(10)=2\) is perfectly possible, while the cumulative failure probability remains below one: \(1-e^{-2}\approx0.864665\).

KEY EQUATION
\[H(t)=\int_0^t h(u)\,du=-\log S(t),\qquad S(t)=e^{-H(t)}\]

8. The conversion map and its checks

For an absolutely continuous lifetime, all five functions describe the same probability law whenever the relevant derivatives and integrals exist. From \(S\) we get \(F=1-S\), \(f=-S'\), \(H=-\log S\), and \(h=-S'/S\). From \(h\) we instead integrate to get \(H\), exponentiate with a negative sign to recover \(S\), and multiply \(hS\) to obtain the density. From \(f\) we integrate to obtain \(F\) and use its complement for survival. The operations are not interchangeable without regard to signs: integrating a hazard gives cumulative hazard, not the CDF.

Use the Exponential case as a five-way checksum at \(t=4\): \(F=0.550671\), \(S=0.449329\), \(f=0.089866;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\), \(h=0.200000;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\), \(H=0.800000\). The first pair sums to one. Multiplying \(hS\) gives \(0.2(0.449329)=0.089866\), the density. The negative logarithm of survival is \(-\log(0.449329)\approx0.800000\), the cumulative hazard. The negative derivative of survival is again the density. Four independent paths agree to rounding.

What if a proposed curve increases from survival 0.60 to 0.70 as time advances? It cannot be a valid ordinary survival curve for the same event and population because the event \(T>t_2\) is a subset of \(T>t_1\) when \(t_2>t_1\). What if a proposed hazard is negative? It cannot represent an event intensity. Such basic range and monotonicity checks are often more informative than verifying algebra alone.

9. Compute interval and conditional probabilities exactly

Suppose an operator asks: “What is the chance that a compressor fails between ages two and five months?” Without any condition, this is \(P(2<T\le5)=F(5)-F(2)=S(2)-S(5)\). Exponential survival gives \(e^{-0.4}-e^{-1}=0.670320-0.367879=0.302441\). That 30.24% refers to a new member of the modeled original population. It includes the fact that some devices fail before month two and therefore are not candidates to fail in the specified interval.

Now change one word: “Among compressors still working at two months, what fraction fail by month five?” Conditional probability gives \(P(2<T\le5\mid T>2)=[S(2)-S(5)]/S(2)\). Substitute \(0.302441/0.670320=0.451188\). Equivalently, \(1-S(5)/S(2)=1-e^{-0.6}\). The conditional figure is larger because the denominator is now the surviving population at month two. No statistical paradox occurs: the population at risk has changed.

The most general continuous-model formula is \(P(a<T\le b\mid T>a)=1-\exp[-{H(b)-H(a)}]\) for \(0\le a<b\) with \(S(a)>0\). The difference \(H(b)-H(a)=\int_a^b h(u)du\) is the accumulated intensity over the interval, not the accumulated intensity since birth. This is why subtracting cumulative hazard values is the correct operation for conditional risk. The formula holds even when the hazard changes with time.

KEY EQUATION
\[P(a<T\le b\mid T>a)=1-\exp\!\left[-\{H(b)-H(a)\}\right]\]

10. The exponential memoryless property

The Exponential model yields \(S(a+b)/S(a)=e^{-0.20(a+b)}/e^{-0.20a}=e^{-0.20b}\). Thus, conditional on reaching age \(a\), the remaining modeled survival over the next \(b\) months has the same form as survival over the first \(b\) months from a new start. This is the memoryless property. The algebra cancels the factor for previous exposure and makes the remaining distribution independent of how long the equipment has already survived.

For example, among devices working at month 10, the probability of surviving an additional three months is \(e^{-0.20(3)}=e^{-0.6}\approx0.548812\). It is identical to the model probability for a new compressor surviving its first three months. The probability of first failure during those three months is \(1-0.548812=0.451188\). These statements are unconditional on any new information about health, repairs, and operating load; such information could destroy memorylessness in a more realistic model.

Memorylessness is not a universal fact about equipment. Mechanical wear may increase the conditional hazard with age, and early manufacturing defects may produce a decreasing hazard as vulnerable units fail first. A constant Exponential hazard is useful for teaching conversion identities and for some random-failure applications, but it is an assumption to test, not a default conclusion from smooth survival plots. The contrast with increasing hazard later in this guide will show precisely which calculations change.

11. Median survival from the model

The population median lifetime \(m\) is a time satisfying \(P(T\le m)=0.5\), equivalently \(S(m)=0.5\) for a continuous law with a unique median. A median is not a hazard, an observed failure age, or a predicted exact date for a particular compressor. It divides the modeled distribution into two equal probability halves. In the Exponential example solve \(e^{-0.20m}=0.5\): take logarithms, giving \(-0.20m=\log(0.5)=-\log2\). Divide by \(-0.20\) to get \(m=\log2/0.20\approx3.465736\) months.

Check the answer using the distribution rather than the algebraic solution alone. The cumulative hazard at that time is \(H(m)=0.20(3.465736)=0.693147\), which equals \(\log2\). Therefore \(S(m)=e^{-0.693147}\approx0.5\) and \(F(m)\approx0.5\). The units are months because a dimensionless logarithm has been divided by a rate expressed in inverse months. A phrase such as “median is 3.47” without the time unit obscures the scientific interpretation.

For a general hazard, median survival solves \(H(m)=\log2\) whenever this equation has an appropriate solution. The curve need not have a unique median, and empirical follow-up may end while estimated survival remains above 0.5. In that situation the data do not identify an unrestricted median by simply forcing a curve to cross half. The distinction between a model-implied median and an empirically estimable median becomes important with right censoring.

KEY EQUATION
\[m=\frac{\log 2}{\lambda}=3.465736\;\mathrm{months}\]

12. Expected lifetime as area under survival

For a nonnegative lifetime \(T\) with finite mean, \(E[T]=\int_0^\infty S(t)dt\). This identity is sometimes called the tail-integral formula. To see the intuition, represent a realized lifetime as the area of a unit-high strip from 0 to \(T\): \(T=\int_0^\infty I(T>t)dt\), where \(I\) is one while the compressor is still event-free and zero afterward. Taking expectations and interchanging the nonnegative integral and expectation gives \(E[T]=\int_0^\infty P(T>t)dt\). The interchange is justified by a standard result for nonnegative integrands; that full theorem is cited rather than proved in this Calc I guide.

For \(S(t)=e^{-0.20t}\), integrate directly: \(E[T]=\int_0^\infty e^{-0.20t}dt=[-5e^{-0.20t}]_0^\infty=0-(-5)=5\) months. The area beneath survival has units of months because height is a dimensionless probability and horizontal width is measured in months. The mean is larger than the median 3.466 months because the Exponential distribution is right-skewed: long-lived units extend its upper tail.

Do not confuse mean time to failure with a sample average computed after deleting censored observations. The latter estimates the mean among observed failures, a selected subset, and may be strongly biased for the original event-time distribution. Nor can one always estimate the unrestricted mean from a survival curve followed only until a finite last date: unobserved tail area remains unknown without additional modeling assumptions. A restricted mean, introduced next, often makes a more defensible estimand.

KEY EQUATION
\[E[T]=\int_0^\infty S(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{\lambda}=5\;\mathrm{months}\]

13. Restricted mean and the interpretation of follow-up

Choose a substantively meaningful horizon \(\tau\), such as six months of preventive-maintenance planning. The restricted mean survival time is \(\operatorname{RMST}(\tau)=E[\min(T,\tau)]=\int_0^\tau S(t)dt\). Its interpretation is the expected event-free time accumulated during the first \(\tau\) months, not the unrestricted lifetime. For the Exponential model at \(\tau=6\), integrate to obtain \([1-e^{-0.20(6)}]/0.20=[1-e^{-1.2}]/0.20\approx3.49403\) months.

Why is the answer less than the unrestricted mean of five months? A compressor surviving past month six contributes at most six months to the restricted quantity, whereas its total lifetime continues beyond that cutoff. The difference between five and 3.494 is the uncounted expected tail time beyond month six. A useful identity is \(E[T]=\operatorname{RMST}(\tau)+\int_\tau^\infty S(t)dt\) when the mean exists. For the Exponential case the tail is \(e^{-1.2}/0.20\approx1.50597\), and \(3.49403+1.50597=5\).

A restricted mean can be appropriate when studies observe a common finite horizon and when comparing policy-relevant uptime. It is not automatically identifiable with arbitrary censoring: sufficient follow-up and suitable assumptions about the censoring process are still needed. Its advantage is that it asks a bounded, clearly specified question rather than requiring a guess about extremely long lifetimes. Reporting \(\tau\), units, event definition, and uncertainty is essential when RMST is estimated from actual data.

KEY EQUATION
\[\operatorname{RMST}(\tau)=\int_0^\tau S(t)\,dt\]

14. A complete numerical conversion table

A common learning error is to calculate each quantity in isolation and never verify that they describe one model. The accompanying table evaluates one Exponential distribution with rate \(0.20;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\) at four time points. Its columns are generated using \(S=e^{-0.2t}\), \(F=1-S\), \(f=0.2S\), \(h=0.2\), and \(H=0.2t\). 15. Increasing hazard: a Weibull alternative

The Exponential model assumes age does not alter the conditional intensity. To see an alternative, consider the synthetic Weibull survival model \(S(t)=\exp[-(t/5)^2]\) for \(t\ge0\), where five months is a scale parameter and the shape parameter is two. The scale is not the mean or the median; those are derived separately. Differentiate \(S\) using the chain rule: \(S'(t)=-(2t/25)\exp[-(t/5)^2]\). The density is \(f(t)=-S'(t)=(2t/25)S(t)\), and dividing by survival yields \(h(t)=2t/25;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\).

Now the instantaneous hazard increases linearly with age. At \(t=1\), it is \(0.08;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\); at \(t=5\), it is \(0.40;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\). Cumulative hazard is \(H(t)=(t/5)^2\), so \(S(5)=e^{-1}\approx0.367879\). The probability of failure by five months is \(0.632121\). The model still obeys \(f=hS\) and \(S=e^{-H}\); only the age pattern of the rate has changed.

Compare conditional failure from month five to six: \(1-\exp[-(H(6)-H(5))]=1-\exp[-(36/25-25/25)]=1-e^{-0.44}\approx0.35596\). The Exponential model's next-one-month probability was \(1-e^{-0.20}\approx0.18127\) at every age. Neither model is “correct” merely because one produces more failures; actual validation needs representative event-time observations, censoring information, and relevant diagnostics. The Weibull example shows how the same calculus adapts to nonconstant hazard.

KEY EQUATION
\[S(t)=e^{-(t/5)^2},\qquad h(t)=\frac{2t}{25},\qquad H(t)=\frac{t^2}{25}\]

16. Weibull median and mean: distinct calculations

For Weibull survival \(S(t)=e^{-(t/5)^2}\), the median solves \((m/5)^2=\log2\). Since time is nonnegative, take the positive square root: \(m=5\sqrt{\log2}\approx4.16277\) months. Check by substituting: \(H(m)=(m/5)^2=\log2\), so \(S(m)=1/2\). The correct branch of the square root matters; negative time has no meaning for this lifetime measured from new operation.

The mean requires an integral, not simply the scale five. By the tail-integral identity, \(E[T]=\int_0^\infty e^{-(t/5)^2}dt\). Substitute \(x=t/5\) and \(dt=5dx\) to obtain \(5\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}dx\). The Gaussian half-integral is \(\sqrt{\pi}/2\); proving its exact value generally requires an additional two-dimensional integration argument outside the Calc I prerequisite. We cite that standard result and obtain \(E[T]=5\sqrt{\pi}/2\approx4.43113\) months, consistent with the Weibull-Gamma expression \(5\Gamma(1+1/2)\).

Both figures differ from the Exponential results of median 3.46574 and mean five months, despite both using the number five somewhere in their parameterization. A scale parameter alone does not fix a median or mean across distribution families. State the family, the parameterization, and its units whenever reporting a numerical survival summary. With a different shape, the Weibull hazard may be decreasing, constant, or increasing, and these summaries shift accordingly.

17. Piecewise hazard and accumulation across regimes

Real operations may shift after an inspection or a change in load. A simple piecewise model illustrates how to integrate such a rate without pretending to fit an advanced survival model. Suppose \(h(t)=0.10;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\) for \(0\le t<3\) and \(h(t)=0.30;\mathrm{month}^{-1}\) for \(t\ge3\). The rate changes at month three; whether this represents age, policy, or load is a modeling assumption. For \(t\le3\), cumulative hazard is \(0.10t\). For \(t>3\), split the integral at the boundary: \(H(t)=\int_0^3 0.10du+\int_3^t0.30du=0.30+0.30(t-3)\).

At month five, \(H(5)=0.30+0.30(2)=0.90\) and \(S(5)=e^{-0.90}\approx0.406570\). The five-month cumulative failure probability is \(1-0.406570=0.593430\). Conditional on surviving three months, failure in the next two months has probability \(1-\exp[-(H(5)-H(3))]=1-e^{-0.60}\approx0.451188\). Do not use a constant-rate formula with 0.30 for all five months; that would erase the earlier lower-intensity period.

The survival curve remains continuous at the change point: \(S(3)=e^{-0.30}\) from either side. The hazard has a jump, which is allowed as a piecewise-defined instantaneous rate; the cumulative hazard stays continuous. For many applied systems, a piecewise hazard is a useful approximation to a smoothly changing reality. It still requires justification and validation; an abrupt artificial boundary is not automatically a real mechanical change.

KEY EQUATION
\[H(5)=\underbrace{0.1(3)}_{0\text{–}3}+\underbrace{0.3(2)}_{3\text{–}5}=0.9\]

18. Compare conditional risks across models

A maintenance decision depends on the time horizon and on which compressors are eligible for intervention. Consider the chance of first failure over the next month for an item still operational at age five. The Exponential model gives \(1-e^{-0.20}\approx0.181269\). Under Weibull shape two and scale five, the next-month probability is \(1-e^{-0.44}\approx0.355964\). Under the piecewise model after month three, it is \(1-e^{-0.30}\approx0.259182\). All three quantities refer to the same conditional event and same one-month horizon; therefore comparison is meaningful even though the model assumptions differ.

Compare instead the unconditional probability of failure before age five: Exponential \(1-e^{-1}\approx0.632121\); Weibull \(1-e^{-1}\approx0.632121\); piecewise \(1-e^{-0.9}\approx0.593430\). The first two models happen to give identical cumulative failure risk at five months, yet their next-month conditional risks at age five differ greatly. This is an excellent counterexample to the claim that matching \(S(5)\) establishes matching hazard. A single survival point cannot identify an entire hazard curve.

If an operator recommends an inspection solely because one hazard curve has a larger value at five months, the recommendation is incomplete. A proper decision considers inspection cost, failure consequence, model uncertainty, replacement effects, and how accurately the candidate models represent the observed fleet. The equations support quantified decision inputs; they do not establish causal effects of maintenance actions or prove which policy maximizes net benefit.

19. Survival under a change of time units

Suppose time measured in months is \(t_m\) and time measured in years is \(t_y=t_m/12\). They describe the same physical lifetime. To preserve the same survival law, \(S_y(t_y)=S_m(12t_y)=e^{-0.20(12t_y)}=e^{-2.40t_y}\). Thus the numerical hazard becomes \(h_y=2.40;\mathrm{year}^{-1}\), twelve times the value expressed per month. It is not a change in the equipment's risk; it is a change of measurement scale.

At four months, \(t_y=4/12=1/3\) year. The cumulative hazard in years is \(H_y(1/3)=2.40(1/3)=0.80\), exactly equal to \(H_m(4)=0.20(4)=0.80\). Survival is \(e^{-0.80}\) in either convention. Density also changes its numerical units: \(f_y(t_y)=12f_m(12t_y)\), since the probability mass of a physical interval is invariant under the change of variable \(dt_m=12dt_y\).

This gives a practical consistency test: hazard and density depend numerically on the time unit, while survival and cumulative hazard at the same physical time do not. A sentence such as “hazard increased from 0.20 to 2.40” is misleading if the only change was measuring months instead of years. The comparison should retain the inverse-time unit beside each rate. This matters particularly when combining engineering logs recorded in hours, warranty dates recorded in months, and reports summarizing annual failures.

KEY EQUATION
\[h_{\mathrm{year}}=12h_{\mathrm{month}}=2.4\;\mathrm{year}^{-1}\]

20. Why hazard ratio is not risk ratio

Suppose two hypothetical compressor types each follow an Exponential law, with constant hazards \(h_A=0.10\) and \(h_B=0.20\) per month. Their instantaneous hazard ratio is \(h_B/h_A=2\) for every age at which both hazards are defined. This compares rates conditional on event-free survival. It does not say the probability of failure by a particular month is twice as large for type B. At one month the risks are \(1-e^{-0.10}\approx0.095163\) and \(1-e^{-0.20}\approx0.181269\); the cumulative risk ratio is approximately \(0.181269/0.095163=1.9048\), not two.

At twelve months, type A has cumulative risk \(1-e^{-1.2}\approx0.698806\), while type B has \(1-e^{-2.4}\approx0.909282\). Their risk ratio is only about 1.3012 even though their constant hazard ratio remains 2. As time passes, both cumulative risks approach one, so their ratio approaches one. This is a mathematical reason why replacing hazard ratios with relative risks is wrong even in simple, perfectly specified models.

In real observational comparisons, a hazard ratio additionally raises problems of confounding, nonproportional hazards, and changing compositions of survivors. This guide uses the two-group calculation solely to explain estimands, not to teach or validate a Cox model. Whenever a published report presents a hazard ratio, ask which event, populations, time origin, time scale, follow-up, and modeling assumptions support it. Do not convert it into a statement about the probability of the event without additional work.

KEY EQUATION
\[\mathrm{HR}=\frac{h_B(t)}{h_A(t)}\ne\frac{F_B(t)}{F_A(t)}=\mathrm{RR}(t)\]

21. The role of censoring and delayed entry

So far every numerical probability was calculated from a specified population model. A real maintenance dataset records only how long each machine was observed and whether failure occurred before observation stopped. For right censoring the recorded duration is \(Y=\min(T,C)\) and the event indicator is \(\delta=I(T\le C)\), with a censoring time \(C\). When \(\delta=0\), we know \(T>Y\) but generally do not know the exact failure time. Dropping censored records, or recording their observation end as a failure, changes the estimand and can bias survival calculations.

With delayed entry, a compressor may become observable after it has already operated for some time. Risk sets must then respect the entry age; an item not yet under observation cannot contribute follow-up it did not provide. Guide 72 built these records before estimation, and Guide 74 will introduce Kaplan–Meier estimation and its uncertainty. The present guide instead focuses on how functions of an underlying lifetime distribution relate mathematically. These are complementary, not interchangeable, learning goals.

The identity \(S=e^{-H}\) is exact for an absolutely continuous distribution, but estimating either curve from censored samples involves additional assumptions and procedures. Independent censoring, representative sampling, time origin, and competing failures affect what data can identify. If only observed failures are used to fit an Exponential model, a seemingly precise \(\lambda\) may be an artifact of excluding long-lived censored devices. Model validation needs appropriate likelihood or nonparametric techniques rather than visual agreement alone.

22. What changes for discrete event times

The calculus identity \(h(t)=f(t)/S(t)\) describes a continuous hazard rate. If failures are only possible at discrete inspection rounds or the event time is genuinely integer-valued, we instead define a conditional event probability at a time point, often called a discrete hazard: \(q_k=P(T=k\mid T\ge k)\). This is a probability, lies between zero and one, and is not obtained by dividing by an infinitesimal interval width. Its survival relation is a product: \(P(T>k)=\prod_{j=1}^k(1-q_j)\) when time begins at integer one and the conventions are aligned.

For a simple teaching example with \(q_1=0.10\) and \(q_2=0.20\), the probability of remaining event-free after two rounds is \((1-0.10)(1-0.20)=0.9(0.8)=0.72\). The cumulative failure probability is \(1-0.72=0.28\). Simply adding \(q_1+q_2=0.30\) would overstate risk because the second-round probability is conditional on surviving the first. Likewise, exponentiating the negative sum \(e^{-0.30}\approx0.7408\) would be wrong for this discrete model.

One may define a log cumulative quantity \(-\sum\log(1-q_j)\) so that survival equals its negative exponential, but this differs from naively summing discrete conditional hazards. This distinction is pedagogically important when software reports estimates from tied or interval-recorded times. The continuous formulas are powerful, but their conditions must not be silently transferred to genuinely discrete event processes.

KEY EQUATION
\[P(T>k)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-q_j)\quad\text{(discrete time)}\]

23. Decision protocol, model uncertainty and causal limits

A useful reliability analysis separates four tasks. Define the event, eligible population, time zero, duration units, and whether machines can leave observation for reasons other than failure. Describe the observed follow-up and censoring; do not confuse it with the full distribution. Model the survival law only with evidence that can support its shape and rate. Decide by translating modeled quantities into a time horizon, cost and uncertainty that match the maintenance question. This sequence prevents a mathematically correct hazard calculation from answering the wrong business question.

A model's numerical rate is not necessarily a measured “true hazard.” A finite fleet produces uncertainty in estimated parameters; different fleets may have different exposure intensities, and systematic changes can invalidate constant-hazard assumptions. Confidence intervals, sensitivity analyses, diagnostics, and validation on later observations belong to empirical analysis. Our synthetic equations intentionally do not attach false frequentist coverage guarantees to chosen parameters. The chosen \(\lambda=0.20\) is stipulated, not estimated.

Finally, statistical survival associations are not causal maintenance effects. A company that inspects frequently may observe fewer failures because its machines differ in age, workload, or factory quality. The fact that two model curves differ does not establish that an inspection caused the difference. A defensible policy statement reports the modeled conditional failure probability over a clear time horizon, alternative model sensitivity, operational cost, and important unknowns. The final exercises require all of these distinctions rather than merely substituting numbers.

E0. Convert a survival function

Problem. A synthetic device follows \(S(t)=e^{-0.4t}\), with time in years. Obtain \(F\), \(f\), \(h\), and \(H\) and check their units.

Solution. Complement survival to obtain \(F(t)=1-e^{-0.4t}\). Differentiate: \(S'(t)=-0.4e^{-0.4t}\), hence \(f(t)=0.4e^{-0.4t};\mathrm{year}^{-1}\). Divide \(f/S\) to get \(h(t)=0.4;\mathrm{year}^{-1}\). Integrate this constant from zero: \(H(t)=0.4t\). Verify \(-\log S(t)=0.4t\) and \(f=hS\). Probabilities \(S\) and \(F\) are unitless, while \(H\) is dimensionless because years cancel against years inverse.

Interpretation and transfer. A reported \(H(2)=0.8\) is cumulative intensity, not an 80% failure probability. Compute the actual risk as \(1-e^{-0.8}\approx0.55067\). The exercise is complete only when all functions tell one consistent story.

E1. Unconditional versus conditional failure

Problem. With \(h=0.2\) per month, calculate (i) failure between months two and five in the original population and (ii) failure in that period among those surviving month two.

Solution. First calculate \(S(2)=e^{-0.4}=0.670320\) and \(S(5)=e^{-1}=0.367879\). For (i), subtract \(S(2)-S(5)=0.302441\). For (ii), divide by the population still at risk: \((0.670320-0.367879)/0.670320=0.451188\). Verify by cumulative hazard increments: \(H(5)-H(2)=1.0-0.4=0.6\) and \(1-e^{-0.6}=0.451188\).

Interpretation. The two answers differ because the second denominator excludes compressors that failed before month two. Calling both answers simply “the failure rate” would hide the essential condition. A proper report identifies the cohort, conditional entry age, and prediction horizon.

E2. From a time-dependent hazard to survival

Problem. Suppose \(h(t)=0.1t\) per month for \(t\) measured in months. Find \(H\), \(S\), \(f\), and the probability of first failure by month four.

Solution. Integrate the rate, not the survival function: \(H(t)=\int_0^t0.1u,du=0.05t^2\). Exponentiation gives \(S(t)=e^{-0.05t^2}\). Multiply \(hS\) for density: \(f(t)=0.1t e^{-0.05t^2}\). At four months \(H(4)=0.05(16)=0.8\) and \(F(4)=1-e^{-0.8}=0.550671\). As a derivative check, \(S'(t)=-0.1t e^{-0.05t^2}\), so \(-S'=f\).

Interpretation. This model has increasing hazard and the same four-month survival as our Exponential case by construction, but a different hazard at month four: \(h(4)=0.4\) rather than 0.2 per month. Matching one cumulative risk does not make the models equivalent.

E3. Median and mean of the exponential model

Problem. For \(\lambda=0.20\) per month, calculate median and mean lifetime and show independent checks.

Solution. For the median solve \(e^{-0.2m}=0.5\), giving \(m=\log2/0.2\approx3.465736\) months. Verify \(H(m)=\log2\) and \(S(m)=0.5\). For the mean use \(E[T]=\int_0^\infty e^{-0.2t}dt=[-5e^{-0.2t}]_0^\infty=5\) months. Independent dimensional check: dividing one by a rate expressed per month gives months. A mean of 5 is not inconsistent with a median of 3.47; the right tail increases the mean.

Interpretation. If the actual fleet is censored after four months, the fitted model can extrapolate to a mean of five, but the empirical data alone may not establish the distant tail. A number calculated from stipulated \(\lambda\) carries model assumptions, not a guaranteed estimate.

E4. Hazard can exceed one without invalid probabilities

Problem. A synthetic part has constant hazard \(h=2\) per year. Calculate its next-three-month conditional failure probability and show why 2 is not a 200% risk.

Solution. Convert three months to \(0.25\) year. Incremental cumulative hazard equals \(2(0.25)=0.5\). Therefore \(P(\text{fail in next 3 months}\mid\text{surviving now})=1-e^{-0.5}=0.393469\). The same calculation in months uses hazard \(2/12=1/6\) per month and interval length three months, producing \(1-e^{-(1/6)3}\). Both results are less than one.

Interpretation. The rate of 2 has inverse-year units, whereas the probability 0.393469 is dimensionless and refers to a specified three-month interval. A local estimate \(2(1)=2\) would be an invalid approximation over one full year; instead use \(1-e^{-2}\).

E5. Piecewise cumulative hazard at a change point

Problem. Let \(h=0.1\) per month before month three and \(h=0.3\) thereafter. Obtain survival at months two, three and five and the month-three-to-five conditional risk.

Solution. For two months, \(H(2)=0.1(2)=0.2\), so \(S(2)=e^{-0.2}=0.818731\). At three, \(H(3)=0.3\) and \(S(3)=e^{-0.3}=0.740818\). At five, integrate both pieces: \(H(5)=0.1(3)+0.3(2)=0.9\); hence \(S(5)=e^{-0.9}=0.406570\). Conditional failure after surviving month three equals \(1-S(5)/S(3)=1-e^{-0.6}=0.451188\).

Interpretation. Multiplying 0.3 by five would incorrectly apply the later rate to the earlier operating period. The integral must respect the time boundary, and the change point must be justified by engineering knowledge rather than chosen to force an answer.

E6. Weibull median, density and hazard

Problem. Under \(S(t)=e^{-(t/5)^2}\), find median, density, hazard and hazard at month five.

Solution. Differentiate survival: \(S'(t)=-(2t/25)e^{-(t/5)^2}\). Thus \(f=(2t/25)S\) and \(h=2t/25\) per month. The median satisfies \((m/5)^2=\log2\), yielding \(m=5\sqrt{\log2}\approx4.16277\) months. At five months, \(h(5)=10/25=0.4\) per month, while \(S(5)=e^{-1}=0.367879\). Verify density at five: \(f(5)=0.4(0.367879)=0.147152\) per month.

Interpretation. The model has increasing hazard but decreasing survival. Density need not be monotone. Do not treat the five-month scale as the mean; the mean is \(5\sqrt\pi/2\approx4.43113\) months, using the cited Gaussian half-integral.

E7. Restricted mean at six months

Problem. Compute the six-month RMST and the missing tail for an Exponential lifetime with \(\lambda=0.2\) per month.

Solution. \(\operatorname{RMST}(6)=\int_0^6e^{-0.2t}dt=[-5e^{-0.2t}]_0^6=5(1-e^{-1.2})\approx3.49403\) months. The mean total lifetime equals five months. The expected tail contribution is \(\int_6^\infty e^{-0.2t}dt=5e^{-1.2}\approx1.50597\). Adding the two pieces gives \(3.49403+1.50597=5.00000\) months, an independent decomposition check.

Interpretation. RMST is limited to the first six months and can inform uptime planning during that horizon. It is not a statement that all devices last only 3.49 months. The difference from unrestricted mean is precisely the survival area beyond the specified cutoff.

E8. Change units without changing physics

Problem. A model has \(\lambda=0.2\) per month. Express the hazard per year, then calculate four-month survival using years.

Solution. One year contains twelve months, so the rate is \(0.2(12)=2.4\) per year. Four months equals \(1/3\) year. Survival from the annual representation is \(S(1/3)=e^{-2.4/3}=e^{-0.8}=0.449329\). In the original representation \(S(4)=e^{-0.2(4)}\) gives the same answer. Both yield dimensionless cumulative hazard \(H=0.8\) at that physical time.

Interpretation. The hazard's numerical value became twelve times larger, but the probability and cumulative hazard did not change. This is a scale conversion, not an increase in real equipment risk. A report must label inverse-month or inverse-year units explicitly.

E9. Different hazard ratios and risk ratios

Problem. Two hypothetical groups have Exponential hazards 0.1 and 0.2 per month. Compare hazard ratio and cumulative risk ratio at twelve months.

Solution. The constant hazard ratio is \(0.2/0.1=2\). At twelve months \(F_A=1-e^{-1.2}=0.698806\) and \(F_B=1-e^{-2.4}=0.909282\). The cumulative risk ratio is \(0.909282/0.698806\approx1.3012\), not two. Check both probabilities lie in \([0,1]\). At one month the risk ratio is closer to two, \(0.181269/0.095163\approx1.9048\), but still not identical.

Interpretation. Hazard ratio describes a comparison of instantaneous conditional rates. Cumulative risk ratio compares event probabilities up to a declared horizon. Neither ratio alone proves an intervention caused the difference between groups.

E10. A discrete survival counterexample

Problem. A component can fail only at discrete inspection rounds; \(q_1=0.1\) and \(q_2=0.2\) conditional on being at risk. Calculate survival after round two and compare two incorrect shortcuts.

Solution. Use multiplication of conditional survival probabilities: \((1-q_1)(1-q_2)=0.9(0.8)=0.72\). Cumulative failure is \(1-0.72=0.28\). Incorrect shortcut one gives \(q_1+q_2=0.30\). Incorrect shortcut two gives \(e^{-(q_1+q_2)}=e^{-0.30}\approx0.740818\) for survival. Both differ from the exact 0.72 because discrete hazard probabilities are not infinitesimal rates.

Interpretation. If continuous-time observations are merely rounded to months, one must investigate whether the underlying event times remain continuous or are truly grouped. Do not silently apply the continuous identity \(S=e^{-\sum q_j}\) to discrete probabilities.

E11. Recover hazard from a proposed survival function

Problem. A model proposes \(S(t)=1/(1+t)\), \(t\ge0\) in years. Calculate \(F\), \(f\), \(h\), \(H\) and identify whether hazard increases or decreases.

Solution. \(F(t)=1-1/(1+t)=t/(1+t)\). Differentiate survival: \(S'(t)=-(1+t)^{-2}\), so \(f(t)=(1+t)^{-2}\) per year. Divide by \(S\): \(h(t)=1/(1+t)\) per year. Taking negative logs yields \(H(t)=\log(1+t)\). Verify by integrating hazard: \(\int_0^t1/(1+u)du=\log(1+t)\). The derivative \(h'(t)=-(1+t)^{-2}\) is negative, so conditional hazard decreases as time passes.

Interpretation. This survival distribution has a long tail. In fact, \(E[T]=\int_0^\infty1/(1+t)dt\) diverges to infinity. A perfectly valid survival function need not have a finite mean; median, however, exists at \(t=1\) year.

E12. Identify an impossible curve

Problem. A report lists survival at ages one, two and three months as 0.82, 0.87 and 0.61. Another report gives cumulative hazards 0.20, -0.10 and 0.55. Diagnose both.

Solution. Survival cannot rise from 0.82 at month one to 0.87 at month two because \({T>2}\subseteq{T>1}\). Its cumulative probability must be nonincreasing. Cumulative hazard from nonnegative rates starts at zero and cannot decline; a negative value of -0.10 is impossible for the ordinary continuous cumulative hazard. Both tables therefore need data, convention, or computation audits before interpretation. One cannot repair them by inventing a new failure time.

Interpretation. These are structural checks, not significance tests. Measurement rounding might explain tiny numerical discrepancies, but not a genuine survival increase or negative cumulative intensity. An honest QA report records the anomaly, possible causes, and what evidence is needed to resolve it.

E13. Censoring versus model-implied survival

Problem. A study follows ten compressors through month four; six fail and four are right-censored at month four. Can we estimate mean lifetime simply as the average of the six observed failure times? Can we state the unrestricted median from those counts alone?

Solution. No to both claims as stated. Deleting the four censored devices conditions the calculation on having observed a failure and discards evidence that each censored device survived at least four months. Those counts alone do not provide the sequence of event times and risk sets required for a Kaplan–Meier curve, nor do they establish the tail after month four. The observed-failure average is not the original population mean. A median may or may not be identifiable depending on the full event-time configuration.

Interpretation. Under the stipulated Exponential model with known \(\lambda=0.2\), the model-implied mean remains five months and median 3.466 months; that is a mathematical consequence of the assumption, not something derived from the ten-person counts. Data construction and estimation must be handled separately.

E14. Final maintenance decision with sensitivity

Problem. A manager wants the one-month first-failure probability for a compressor still working at age five. Compare Exponential \(\lambda=0.2\), Weibull \(S=e^{-(t/5)^2}\), and piecewise hazard 0.1 before month three and 0.3 thereafter. Propose a scientifically defensible recommendation.

Solution. Use the same conditional event and horizon. Exponential: \(1-e^{-0.2}=0.181269\). Weibull: \(H(6)-H(5)=36/25-25/25=0.44\), so \(1-e^{-0.44}\approx0.355964\). Piecewise after month three: one-month cumulative-hazard increment 0.3, so \(1-e^{-0.3}=0.259182\). The interval of model-based risks is about 18.1%–35.6%. The models are illustrative assumptions, not estimates from a fleet. No false precision or causal maintenance effect can be inferred.

Interpretation and decision. Report that the decision is sensitive to hazard shape. Before specifying a replacement interval, obtain actual failure/censoring logs, operational covariates, the costs of preventive service and unplanned failure, and validation of model calibration. A reasonable provisional choice could be targeted inspection, not automatic replacement; its desirability cannot be proven without policy costs and effectiveness evidence. Transfer: repeat the calculation at ages one and eight and explain how the preferred intervention could change when Weibull hazard rises with age while Exponential hazard does not.

39. References and further methodological boundaries

The identities and interpretations in this guide follow established survival-analysis terminology, not an invented framework. For further study consult Stanford STATS 305B, Survival Analysis I (https://web.stanford.edu/class/stats305b/notes/Survival_I.html); UCLA Statistical Consulting, Introduction to Survival Analysis in SAS (https://stats.oarc.ucla.edu/sas/seminars/sas-survival/); and the University of California, Davis teaching text Survival Analysis (https://dmrocke.ucdavis.edu/Class/EPI204-Spring-2021/2012_Book_SurvivalAnalysis.pdf). The references explain continuous hazard, cumulative hazard, and the conditional-probability interpretation; the original arithmetic examples here are fully synthetic.

The Gaussian half-integral used for the Weibull mean is a standard cited mathematical result rather than a result independently derived from first-year single-variable calculus in this guide. The nonnegative tail-integral identity can be supported by a nonnegative integral-interchange theorem whose proof is also outside the Calc I prerequisite. These two limited citations should be distinguished from the complete derivations of \(S\), \(f\), \(h\), and \(H\), which are carried out step by step. More advanced topics—Kaplan–Meier uncertainty, Cox regression, competing risks, and recurrent events—require additional model assumptions and belong in later curriculum units.
