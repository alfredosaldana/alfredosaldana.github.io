0. Orientation: the question ANOVA can actually answer

Learning promise. By the end of this guide, you should be able to take complete measurements from three or more independent groups, calculate every entry in a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) table by hand, explain why its F statistic responds to differences among population means, verify the arithmetic in two independent ways, and state precisely what a significant or nonsignificant global result does and does not establish. Our audience knows introductory statistics and Calculus I; no matrix algebra or statistical programming is required. Essential curriculum prerequisites are Guides 29 and 42. We revisit sample variance, the null hypothesis, degrees of freedom, and the interpretation of a p-value rather than treating them as unspoken requirements.

A factory compares three production processes. Each process produces units measured in kilograms of usable output per hour (kg/h). Are their mean outputs identical in the populations of future, comparable runs? One approach is to inspect three sample means, but different sample means appear even when processes are equally capable: repeated measurements contain ordinary randomness. Another approach is to run three pairwise t tests, but that raises a multiple-testing question and fails to provide one coherent answer to the global question. One-way ANOVA instead evaluates the hypothesis that all group population means are equal in one test. One-way means a single categorical explanatory factor—in this example the process—with three levels A, B, and C. The outcome is numerical. The name analysis of variance sounds paradoxical: we are asking about means, but we compare two sources of variation to learn whether their observed separation is larger than random within-process scatter would plausibly produce.

The word population matters. Nine readings from today's runs describe samples, not automatically all future production. The scientific parameter for process A is its population mean, written μ_A, not the mean of its three observed readings. Data alone cannot guarantee representativeness; design and independence connect a calculation to its intended population. Likewise, a statistically significant result is evidence concerning an equality hypothesis under stated assumptions, not proof that changing the factory process will cause higher output. A randomized assignment of comparable runs could strengthen a causal interpretation; a comparison of historical production lines with different operators, raw materials, or shifts could be confounded.

Route through the lesson. We will specify the model and hypotheses, expose a complete small dataset, build group means and the grand mean, decompose each squared deviation into between- and within-group pieces, count degrees of freedom, construct the table, interpret F and its p-value, examine assumptions, demonstrate the relationship to a pooled two-sample t test, and transfer the reasoning to unequal group sizes, edge cases, and production decisions. Figures and tables are explanatory tools, not decoration. All numerical observations in this guide are invented solely for teaching; they are not measurements from an actual factory.

1. The statistical design and the one-factor model

A factor is a categorical variable used to label groups. A level is a particular category, such as A, B, or C. A response is the numerical quantity measured for each independent experimental unit; here an experimental unit might be a separate production run. The important count is not how many measurements were entered into a spreadsheet, but how many independent units were genuinely observed. Measuring the same run three times does not produce three independent production runs. A good study specifies the unit before collecting data, because pseudo-replication can make the error denominator artificially small and exaggerate evidence against the null.

Write Y_ij for the observed response from unit j in group i. The subscript i identifies the process, running from 1 through k; j identifies a unit within that process, running from 1 through n_i. The number of groups is k, the sample size in group i is n_i, and the total sample size is N = n_1 + ... + n_k. With k=3 and three runs per process, N=9. These are counts, not physical units. Y_ij and every mean of Y have units kg/h. A difference between two means also has kg/h, while a squared difference or sum of squares has (kg/h)².

The classical fixed-effects one-way model is Y_ij = μ_i + ε_ij, or equivalently Y_ij = μ + τ_i + ε_ij subject to a constraint that identifies the overall level and group effects. The first expression is simpler for beginners: μ_i is the unknown true mean of process i; ε_ij is its deviation, or random error. For the usual exact F reference distribution, the errors are independent, normally distributed with mean zero, and have a common population variance σ². The variance parameter σ² is unknown and has squared outcome units. The model does not say that the three processes have equal means; equality is the null hypothesis we will test. Nor does normality mean the collection of all outcomes combined is normal; it concerns the within-group errors around their respective means.

Our global hypotheses are H₀: μ_A = μ_B = μ_C and H₁: not all three population means are equal. The alternative is logically broad: it includes a situation in which only A differs, one in which B alone differs, and one in which all three differ. Rejecting H₀ establishes evidence of at least one inequality, not of every pairwise inequality. This distinction is a central learning outcome. We use significance level α = 0.05, a preselected threshold for the long-run Type I error probability under the model. A p-value below 0.05 leads us to reject H₀ under that rule; it is not a 95% probability that H₁ is true.

ANOVA is appropriate for independent groups with one categorical factor and one numerical response. A repeated measurement on the same machine across days may require a design that models dependence. Two categorical factors, such as process and operator shift, create questions about main effects and interactions that a one-way analysis cannot isolate. When group variances are markedly unequal, a Welch-type alternative may be appropriate, especially with unequal n_i. Those alternatives are useful boundaries of the classical model, not excuses to ignore the conditions behind the calculation.

The teaching dataset contains exactly nine synthetic observations. Process A records 8, 9, and 10 kg/h. Process B records 11, 12, and 13 kg/h. Process C records 9, 10, and 11 kg/h. There are three independent runs in each category by assumption for this exercise. No values are missing, no runs are duplicated, and there are no hidden records. Those claims describe the invented exercise, not verified real-world data. The readings form a balanced design because all groups have n_i=3. Balanced does not mean that the group means are equal; it only means their sample sizes agree.

Before calculating, look at the clusters. A has center 9, B has center 12, and C has center 10. B appears above A, whereas C lies in between. Inside every process, readings span only two units, so the separation between group centers looks large relative to the variation among repeated runs. The global question is whether this between-center separation is sufficiently large relative to within-group scatter after accounting for how many centers and residual observations we used. A plot should display individual measurements along with the group means; a bar chart of means alone hides the very noise ANOVA needs to estimate.

Step 1: group sums and means. A sums to 8+9+10=27 kg/h, so its mean is 27/3 = 9 kg/h. B sums to 11+12+13=36, giving 36/3 = 12 kg/h. C sums to 9+10+11=30, giving 30/3 = 10 kg/h. The mean is the arithmetic center minimizing squared deviations within each group. That least-squares property helps explain why subtracting the group mean produces the within-group residuals used later.

Step 2: the grand mean. All readings sum to 27+36+30=93, so the grand mean is 93/9 = 31/3 ≈ 10.3333 kg/h. The exact fraction 31/3 is useful because intermediate rounding can destroy an otherwise exact sum-of-squares identity. A second check is the sample-size-weighted average of group means: (3·9 + 3·12 + 3·10)/9 = 93/9. It is not generally correct to average the group means without weighting them: the simple average happens to work here because the groups have equal sizes, but fails when sample sizes differ.

Step 3: distinguish arithmetic from inference. The observed means differ by 3 kg/h between B and A, but those are sample differences. We do not know the corresponding population differences, their uncertainty, or whether selection bias changed the data. Thus the computed 3 is an observed descriptive effect, not a causal effect and not yet a statistically tested conclusion. We will preserve the units and these qualifications when interpreting F.

3. Why squared deviations, not absolute differences?

Suppose we measured the distance of every observation from a single common mean. That would quantify the total variation in the sample. But if the groups have different centers, part of the variation comes from the displacement of each group center relative to the grand center. Another part comes from individual readings scattered around their own group centers. ANOVA separates these two pieces. The essential insight is that the same observation can be decomposed into a group-level displacement plus an individual residual; we do not need to choose one explanation and discard the other.

For each observation, write Y_ij − grand_mean = (Y_ij − group_mean_i) + (group_mean_i − grand_mean). The first term is its within-group residual; the second is its group's between-group displacement. Their sum is exactly the original deviation. For example, B's reading 13 differs from the grand mean 31/3 by 13−31/3=8/3. Relative to the B mean of 12, its residual is 13−12=1; B's group mean differs from the grand mean by 12−31/3=5/3. Therefore 1+5/3=8/3. We have decomposed the observed deviation without approximation or statistical assumptions.

Why square? Positive and negative signed deviations cancel: within group A, −1 + 0 + 1 = 0, although the three observations are not identical. Squaring turns distances into nonnegative magnitudes, makes large departures count more strongly, and connects naturally to sample variance and least-squares fitting. The sum of squared deviations is abbreviated SS, for sum of squares. It has squared units, not the original outcome units; dividing by degrees of freedom yields a mean square or MS, also with squared units. Their ratio will be dimensionless. Absolute deviations can also summarize spread, but their decomposition and null reference distribution do not give the classical one-way F statistic developed here.

The identity for squared deviations is more subtle than the unsquared decomposition. Squaring (a+b) produces a² + 2ab + b², so a cross-term appears. It disappears after summing within each group because residuals around that group's mean sum to zero: Σ_j(Y_ij−group_mean_i)=0. This algebra—not a visual guess—explains why the total sum of squares can equal the between plus within sums exactly.

The graphical interpretation is a diagram of three horizontal group means and nine individual points. A reading is connected first to its own mean, then that mean to the grand mean. The squared distances are not literally additive point by point; the summed cross-terms cancel across each complete group. This distinction prevents a common mistake: a correct decomposition of signed differences does not imply an independent squared-distance identity for each individual reading.

4. Calculate the within-group sum of squares

The within-group sum of squares is SSW = Σ_i Σ_j (Y_ij − group_mean_i)². For every group, subtract its own mean rather than the grand mean. This step answers: how much do independent observations vary around their local process center, ignoring the shifts among the centers? It is the natural estimate of unexplained variation after allowing each process to have a separate mean. It does not establish that the remaining scatter is harmless or random in reality; a residual pattern could reveal a missing variable.

Process A: its mean is 9. The three differences are 8−9=−1, 9−9=0 and 10−9=1 kg/h. Squaring gives 1, 0 and 1 (kg/h)². Thus SSW_A=1+0+1=2 (kg/h)². Process B: relative to 12, the differences from 11,12,13 are −1,0,1, so SSW_B=2. Process C: relative to 10, the differences from 9,10,11 are likewise −1,0,1, so SSW_C=2. The pooled within-group sum is SSW=2+2+2=6 (kg/h)².

Why pool rather than average group standard deviations? Under the common-variance assumption, all three groups share one σ². Each group offers information about that same quantity. The quantity SSW pools squared residuals, and the corresponding degrees of freedom account for how many group means had to be estimated. An unweighted average of three sample standard deviations is not equivalent to this pooled mean square in an unbalanced design, and in general is not the correct ANOVA error estimator.

Check the arithmetic using the identity Σ_j(y_j−mean)² = Σ_j y_j² − (Σ_j y_j)²/n_i. For A, squares sum to 64+81+100=245; the correction term is 27²/3=243; difference=2. For B, squares sum to 121+144+169=434 and 36²/3=432; again 2. For C, squares sum to 81+100+121=302 and 30²/3=300; again 2. This second route uses only sums and squared sums, and independently reproduces SSW=6.

If all observations in every group were equal to their group mean, SSW would be zero. The usual F ratio would then have a zero denominator and could be infinite or undefined; this is an edge case, not automatic evidence of an infinitely strong scientifically meaningful effect. Constant readings may reflect rounding, equipment limits, or duplicated measurements. Sound analysis first checks the measurement process rather than blindly reporting a numerical infinity.

5. Calculate the between-group sum of squares

The between-group sum of squares is SSB = Σ_i n_i(group_mean_i − grand_mean)². It measures how far the group centers lie from the grand center, weighting each center by the number of observations it represents. If each process mean equaled the grand mean, SSB would be zero. Larger distances lead to larger SSB; additionally, a larger n_i makes evidence about that group's center more influential. This weighting is essential, not merely a presentational choice.

In our data, grand_mean=31/3. For A, the displacement is 9−31/3=−4/3; squared it is 16/9; multiply by n_A=3 to get 16/3. For B, 12−31/3=5/3, square 25/9, multiply by 3 to get 25/3. For C, 10−31/3=−1/3, square 1/9, multiply by 3 to get 1/3. Adding: SSB = 16/3 + 25/3 + 1/3 = 42/3 = 14 (kg/h)². We have shown each substitution so the result is reproducible without a calculator that knows ANOVA.

A useful error check: the weighted signed displacements must sum to zero. Here 3(−4/3) + 3(5/3) + 3(−1/3)=−4+5−1=0. This constraint follows from the definition of the grand mean. If it fails, either one group mean or the grand mean was calculated incorrectly. Squared weighted displacements obviously do not sum to zero—that is why they can measure between-group separation.

Notice the two sums of squares describe different questions. SSB is 14 because the observed process means are separated; SSW is 6 because repeated runs within each process are not identical. Neither alone supplies an inferential test. If we collected many more observations and the group centers remained comparably separated, SSB would typically grow with sample size; and SSW would also grow because more residuals enter. The ratio of mean squares, not the raw SS numbers, adjusts for the different opportunities to accumulate variation.

This is also where unbalanced designs matter. If one process contributes 100 runs and another only 3, the grand mean is weighted toward the large group, and SSB must retain the n_i factors. Dropping the weights could produce a table whose SS do not add correctly and whose F lacks the desired interpretation. Later we will solve a separate unbalanced dataset all the way through rather than assuming the balanced recipe always applies.

6. Prove the decomposition of total variation

The total sum of squares is SST = Σ_i Σ_j (Y_ij − grand_mean)². It asks how variable the nine observations are when we ignore process labels and describe them with only the grand mean. Since that grand mean is 31/3, we can calculate SST by adding the nine squared differences. But before doing a second long sum, we should understand the fundamental identity SST = SSB + SSW. This is not a statistical approximation; it is exact arithmetic whenever the same observations and their properly calculated group means are used.

Derivation. Let r_ij=Y_ij−group_mean_i and d_i=group_mean_i−grand_mean. For each reading, Y_ij−grand_mean = r_ij+d_i. Square and sum over all groups: SST = Σ_iΣ_j[r_ij² + 2r_ij d_i + d_i²]. The first part is SSW. In the middle term, d_i is constant within group i, so Σ_j 2r_ij d_i=2d_iΣ_j r_ij=0 because residuals around their sample mean sum to zero. The last part has n_i identical copies of d_i², giving Σ_i n_i d_i²=SSB. Therefore SST=SSW+0+SSB. This proof requires no assumption of independent or normally distributed observations: it is an algebraic identity.

For the example, SSW=6 and SSB=14, so the identity predicts SST=20 (kg/h)². Verify independently through the computational formula SST=Σ y²−(Σ y)²/N. We already found group squared sums 245, 434, and 302, which sum to 981. The total-sum correction is 93²/9=8649/9=961. Thus SST=981−961=20 (kg/h)². This independently checks the entire decomposition: 14+6=20. A third approach could square each of the nine deviations from 31/3, but the computational identity is more efficient and avoids excessive fractions.

The decomposition has a geometric interpretation. Fitting one shared mean leaves SST squared error; fitting one mean per group leaves SSW squared error. The improvement in fit is SST−SSW=SSB=14. Because separate group means can only fit the data at least as well as one common mean, SSW cannot exceed SST. Yet extra parameters always improve—or at least do not worsen—fit to a finite sample, even when the population means are equal. That is why ANOVA must penalize the improvement for the extra estimated means through degrees of freedom.

Important boundary. The exact equality of sums of squares does not prove that the classical F distribution applies. Data can satisfy SST=SSB+SSW while observations are highly dependent, while variance differs between groups, or while distributions are heavily skewed. Arithmetic correctness is necessary for an accurate table; statistical assumptions are additionally needed for a trustworthy p-value.

7. Count degrees of freedom and form mean squares

A degree of freedom is an independently variable piece of information remaining after accounting for estimated constraints. If a group contains three observations with a fixed sample mean, its three residuals must sum to zero. Once the first two residuals are known, the third is determined. Hence each group has n_i−1 residual degrees of freedom. For three groups of size three, the within-group degrees of freedom are (3−1)+(3−1)+(3−1)=2+2+2=6, or generally df_within = N−k.

Between-group variation involves k group centers subject to a weighted zero-sum constraint relative to the grand mean. Therefore df_between = k−1. Here k=3, so df_between=2. The total sample variance around one estimated grand mean has df_total=N−1=8. As a check, the pieces add: 2+6=8. Degrees of freedom are dimensionless counts; their sum checks the decomposition of fitted information just as the SS identity checks variation.

Mean square does not mean the ordinary mean of the raw observations. It is a sum of squared deviations divided by its degrees of freedom. Thus MS_between=SSB/(k−1)=14/2=7 (kg/h)². MS_within=SSW/(N−k)=6/6=1 (kg/h)². We can now compare quantities with the same units. The within mean square is also called MSE, mean squared error, and estimates the common population error variance σ² under the homoscedastic model. Its square root, √1=1 kg/h, is the residual standard deviation: an estimate of the within-process scatter in the original units.

Under H₀ and the classical model, both MS_between and MS_within target the same error variance σ². When group means are truly unequal, between-group variation tends to contain an additional signal caused by their different population centers. In the fixed-effects normal model, one can express the expected between mean square as σ² plus a nonnegative term measuring weighted squared population mean departures from their pooled mean, divided by k−1. This explains, rather than merely memorizes, why a large numerator relative to the denominator suggests a violation of equal population means.

Be careful with estimating variance. Dividing SSW by N rather than N−k would ignore the fact that k group means were estimated, systematically depressing the pooled error estimate. Dividing SSB by N−1 would confuse between-group degrees of freedom with the total sample degrees of freedom. Either error changes F. A reliable manual solution writes the rows of the ANOVA table in this order: source of variation, SS, df, MS, and finally F and p-value.

The ANOVA table for the nine measurements has three rows: between groups (the process factor), within groups (residual/error), and total. Between: SS=14, df=2, MS=7. Within: SS=6, df=6, MS=1. Total: SS=20, df=8. The F statistic appears only in the factor row and is F=MS_between/MS_within=7/1=7. It has no units, since the numerator and denominator both have (kg/h)². The equalities 14+6=20 and 2+6=8 verify the two table totals.

Intuition for F. If the group differences are no larger than expected from normal sampling scatter, numerator and denominator are two related estimators of the same σ² and their ratio often lies near one. It is not a rule that F must equal one under H₀ in a particular sample: F is random, nonnegative, and can lie below or above one. Values well above one indicate that the process centers are more separated than the estimated within-process noise suggests. Very small values generally are not evidence for the usual one-sided ANOVA alternative; the rejection region is in the upper tail of the F distribution.

Under H₀, independent normal errors and common variance give F a reference distribution with 2 numerator and 6 denominator degrees of freedom, written F_(2,6). The p-value is the right-tail probability P(F_(2,6)≥7). For this particular degrees-of-freedom combination, the survival function has a convenient form: P(F_(2,6)≥f)=[6/(6+2f)]³ for f≥0. Substitute f=7: [6/(6+14)]³=(6/20)³=0.3³=0.027. The probability is dimensionless. A general F table, calculator, or scientific function can obtain the same tail probability without memorizing this special formula.

At α=0.05, 0.027<0.05, so we reject H₀ in this teaching example. The conclusion is: the synthetic sample provides evidence that not all three underlying process means are equal under the classical model assumptions. Do not conclude that B exceeds A in the population solely from this global test, that every pair differs, or that there is a 97.3% probability the alternative is true. The p-value is a probability of observing a statistic at least as extreme as ours under H₀ and its model, not a probability assigned to H₀ itself.

As a second check, the 5% upper-tail cutoff for F_(2,6) is about 5.143. Our observed 7 exceeds it, consistent with p=0.027. This critical-value decision and the p-value decision must agree whenever they use the same null distribution and threshold. When they disagree in a spreadsheet, investigate reversed tails, swapped numerator/denominator degrees of freedom, or an accidental change of alpha.

9. A graph that explains the arithmetic

The first figure shows the nine measurements on one common vertical axis, grouped by process. Individual dots represent the invented observations; horizontal markers represent sample means. You can inspect the evidence with your eyes: process B's dots are shifted upward, while the within-process ranges overlap only partly. The display separates centers from spread more effectively than a bar chart showing only 9, 12, and 10. Each dot is a run, not a person or an estimated population parameter. The vertical axis is kg/h, so a difference of one unit has a clear physical meaning.

The second figure describes the partition SST = SSB + SSW with a proportionate stacked bar: 14 of 20 squared units come from differences among the observed group centers; 6 of 20 come from residual spread within processes. The percentages are 14/20=70% and 6/20=30%. These shares help answer a descriptive question: how did variation in this particular sample partition under the one-factor least-squares fit? They are not probabilities that the hypothesis is true, and they are not percentages of future production explained causally by the process.

One may report the descriptive effect-size statistic η²=SSB/SST=14/20=0.70. It measures the proportion of corrected sample variation accounted for by process-group differences in this one-factor decomposition. The numerical value is large for this small constructed case, but a nine-observation synthetic exercise cannot support a reliable claim that 70% of variation in actual factory output will be explained by selecting a process. Sample η² tends to be optimistic as a population effect-size estimator in small samples; uncertainty, external validity, and study design matter.

Notice that rejecting the global test and obtaining a large η² answer different questions. The p-value addresses compatibility of the data with the null under a model, incorporating degrees of freedom and noise. η² describes the fraction of sample variation assigned to the factor, without being a p-value or proof of cause. Both can be reported, but neither substitutes for the other. If output improvements are expensive or energy-intensive, decision makers should also weigh cost, reliability, and operational feasibility, not merely chase the smallest p-value.

A further graphical caution: a plot of raw readings is not a formal check that errors are normal, independent, or have equal variances. The nine points reveal the observed scale but cannot expose whether successive measurements were autocorrelated or whether process labels coincided with operator shifts. The production protocol—not a beautiful figure—determines what inferential language is justified.

10. Why an F distribution appears

The F distribution is the distribution of a ratio of appropriately scaled independent chi-square variables. This result can sound abstract, but it follows a teaching sequence. A single normally distributed error, divided by σ, becomes standard normal. Squaring that standardized error produces a contribution to a chi-square sum. Summing squares of independent normal residual components produces a chi-square variable whose degrees of freedom count those components. In the one-way normal model, the between-group and within-group sums of squares have orthogonal directions, so the relevant scaled sums are independent under H₀. Dividing each by its own degrees of freedom and forming their ratio produces F.

More precisely, under the null and the assumptions, SSB/σ² has a chi-square distribution with k−1 degrees of freedom, SSW/σ² has a chi-square distribution with N−k degrees of freedom, and these quantities are independent. Consequently (SSB/(k−1))/(SSW/(N−k)) has F_(k−1,N−k) distribution. This is a cited standard theorem of the normal linear model; the prerequisites for a complete proof include multivariate normal geometry, which we do not assume here. The algebraic SS decomposition was proved directly above; the distributional theorem is distinct and depends on stronger assumptions.

The numerator and denominator degrees of freedom determine the shape of the F distribution. F cannot be negative because both mean squares are nonnegative, except for undefined degenerate cases. The distribution is typically right-skewed, particularly with small degrees of freedom. Hence the tail beyond a large F value, rather than a symmetrical two-tail distance from one, supplies the classical p-value. Changing sample sizes changes the denominator degrees of freedom and therefore the reference distribution even if a numerical ratio stays identical.

It is important not to reason backward from an observed large F to the truth of the model assumptions. We use the assumptions to assign probabilities to possible F values; the F value does not validate those assumptions. The same ratio could arise from systematic drift, a variance imbalance, outliers, or legitimately different process means. The numerical operation remains possible in all such situations, but the advertised false-positive rate may be wrong without an appropriate design or alternative method.

A final interpretive subtlety: the word variance appears in several roles. Sample variance measures squared scatter around a sample mean. The model error variance σ² is a population parameter. MS_within estimates σ² under the common-variance model. MS_between has the same target under H₀ but includes additional group-mean signal under alternatives. F compares these estimators; it does not directly compare the three observed sample variances and ask whether they are equal. Homoscedasticity is an assumption of the classical test, not the null hypothesis being tested.

11. Assumptions: what can fail and why

Independence. The runs within and across processes must act as independent experimental units for the standard reference result. This property is primarily about how data were generated. If A was measured in the morning and B at night, systematic time or temperature differences may coincide with process labels. If three readings are repeated sensor measurements of one physical batch, their errors are correlated. Artificially increasing N reduces the estimated standard error and can turn a harmless pattern into a misleadingly small p-value. Randomization, separate runs, and transparent sampling records are more informative than testing independence with nine numbers.

Normality of errors. The exact small-sample F theory assumes approximately normal within-group errors around the respective μ_i. It does not require the pooled raw observations to look like one bell curve: combining populations with different means can generate a multimodal distribution even when each group's errors are normal. With small samples, normality is difficult to assess reliably; a normal probability plot of residuals or a review of measurement mechanisms may help, but it cannot prove the assumption. Strong outliers, heavy tails, bounded outcomes, or ceiling effects can make classical reference probabilities questionable, particularly when sample size is modest.

Equal population variances. Homoscedasticity means Var(ε_ij)=σ² for all groups. It does not mean the observed sample variances must be numerically identical in any given dataset. In our constructed case the sample variances happen to match exactly: within each group SS=2 and df=2, so s²=1 (kg/h)². The assumption concerns the unknown variances across repeated samples, not a guarantee that this particular pattern must occur. Severe heteroscedasticity can distort the pooled denominator, especially when group sizes are unequal and variance is related to group size. A Welch one-way ANOVA is one well-established alternative when the equal-variance condition is implausible.

Additive one-factor mean structure. Each group is described by its own constant population mean plus error. If productivity changes dramatically as the day progresses, if there is an unmodeled treatment-by-shift interaction, or if the same run responds across multiple time points, one-factor independent-error analysis may misdescribe the experiment. The analysis can still calculate a contrast among observed group means, but treating it as the sole explanation may omit essential structure. Specify intended population, experimental units, and possible confounders before applying any inferential label.

Practical diagnostic sequence. Start with the sampling/assignment protocol, count true independent units, verify units and missing values, inspect individual observations and group spreads, check residual-versus-fitted patterns for widening or systematic drift, and use a normal quantile plot cautiously. Do not treat a formal assumption test with a nonsignificant p-value as proof of the assumptions; low power can conceal real violations. When evidence suggests unequal variances, select and justify a suitable robust analysis rather than mechanically reusing the equal-variance denominator. Detailed diagnostics and post-hoc testing are developed in the next curricular guide, not silently treated as completed here.

12. Two groups: derive the identity F = t²

One-way ANOVA works with k=2 as well as three or more groups. In the two-group case, the classical equal-variance independent-samples t test and one-way ANOVA test the same null hypothesis. Under identical pooled-variance assumptions, their statistics satisfy F_(1,N−2)=t_(N−2)². The sign of t tells which sample mean is larger; squaring loses that direction. The standard ANOVA F test is an upper-tail test, corresponding to the two-sided pooled t test, not a one-sided t decision. We can verify this equivalence on complete data rather than accepting it as a slogan.

Use the first two process groups only: A=[8,9,10], B=[11,12,13], with sample means 9 and 12 and overall mean 10.5 kg/h. The pooled within SS is 2+2=4; df_within=(3−1)+(3−1)=4; MS_within=4/4=1. The between SS is 3(9−10.5)²+3(12−10.5)²=3·2.25+3·2.25=13.5. Since df_between=1, MS_between=13.5. Hence F=13.5/1=13.5 with df (1,4).

Now calculate the usual pooled t statistic: t=(mean_B−mean_A)/[s_p√(1/n_A+1/n_B)], where pooled s_p²=MS_within=1. Substitution gives t=(12−9)/[1·√(1/3+1/3)]=3/√(2/3)=3√(3/2)≈3.6742. Square it: t²=9/(2/3)=13.5. Exactly the same number appears, not merely a close numerical coincidence. The two-sided t p-value with df=4 equals the F right-tail p-value with df (1,4), approximately 0.0213.

Why is the identity generally true with unequal n? For two groups, SSB=n_A n_B/(n_A+n_B)². Its one degree of freedom leaves MS_between=SSB. The pooled t denominator squared is MS_within(1/n_A+1/n_B). Because 1/n_A+1/n_B=(n_A+n_B)/(n_A n_B), dividing the squared mean difference by this denominator gives exactly SSB/MS_within. The result depends on pooled equal-variance t, not Welch's unequal-variance t, and assumes independent groups in both descriptions.

This identity is a valuable verification tool: if a spreadsheet reports F=13.5 but the pooled t statistic on the same six observations squares to another number, a formula, grouping, or degrees-of-freedom error occurred. It also explains why ANOVA is a genuine generalization of a familiar hypothesis test rather than an unrelated procedure invented solely for three groups.

13. An unbalanced design, computed all the way through

Balanced datasets make group means easy to average, but real studies may have different sample sizes. Consider a second independently invented dataset measuring the same type of output: process A has [7,9] kg/h; process B has [10,12,11] kg/h; process C has [9,10,12,13] kg/h. This time n_A=2, n_B=3, n_C=4, and N=9. The group means are 8, 11, and 11 kg/h. The raw sums are 16, 33, and 44, totaling 93, so the correctly weighted grand mean remains 93/9=31/3≈10.3333 kg/h. The unweighted mean of the three group means would be (8+11+11)/3=10, which is wrong for these nine individual observations.

Compute within-group SS from actual deviations. A: (7−8)²+(9−8)²=1+1=2. B: (10−11)²+(12−11)²+(11−11)²=1+1+0=2. C: (9−11)²+(10−11)²+(12−11)²+(13−11)²=4+1+1+4=10. Therefore SSW=2+2+10=14 (kg/h)². The four readings in C have more within-group spread than those in A and B, so the equal-population-variance assumption deserves particular scrutiny if these were field data. A single small illustrative sample cannot establish whether that population assumption truly fails.

Compute between-group SS retaining each sample-size weight. A contributes 2(8−31/3)² = 2(−7/3)²=98/9. B contributes 3(11−31/3)²=3(2/3)²=12/9. C contributes 4(11−31/3)²=4(2/3)²=16/9. Add: (98+12+16)/9=126/9=14 (kg/h)². The weighted signed differences also check: 2(−7/3)+3(2/3)+4(2/3)=−14/3+6/3+8/3=0. Then SST=SSB+SSW=14+14=28.

There are k=3 groups and N=9 observations, so df_between=2, df_within=6, df_total=8. The mean squares are 14/2=7 and 14/6=7/3≈2.3333 (kg/h)². Hence F=7/(7/3)=3, with df (2,6). The right-tail probability is [6/(6+2·3)]³=(6/12)³=0.125. At α=0.05, we fail to reject the equal-means null. This does not prove equality; the example has limited data, unequal group spreads, and potentially substantial uncertainty.

Notice how two datasets with the same N and even the same grand mean can lead to different conclusions. In the balanced case, F=7 and p=0.027. In the unbalanced case, F=3 and p=0.125. The difference results from the pattern of individual readings and their allocation to groups, not from whether group means are superficially “close.” A correct analysis uses every observation, the correct group sample sizes, and the appropriate within-group denominator.

14. Interpreting decisions without overclaiming

A global ANOVA has a well-defined target: the population statement μ_A=μ_B=μ_C. Rejection of H₀ indicates evidence against the joint equality. It does not by itself identify which mean differs from which other mean. Our first dataset showed observed centers at 9, 12, and 10 kg/h. Even though B's sample center is above A's, the ANOVA p-value alone cannot be relabeled as a confirmed population pairwise contrast. Finding specific differences while controlling multiple comparisons calls for planned contrasts or suitable post-hoc methods, the focus of Guide 48. The previous multiple-testing material in Guide 46 is relevant background, but this guide intentionally stops at the global question.

Failing to reject H₀ means insufficient evidence to reject at the chosen alpha under the tested model. It does not mean every process has the same population mean or that the probability of equality is high. In the unbalanced exercise p=0.125 might arise from genuinely identical means or from differences that are difficult to detect with a small sample and nontrivial noise. A claim of practical equivalence requires defining an acceptable difference and using an appropriately designed equivalence or noninferiority analysis, not simply observing p>0.05.

Statistical significance and practical importance should be reported separately. A 0.1 kg/h improvement might be statistically detectable with thousands of runs but too small to justify expensive equipment. Conversely, a 3 kg/h observed gap in nine runs may be operationally promising yet too uncertain to guarantee an investment return. Report group means, sample sizes, uncertainty and dispersion, the preselected alpha, the F statistic with both degrees of freedom, and the p-value. Contextualize units and constraints. In our constructed study the result is properly written: F(2,6)=7.00, p=0.027, η²=0.70, with the important label illustrative, synthetic dataset.

Do not interpret observational grouping as a randomized causal intervention. If process A was run with a novice operator and B with an expert, the apparent process effect can include operator differences. A random assignment of comparable units to process settings, if feasible, helps separate those influences. If randomization is impossible, measured confounders and design limitations should be stated. ANOVA itself does not repair a flawed design or turn a group association into causality.

Lastly, choose a single defensible analysis plan before inspecting outcomes where possible. Selecting the process, transformation, response, exclusions, or testing threshold only after seeing favorable results can distort Type I error control. Transparency about exclusions, measurement failures, and exploratory reanalysis helps readers know what the reported probability means. A well-computed table is the start of responsible statistical communication, not the end.

15. A complete manual checklist and common mistakes

A reproducible solution begins with a research question and a factor, not with the F formula. State the population of interest, what constitutes an independent unit, which factor levels are being compared, the numerical response and its units, and the intended design. Write H₀ and H₁ with population means μ_i, not observed sample means. Identify the α chosen before analysis. List every observation and its group, check omissions, and calculate n_i and N. Then compute raw group sums and means and the sample-size-weighted grand mean.

Next calculate SSW by subtracting each observation's own group mean, squaring and adding. Independently check with group squared sums and squared group totals. Calculate SSB from n_i times squared differences between group means and the grand mean. Independently check the weighted signed deviations sum to zero. Calculate SST directly or using Σy²−(Σy)²/N, and verify SST=SSB+SSW. If any identity fails, do not proceed to F until the arithmetic or group assignments are corrected. The exact decomposition remains valid even before distributional assumptions are evaluated.

Then determine df_between=k−1, df_within=N−k and df_total=N−1, and check the degrees of freedom add. Divide each SS by the correct df to form MS. Compute F=MS_between/MS_within, specifying which df belongs in the numerator and which in the denominator. Calculate the upper-tail F probability, compare it with α, and write a conclusion that respects the global null. Inspect the study design and model assumptions before treating that numerical probability as scientifically credible.

Four mistakes recur. First, dividing SSB by N−1 instead of k−1: that understates MS_between and changes F. Second, using an unweighted average of group means in an unbalanced design: that breaks the grand mean, SSB, and often the total identity. Third, substituting group standard deviations for the pooled residual mean square: those are not the same quantity and can have the wrong units. Fourth, declaring the largest sample mean a proven winner because the omnibus test rejected equality: ANOVA answered a collective question, not every comparison. The remedy in each case is to name the precise statistical quantity before carrying out the arithmetic.

This checklist is also an exercise in reasoning. Explain why every operation is performed: group means define residuals; squaring prevents cancellation; pooling residuals estimates common noise; degrees of freedom compensate for fitted means; dividing mean squares produces a noise-standardized signal; the reference distribution converts that statistic into a calibrated probability under a null model. If one of these steps is merely memorized without purpose, revisit the corresponding section before trusting a final answer.

16. Worked transfer exercises

Exercise 1. Rebuild the balanced ANOVA without looking up the table

Task. A student is given A=[8,9,10], B=[11,12,13] and C=[9,10,11] kg/h. Find all group means and the grand mean, calculate SSW, SSB and SST, construct the degrees of freedom and mean squares, then obtain F and its p-value. Explain exactly what rejecting H₀ would mean at α=0.05. Before opening this solution, predict which variation term will be larger and why.

Full solution. A, B and C each have n=3, and the group sums are 27, 36, 30, so the means are 9, 12, 10. The combined sum is 93 over N=9, giving grand mean 31/3. In each group the deviations are −1,0,+1, so each contributes SS=2; within total=6. Between contributions are 3(−4/3)²=16/3, 3(5/3)²=25/3 and 3(−1/3)²=1/3, adding to 14. Total SS=20. Degrees of freedom 2,6,8 produce mean squares 7 and 1. F=7, and its upper-tail probability for df (2,6) is 0.027. Since 0.027<0.05, reject equality of all population means under the model; the global outcome does not identify specific different pairs. Check: 6+14=20; 2+6=8. Every SS has units (kg/h)², while F and p have no units.

Exercise 2. Find and repair an unbalanced-grand-mean mistake

Task. With A=[7,9], B=[10,12,11], and C=[9,10,12,13], a student computes grand mean (8+11+11)/3=10 and reports a significant F test without showing the group sizes. Identify the mathematical mistake, calculate the correct ANOVA F and p, and decide whether to reject at 5%. Explain why the corrected result is not a proof of equality.

Full solution. The grand mean must average nine individual observations, not three unequally sized groups. It is (2·8+3·11+4·11)/9=93/9=31/3. The within SS values are 2,2,10, totaling14. Between SS is 2(−7/3)²+3(2/3)²+4(2/3)²=(98+12+16)/9=14. With df_between=2 and df_within=6, MS_between=7 and MS_within=14/6=7/3, so F=3 and p=[6/(6+6)]³=0.125. Do not reject at α=.05. The small data set may lack power to detect real differences and the larger C variance calls for design-based scrutiny. The sum-of-squares check is SST=28=14+14.

Exercise 3. Prove the identity for a single group, then for all groups

Task. Let r_ij=y_ij−mean_i and d_i=mean_i−grand_mean. Starting from y_ij−grand_mean=r_ij+d_i, explain why adding squared deviations over every group does not leave a cross-term. State whether the result requires normality. Use one observation from group B to verify the unsquared decomposition.

Full solution. Expand (r_ij+d_i)²=r_ij²+2r_ij d_i+d_i². Summing the middle term within a fixed group gives 2d_iΣ_j r_ij. Because mean_i is the arithmetic mean of the group's observations, Σ_j(y_ij−mean_i)=0; thus this middle term is zero for each group. Summing first and last terms yields SSW+Σ_i n_i d_i²=SSW+SSB=SST. Normality, independence and common variance are unnecessary for this algebraic result. For the B reading 13, r=13−12=1 and d=12−31/3=5/3, so the total deviation is 1+5/3=8/3=13−31/3. The squared identity is a sum-level property, not a per-observation equality of squares.

Exercise 4. Repair incorrect degrees of freedom

Task. An analyst uses SS_between=14, SS_within=6, N=9 and k=3, but divides SS_between by 8 and SS_within by 9. Explain the error and calculate both the incorrect and correct F ratios. What scientifically important issue does this illustrate?

Full solution. The analyst confuses the total df N−1=8 with the between df k−1=2, and overlooks that estimating three group means leaves only N−k=6 error df. The incorrect ratio is (14/8)/(6/9)=1.75/(2/3)=2.625. The correct ratio is (14/2)/(6/6)=7/1=7. Dividing by wrong degrees of freedom changes both variance estimates and inferential evidence; the wrong number cannot be interpreted using the intended F_(2,6) reference law. Check the df identity 2+6=8 before computing mean squares. The arithmetic identity for SS can be right while the test statistic remains wrong.

Exercise 5. Show F = t² from complete data

Task. Compare only A=[8,9,10] and B=[11,12,13]. Compute the pooled-variance t statistic for the difference of means and then compute the one-way F ratio. Explain why the sign of t disappears and why the two methods correspond to a two-sided test.

Full solution. Means are 9 and 12, group SS values are 2 and 2, and pooled variance is (2+2)/(3+3−2)=1. Standard error of the mean difference is √[1(1/3+1/3)]=√(2/3). Therefore t=(12−9)/√(2/3)=3√(3/2)≈3.6742 with 4 df. With grand mean 10.5, SSB=3(−1.5)²+3(1.5)²=13.5; between df=1 and within MS=1 give F=13.5. Squaring t yields 9/(2/3)=13.5. The sign of the original 3 kg/h difference is lost in a square, so F is sensitive to either direction. The right-tail F p-value matches the two-sided pooled t p-value, approximately 0.0213, not an arbitrary one-sided t p-value.

Exercise 6. A significant global test does not name winners

Task. Suppose the balanced example yields F(2,6)=7 and p=0.027. A manager writes: “All three processes are significantly different; switching from A to B will cause a 3 kg/h improvement with 97.3% certainty.” Diagnose every incorrect claim, then write a scientifically defensible two-sentence summary.

Full solution. The global alternative is only “not all three population means equal.” It does not prove A differs from B, B from C, or A from C separately, much less all three comparisons. The observed difference B−A=12−9=3 kg/h is descriptive, not an established causal effect; randomization and confounder control matter. The p-value is P(F≥7 | equal means and model assumptions), not 1−P(alternative), and cannot be turned into “97.3% certainty.” A defensible summary is: “In an illustrative nine-run synthetic dataset, the one-way equal-variance ANOVA rejected the hypothesis of three equal process population means, F(2,6)=7, p=0.027.” “The observed group means were 9, 12 and 10 kg/h; determining specific pairwise differences or causal benefits requires further design and appropriate analyses.”

Exercise 7. Separate descriptive effect size from inference

Task. Given SSB=14 and SST=20, compute η². If an analyst says that 70% of future factory output is caused by which process is chosen, explain the difference between the arithmetic result and that causal statement. Suppose you doubled every measurement's units by recording a converted scale: would F or η² change?

Full solution. η²=SSB/SST=14/20=0.70, meaning 70% of the corrected sample squared variation in this simple one-factor fit is assigned to between-group differences. This is not a causal population parameter inferred with certainty; unmeasured process correlations and tiny sample size limit generalization. Multiplying every reading by constant c=2 multiplies means and residuals by 2, sums of squares and mean squares by c²=4, but F=MSB/MSW and η²=SSB/SST are ratios, so the factor cancels. Therefore both remain 7 and 0.70. Units of SS change quadratically; ratios are unit-free. A change of scale does not produce new statistical evidence.

Exercise 8. Examine a violated design assumption

Task. A technician measures one batch three times under process A and independently measures one batch three times under process B, then enters six readings as if they represented six independent production runs. Explain the problem, why the usual F reference may mislead, and what change in data collection would make the experiment more credible. Do not invent a p-value.

Full solution. The independent experimental unit is a batch, not a sensor reading. Repeated measurements within one batch may share batch conditions and correlated errors, so treating them as independent creates pseudo-replication. The arithmetic ANOVA identities can still be computed, but N−k residual df do not capture independent information as claimed, and the classical F calibration is unsupported. A better design samples multiple separately assigned production batches for each process, ideally randomizing comparable batches and documenting run order, operators and measurement variation. If only repeated measures are available, a hierarchical or repeated-measures model must acknowledge clustering. Without the actual batch-level structure no valid p-value should be fabricated.

Exercise 9. Compare a change in scatter without changing means

Task. Imagine every group's three observations were changed so the mean stayed the same but each deviation from that mean doubled. Starting from the balanced example, derive the new SSW, SSB, SST, mean squares, F and η². What happens to the evidence against the global equality null, and why? Make no claim that such manipulation is a real experiment.

Full solution. Original residuals −1,0,1 become −2,0,2, so each group's within SS changes from 2 to 8, and SSW changes from 6 to 24. Group centers 9,12,10 and grand mean 31/3 do not change, hence SSB remains 14. The exact decomposition gives SST=14+24=38. Degrees of freedom remain 2 and 6, so MS_between=14/2=7 and MS_within=24/6=4. Thus F=7/4=1.75 and η²=14/38≈0.3684. With df (2,6), p=[6/(6+2·1.75)]³=(6/9.5)³≈0.2519. The same gaps among means look less compelling when individual runs are noisier; fail to reject at α=.05. The exercise shows why ANOVA judges between separation relative to within noise rather than ranking group means alone.

Exercise 10. Design a responsible report for a new application

Task. A hospital compares patient waiting time (minutes) across three clinics using separate patients. Describe how to define the unit, factor, response, null and alternative; list three information items needed before trusting an F result; and explain the interpretation of rejection. Assume no actual patient records were supplied; do not generate fictitious findings.

Full solution. The unit is a distinct patient visit only if visits are genuinely independent; repeated visits by the same patient require separate modeling. The factor is clinic (three categories); response is waiting time in minutes, with a prespecified definition such as minutes from check-in to clinician contact. The null is equality of the three clinic population mean waits, and the alternative is that not all are equal. Before inference, document sampling periods and allocation, independence/repeated visits, distribution of errors, variance patterns, missing records, and possible confounders such as appointment type. A global rejection provides evidence of at least one differing clinic mean under the chosen model, not a ranking of all clinics and not proof that a clinic's identity causes delay. Follow-up comparisons and possible robust methods require their own justified plan.

17. Final synthesis: explain every operation in your own words

The journey began with a question about population means, which could not be answered merely by observing different sample means. We introduced one categorical process factor and nine numerical readings, then defined an independent run as the meaningful unit. Three group means (9,12,10 kg/h) described the local centers; the grand mean 31/3 kg/h described their weighted overall center. Every deviation from the grand mean could be written as a residual around a group mean plus a displacement of the group mean from the grand mean. Squaring and summing, while using the zero-sum property of within-group residuals, yielded the exact identity SST=SSW+SSB.

The actual arithmetic made the identity concrete: within SS=6, between SS=14, and total SS=20 (kg/h)². Estimating three group means used three constraints, leaving N−k=6 error degrees of freedom; comparing three centers around a grand center used k−1=2 between degrees of freedom. Dividing sums of squares by the appropriate df gave MS_within=1 and MS_between=7 (kg/h)². Their unitless ratio F=7 was compared to an F distribution with df (2,6), producing p=0.027. Under the conventional α=0.05 threshold and the stated model, we rejected the global equality null—not every specific pairwise equality.

You should now be able to answer the following without reciting formulas mechanically. Why does SSW use group means while SSB uses the grand mean? Why must the group sizes appear in SSB? Why do squared residuals not cancel? Where did the cross-term go in SST=SSB+SSW? Why are there N−k residual rather than N degrees of freedom? Why does a larger F indicate stronger evidence when all else is equal? Which assumptions support the F probability, and which calculations remain correct even when those assumptions fail? Why does the global rejection leave the identity of different pairs unresolved?

The unbalanced example made the same logic work without equal group sizes and produced a contrasting p-value of 0.125. The two-group demonstration proved F=t² for the pooled equal-variance test. The altered-scatter exercise showed how the same mean differences can become less convincing when within variation increases. These transfers are the real test of understanding: the method is not a fixed arithmetic template for nine particular numbers, but a transparent comparison of modeled signal to residual noise. Before using it on real decisions, establish independent units, credible assumptions, relevant effect sizes, and appropriate reporting.

If you were encountering ANOVA for the first time, could you reproduce the full table, explain the purpose of every subtraction, square, sum, degree of freedom and ratio, and defend both your conclusion and its limits in ordinary language? If not, redo one exercise without looking at the answer, then verify with the two SS identities.
