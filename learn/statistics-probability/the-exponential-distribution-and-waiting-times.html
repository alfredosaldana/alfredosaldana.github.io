01From counts to a clock: what this guide will teach

A call center observes arrivals. Counting calls in twenty minutes answers one question; measuring how long until the next call answers another. The count can be zero, one or two, while a waiting time might be 2.4 minutes. The Exponential Distribution connects these two views under a specific arrival model. Our aim is to understand that connection well enough to choose a probability, explain the calculation and recognize when the model should change.

The central question is not simply whether a duration is positive. A positive duration can follow many distributions. We must ask what event ends the wait, where the clock starts and whether having waited already changes the distribution of the remaining time. Those questions will guide the formulas rather than appear as qualifications after the arithmetic.

By the end you should be able to derive the density and cumulative probability from a Poisson count, convert Rate and Scale between time units, calculate an interval and a conditional probability by different routes, derive moments, and invert the cumulative distribution to obtain a deadline. You will also work through counterexamples in which a positive wait has the same mean but a different residual law.

Preparation comes from continuous random variables, expectation and variance, and Poisson counts: Guides 10, 11 and 17. We recall the necessary ideas where they enter. A local bridge develops integration by parts from the product rule, and every improper integral begins on a finite interval. Programming is optional. The worked solutions at the end are part of the teaching, with the same attention to assumptions and interpretation as the opening sections.

02Define the event, the clock and the experiment

Our running case is a constructed mathematical example, not a dataset collected from a company. An event is one incoming call recorded at arrival. It is not a completed conversation, a person joining a queue or a distinct caller. Start a clock at a fixed reference time and define T as the number of minutes until the first arrival after that origin. The experiment ends at that arrival even if several other calls arrive later.

Assume a homogeneous Poisson Process with stipulated rate ρ = 6 arrivals per hour, equivalently 0.10 arrivals per minute. Homogeneous means that equal-length intervals have the same count distribution regardless of their location. Counts in disjoint intervals are independent. These are assumptions about the whole process; a Poisson-shaped histogram or one observed wait cannot establish them.

T = minutes from the fixed origin to the next arrivalρ = 0.10 min⁻¹; N(t) = arrivals during (0,t]

One realization could be T = 7.3 minutes. The parameter ρ is fixed in this model; the realized duration varies. If we repeatedly run this experiment, we need comparable origins and the same stipulated conditions. We cannot treat successive readings of the same unfinished wait as independent new observations.

The fixed origin matters. For a generic transport schedule, arriving at an arbitrary time and measuring the forward wait need not have the same distribution as the gap between departures. Under the homogeneous Poisson model, the forward wait from a fixed time and the independent interarrival gaps do have the same Exponential law. We will derive the forward-wait result first instead of assuming every positive duration is a new exponential experiment.

03Rate, Scale and the discipline of units

A rate measures how frequently events occur per unit time. A scale measures the characteristic size of a duration. In the rate parameterization used here, T ∼ Exponential(rate ρ), with ρ > 0. Define the scale β = 1/ρ. The symbol ∼ means “has distribution.” Because some books use λ for rate and others use β for scale, read the meaning and units before copying a parameter.

ρ = 0.10 min⁻¹; β = 1/0.10 = 10 minutesρt = (0.10 min⁻¹)(15 min) = 1.5

The product ρt must be dimensionless: it appears in an exponential. If t is measured in hours, use ρ = 6 h⁻¹; if t is measured in seconds, use ρ = 1/600 s⁻¹. The same physical wait has scale 10 minutes, 1/6 hour or 600 seconds. Changing units changes the numbers together, not the event being calculated.

The reciprocal relationship is worth checking in words. A higher arrival rate means a shorter typical wait, so increasing ρ should decrease β. Supplying 0.10 as a ten-minute scale would reverse that interpretation. A software argument called scale normally expects 10 here, not 0.10. The mean will later be derived as β; at this stage β is a parameter definition, not an unproved moment formula.

Keep rate separate from a probability over an interval. A rate of 0.10 per minute does not literally mean probability 0.10 in every interval of any length. The interval length must enter the calculation. We next use a zero-arrival probability to obtain that exact relationship.

04Derive the waiting law from no arrivals

The event T > t says that the first arrival has not occurred by time t. That happens exactly when N(t) = 0. The two statements describe the same outcome, one with a clock and one with a count. Under our process assumption, N(t) has Poisson distribution with expected count ρt.

P(T > t) = P(N(t) = 0), for t ≥ 0P(N(t) = 0) = e−ρt(ρt)⁰/0! = e−ρt

At t = 20 minutes, ρt = 2, so the probability that the wait exceeds twenty minutes is e−2 ≈ 0.135335. This is the same probability as zero calls in a twenty-minute window. It is not the probability of twenty calls or of a call exactly at minute twenty. The unit of t and the unit of the count parameter distinguish the questions.

Write S(t) = P(T > t), the Survival Function. Here “survival” simply means that the wait continues past t; no health or mortality interpretation is required. You can read it as an exceedance probability. At the clock origin S(0) = 1, and as t grows the curve decreases toward zero. Longer requirements of no arrivals are more restrictive events.

This derivation supplies a mechanism for Exponential waiting. It also identifies its limits: a distribution for the count in one interval does not automatically establish the full process assumptions across all intervals. We have stipulated that structure. When we later change the rate over time or hide different rates within a population, we must reconsider the residual waiting law rather than carry this conclusion over by habit.

05CDF and Survival: complementary questions

The Cumulative Distribution Function is F(t) = P(T ≤ t). It asks whether the arrival has happened by a deadline. S(t) asks whether it has not happened yet. These events partition the outcomes, so F(t) + S(t) = 1. The notation ≤ includes the deadline; a continuous waiting law assigns probability zero to the single endpoint, so strict and weak endpoints give the same interval probability here.

For t ≥ 0: S(t) = e−ρt; F(t) = 1 − e−ρtFor t < 0: S(t) = 1; F(t) = 0

The negative-time branch is necessary. The clock cannot finish before it starts, so a negative deadline has zero completion probability. Extending 1 − e−ρt to a negative t would give a negative number; that expression is only the nonnegative branch of the CDF.

For ten minutes, S(10) = e−1 ≈ 0.367879 and F(10) ≈ 0.632121. These probabilities describe the same population of waiting runs. About 63.2% finish by ten minutes under the model; the remaining 36.8% continue longer. Neither number is a probability density height. Both are dimensionless probabilities between zero and one.

The two curves move in opposite directions because they ask opposite questions, not because there are two different models. Their rates of change will also be opposite. Before differentiating, inspect the endpoints: F starts at zero and tends to one; S starts at one and tends to zero. A plot with these limits reversed usually signals that completion and exceedance have been confused.

06Density is probability per unit time

Differentiate the CDF on positive time. The derivative of e−ρt is −ρe−ρt; the minus sign in 1 minus that expression cancels it. The Probability Density Function is therefore f(t) = ρe−ρt for t ≥ 0, with zero density for negative times. We choose f(0) = ρ; changing a density at one point does not change any probability.

f(t) = F′(t) = ρe−ρt, t ≥ 0P(a < T ≤ b) = ∫ab f(t) dt, for 0 ≤ a < b

A density height is not P(T = t). The probability at one exact time is the integral across a zero-width interval and is zero. That does not make observed times impossible: every nonempty interval of positive length has positive probability. A measurement rounded to a minute represents such an interval, rather than an exact mathematical point.

The units make the distinction concrete. f(5) = .10e−.5 ≈ .060653 per minute. Multiplying that height by a short width, say .1 minute, approximates probability in a narrow interval near five. For [5,5.1], the exact probability is e−.5 − e−.51 ≈ .006035; the rectangle approximation is .006065. The declining curve explains why the rectangle using the left endpoint is slightly too large.

A density may exceed one without violating any probability rule. If ρ = 2 per minute, f(0) = 2 per minute, but the probability of an arrival within .1 minute is 1 − e−.2 ≈ .181269. An area is constrained by one; an inverse-time height is not. Reading the vertical axis correctly prevents a unit error from becoming a probability claim.

07Build an interval probability from the area

Suppose the question is: what is the probability that the next arrival occurs between five and fifteen minutes after the clock starts? First translate the words into an event, 5 ≤ T ≤ 15. Both endpoints are measured from the original clock, not from the moment five minutes have elapsed. This is an unconditional interval probability across all modeled runs.

P(5 ≤ T ≤ 15) = ∫515 .10e−.10t dt= [−e−.10t]515 = e−.5 − e−1.5= .6065306597 − .2231301601 = .3834004996

The primitive −e−.10t works because differentiating it restores the factor .10 and the positive sign. Evaluate at the upper endpoint and subtract the value at the lower: −e−1.5 − (−e−.5). Keeping both minus signs visible explains why the final answer is the earlier survival minus the later survival.

There are two useful independent checks. Subtract cumulative probabilities: F(15) − F(5) = .7768698399 − .3934693403. Or partition the outcomes into finishing by five, finishing from five to fifteen, and still waiting after fifteen. Their probabilities .3934693403, .3834004996 and .2231301601 add to one. Endpoint probabilities are zero, so these pieces do not double-count mass.

The shaded region in the figure is the answer, not the curve height at either endpoint. A wider interval need not always contain more probability than a shorter interval elsewhere: its position under the declining density matters. Compare equal-width intervals at different starting times before assuming that a duration width alone determines its probability.

08Why the entire density has area one

The density must be nonnegative and integrate to one. Nonnegativity follows from ρ > 0 and a positive exponential. The upper endpoint is unbounded, so the total area is an improper integral. That phrase means a limit of ordinary integrals over finite intervals; it does not mean substituting infinity into an algebraic expression as if it were a real number.

∫0L ρe−ρt dt = [−e−ρt]0L = 1 − e−ρL∫0∞ ρe−ρt dt = limL→∞(1 − e−ρL) = 1

For every finite L, the missing area is e−ρL. At sixty minutes in our case, it is e−6 ≈ .002479. A plot ending at sixty therefore displays almost all of the probability, but its endpoint is not the end of the support. At every finite deadline a positive tail remains.

This produces an important distinction: T is finite with probability one, yet there is no finite number that bounds every possible wait with certainty. The first statement follows because the tail tends to zero. The second follows because the tail is positive at each finite time. These statements coexist without contradiction.

Normalization checks a mathematical distribution. It does not verify whether real arrivals have constant rate or independent increments. Those questions require design information or data. A perfectly normalized curve can still be a poor description of a particular operation. Keep the two tasks separate: first establish that the formula defines a probability law, then consider whether the mechanism supports applying it.

10Condition on an unfinished wait: two routes

Seven minutes have passed without an arrival. What is the probability that the total wait exceeds fifteen minutes? The information restricts attention to runs with T > 7. We ask how many of those runs also satisfy T > 15. Because the second event implies the first, their intersection is simply T > 15.

P(T > 15 | T > 7) = P(T > 15)/P(T > 7)= e−1.5/e−.7 = e−.8 = .4493289641

The denominator is not optional. The unconditional probability .223130 describes all runs, including ones already completed before minute seven. The conditional probability describes only the smaller reference population still waiting. The unconditional probability of 7 < T ≤ 15 is S(7) − S(15) ≈ .273455; dividing that quantity by S(7) gives conditional completion probability .550671. These are three different questions.

For the second route, start with process information. Since the first seven minutes were empty, exceeding total time fifteen requires no arrival in the next eight minutes. Independent increments make the future count independent of the observed empty interval; stationarity gives Poisson(.10×8). Its zero probability is e−.8, agreeing with the ratio.

Define the additional wait R = T − 7, conditional on T > 7. The same answer is P(R > 8 | T > 7). The “eight” is a remaining duration; the “fifteen” is an original-clock deadline. Drawing both numbers on the same timeline makes the subtraction visible and prevents replacing fifteen with a fresh fifteen-minute wait.

11Prove Memoryless and state exactly what it preserves

The preceding calculation did not depend on the special numbers seven and fifteen. For any elapsed time s ≥ 0 and additional duration u ≥ 0, the event T > s + u is contained in T > s. Conditional probability and the exponential rule give a general identity.

P(T > s + u | T > s) = S(s+u)/S(s)= e−ρ(s+u)/e−ρs = e−ρu = P(T > u)

This is the Memoryless property: the remaining duration, given that the event has not happened, has the same distribution as a new wait. The total T has not become independent of the information T > s; that information already rules out all smaller total values. What remains invariant is the conditional distribution of T − s, not the distribution of T itself.

For example, after ten unfinished minutes, the probability of finishing in the next five is 1 − e−.5 ≈ .393469. After twenty unfinished minutes it is the same number. A fresh five-minute experiment also has that probability. None of these statements says the arrival is guaranteed or increasingly “due” as failures accumulate.

Once we derive the mean as ten minutes, the distinction also gives E[T − s | T > s] = 10 and E[T | T > s] = s + 10. At s = 7, expected remaining time is ten and conditional expected total is seventeen. Memoryless does not erase the seven minutes already spent. This is why remaining effort and total accumulated effort must be reported separately in an operational explanation.

The identity is a mathematical property of this distribution. It cannot establish that a real system is memoryless merely because we used an exponential formula. Aging, schedules and unobserved rates will provide useful counterexamples later.

12A fixed deadline and a fresh allowance are different

Memoryless is often misapplied when a deadline is expressed ambiguously. Compare “finish by minute fifteen from the original start” with “finish within fifteen more minutes.” After seven unfinished minutes, the first permits eight additional minutes; the second permits fifteen. The distribution of remaining time is the same, but the events have different widths.

Original deadline: P(T ≤ 15 | T > 7) = 1 − e−.10(15−7) ≈ .550671New allowance: P(T ≤ 22 | T > 7) = 1 − e−.10×15 ≈ .776870

If the original deadline has already passed, completion before it is impossible conditional on still waiting. More generally, for a deadline d and elapsed s, conditional completion is zero when d ≤ s, and is 1 − e−ρ(d−s) when d > s. Keeping that domain restriction prevents a negative remaining duration from producing a nonsensical probability.

Now consider a policy that grants ten additional minutes whenever someone asks for an update. The model probability of finishing during the newly granted ten is always .632121. But the original-clock deadline moves each time. That is a different policy from maintaining one fixed deadline, whose remaining opportunity shrinks. Memoryless describes the wait; it does not make two policies equivalent.

When communicating a result, report the origin, elapsed information, additional duration and probability in one sentence. For our example: “Given no arrival in the first seven minutes, the probability of an arrival by original minute fifteen is .550671 under the stipulated constant-rate model.” That sentence carries the conditioning and the interpretation that a bare decimal omits.

13A local bridge: integration by parts

To derive the expected wait, we need to integrate t times an exponential. Rather than introduce a rule as a memorized instruction, start from the derivative of a product: (uv)′ = u′v + uv′. Integrating both sides over a finite interval and rearranging yields integration by parts. The method transfers a derivative from one factor to the other.

∫ u(t)v′(t) dt = u(t)v(t) − ∫ u′(t)v(t) dt

Choose u(t) = t and v′(t) = ρe−ρt. Then u′(t) = 1, which is simpler, and v(t) = −e−ρt, which we can integrate again. The choice is useful because differentiating the polynomial reduces its degree while integrating the exponential keeps its form. We will apply the rule on [0,L] and only afterward let L grow.

There is a boundary issue to resolve before using the result at infinity. Products such as Le−ρL and L²e−ρL tend to zero even though one factor grows. “Infinity times zero” is not a numerical rule. For any fixed nonnegative integer m, the exponential series implies eρL ≥ (ρL)m+1/(m+1)! for L > 0. Therefore:

0 ≤ Lme−ρL ≤ (m+1)!/(ρm+1L) → 0

This bound is enough for the first and second moments below. It explains why exponential decay eventually dominates a fixed polynomial and justifies discarding the boundary terms. It does not mean every product involving a vanishing exponential has limit zero: the growth of the other factor must still be considered. Here we have stated exactly which factors occur and why their limits are controlled.

14Derive and interpret the mean waiting time

The expected value of a continuous variable is a probability-weighted average, written as an integral. Multiplying t by f(t) weights each possible duration by its density. For the Exponential law, use the integration choice just developed. Keep a finite cutoff L so the boundary calculation remains explicit.

E[T] = limL→∞ ∫0L tρe−ρt dt∫0L tρe−ρt dt = [−te−ρt]0L + ∫0L e−ρt dt= −Le−ρL + (1 − e−ρL)/ρE[T] = 0 + 1/ρ = β

The sign of the remaining integral is positive because the parts formula subtracts a negative v. At the lower boundary t = 0, the product term is zero. At the upper boundary, the preceding limit argument makes Le−ρL vanish. Both steps matter: simply stating 1/ρ would hide why the integral is finite and why the reciprocal appears.

For ρ = .10 per minute, the mean is ten minutes. It is not a deadline, the most likely exact time or the duration of every run. The density decreases from the origin, but a long right tail raises the average. Completion by the mean has probability 1 − e−1 ≈ .632121, leaving about 36.8% of waits above it.

The units provide a check: inverse minutes inverted gives minutes. If you changed to an hourly rate of six, the result would be 1/6 hour, again ten minutes. A mean that changes physically when only the clock unit changes reveals a parameter-convention error rather than new information about arrivals.

15Derive the second moment and variance

Variance measures the average squared deviation from the mean. It is convenient to calculate E[T²] first and then use Var(T) = E[T²] − E[T]². This identity does not say that squaring a mean equals averaging squares; the difference between those operations is exactly the variance.

Apply integration by parts again with u(t) = t², u′(t) = 2t and v(t) = −e−ρt. The boundary term now involves L²e−ρL, whose limit we justified. The remaining integral is related to the one used for E[T].

E[T²] = limL→∞{−L²e−ρL + 2∫0L te−ρt dt}∫0∞ te−ρt dt = E[T]/ρ = 1/ρ²E[T²] = 2/ρ²; Var(T) = 2/ρ² − (1/ρ)² = 1/ρ²SD(T) = √Var(T) = 1/ρ

At .10 per minute, E[T²] = 200 minutes², Var(T) = 100 minutes² and Standard Deviation is ten minutes. Mean and SD share the numerical value ten and have time units; variance has squared time units. A variance of one hundred does not mean a typical wait lasts one hundred minutes. SD is a scale of spread around the mean, not another expected duration.

As a numerical check, integrate (t − 10)²f(t) directly. It gives one hundred, agreeing with the raw-moment subtraction. The direct centered integral is useful because it checks the interpretation of variance by a different expression. It does not imply that waits are symmetrically distributed around ten: Exponential is right-skewed, bounded below by zero and unbounded above.

16Invert the CDF: median and quantiles

A quantile answers a different question from an average: how long must the deadline be so that a specified fraction of waits finishes by it? For 0 < u < 1, define qu by F(qu) = u. The CDF is continuous and strictly increasing on nonnegative time, so this equation has one solution there.

1 − e−ρqᵤ = u ⇒ e−ρqᵤ = 1 − u−ρqu = ln(1−u) ⇒ qu = −ln(1−u)/ρ

The logarithm is negative because 1−u is between zero and one; the leading minus sign makes the quantile positive. For u = .50, the median is ln(2)/.10 ≈ 6.931472 minutes. It is below the mean of ten because the long right tail influences the mean more strongly. Half the waits finish by the median, whereas about 63.2% finish by the mean.

As u approaches one the quantile grows without bound. There is no finite q₁ that guarantees completion with probability one. At u = 0 we can define q₀ = 0 by continuity. A numerical routine returning infinity for the endpoint one is reflecting the support, not failing to find a finite deadline. Match the probability target to the question before choosing mean, median or a high quantile.

17From a quantile to a reporting decision

Suppose a report uses whole-minute deadlines and requires at least .95 model probability of one arrival. The continuous .95 quantile is 29.9573227355 minutes. If only integer deadlines are permitted, choose the smallest integer d satisfying F(d) ≥ .95. Rounding to the nearest integer can happen to work here, but the general operation is to round upward and verify the neighbors.

F(29) = 1 − e−2.9 ≈ .944976780 < .95F(30) = 1 − e−3 ≈ .950212932 ≥ .95

Since F increases, every earlier integer also fails and thirty is minimal. The integer restriction belongs to the reporting rule; it does not turn T into a discrete distribution. A call can still arrive at 29.4 minutes. Likewise, rounding an observed duration to whole minutes produces interval information about T, not an exact integer-valued exponential variable.

This calculation is conditional on a known, stipulated rate. A real report that estimates ρ from limited data has another source of uncertainty. Plugging an estimate into the same formula gives a model calculation at that estimate, not automatic .95 performance over all plausible rates. Rate estimation and confidence intervals are later topics; recognizing the distinction now prevents overclaiming.

Finally, our event is “at least one arrival by a deadline.” It is not “every caller is served within thirty minutes,” “a queue never forms,” or “an agent should be scheduled for thirty minutes.” Service durations, parallel servers and arrivals after the first require additional variables. A useful probability model can inform a decision without deciding an operational policy by itself.

18Compare rates and standardize a duration

Hold the event definition and time unit fixed, and compare rates .05, .10 and .20 per minute. Their scales are twenty, ten and five minutes. At fifteen minutes their completion probabilities are 1−e−.75, 1−e−1.5 and 1−e−3: approximately .527633, .776870 and .950213. Doubling the rate halves the mean, but it does not double each event probability.

The density with a higher rate is taller at the origin and decays faster. Its total area remains one. Comparing only heights can therefore give the wrong impression that an entire model has “more probability”; all models have the same total probability, allocated over different durations.

Define Z = ρT. Its units cancel, so it is a dimensionless standardized wait. For z ≥ 0, P(Z > z) = P(T > z/ρ) = e−z. Thus Z has Exponential rate one, mean one and variance one. A value z = 2 means a wait of two mean durations, whether the original mean was five or twenty minutes.

Do not confuse standardizing one variable with adding independent waits. Multiplying T by a positive constant c gives a new Exponential rate ρ/c and scale c/ρ. Adding two independent Exponential waits does not simply rescale one wait; its mean–variance relationship changes. We will check that difference explicitly before connecting to the next guide.

19A rate is not a probability per window

Consider a short interval of length Δ minutes. Conditional on still waiting at its start, the exact probability of completion during that interval is 1−e−ρΔ. For a very small product ρΔ, the exponential expansion gives e−ρΔ ≈ 1−ρΔ, so probability is approximately ρΔ. The approximation concerns a small dimensionless product, not every interval called “short” in ordinary language.

Exact: 1 − e−ρΔ; first-order approximation: ρΔ

With ρ=.10 and Δ=1 minute, the exact value is .0951626, compared with .10. For Δ=10, it is .632121, compared with 1. For Δ=20 the linear expression is 2, which cannot be a probability. These examples show why a rate can be multiplied by time to obtain an expected count, while a completion probability needs the exponential transformation.

There is another useful comparison. The unconditional probability of an arrival between s and s+Δ is S(s)[1−e−ρΔ]. The conditional probability given no arrival before s drops the S(s) factor. Fewer runs remain available at later original-clock times, even though the chance of completion among those remaining is unchanged. This reconciles a declining density with Memoryless.

Formal Hazard analysis studies this local conditional event rate and is developed in Guide 73. Here it is enough to recognize the mechanism: compare the next short interval among unfinished runs, then distinguish that conditional question from the fraction of all runs finishing near a late time. We have not assumed that the density itself is constant; it is the local conditional rate that is constant in this model.

20Same positive mean, different residual law

A counterexample helps identify what Exponential assumes. Let U be uniform between zero and twenty minutes: its density is 1/20 on that interval and zero outside. It is a mathematical comparison with the same mean ten as our exponential wait, not a claim about an observed transport system. Every equal-length subinterval inside its support has equal unconditional probability.

P(U > t) = (20−t)/20, for 0 ≤ t ≤ 20P(U > 15 | U > 10) = (5/20)/(10/20) = 1/2P(U > 5) = 15/20 = 3/4

After ten unfinished minutes, five additional unfinished minutes have probability one half, not the three quarters of a fresh five-minute wait. Memoryless fails. Knowing U > 10 restricts the uniform distribution to (10,20), so the residual U−10 is uniform on (0,10), with mean five rather than ten. The finite endpoint moves closer as elapsed time increases.

Compare with Exponential(.10): the corresponding conditional tail and fresh tail are both e−.5 ≈ .606531, and the remaining mean stays ten. Equal positive means do not determine the distributions, and positivity alone does not establish Memoryless. The difference comes from the mechanism and support, not an arithmetic inconsistency.

The comparison figure uses a common time axis and probability scale. Each original tail starts at one, but one reaches zero at twenty while the other approaches zero without reaching it at any finite time. To check memorylessness visually, shift to residual time after a specified elapsed duration; comparing only the two original curves is not itself a test of the conditional identity.

21A scheduled rate change breaks the constant-rate law

Now retain independent increments but change the arrival schedule. Suppose the rate is .05 per minute during the first ten minutes and .15 afterward. The expected count accumulates at the current rate. Define A(t), the cumulative expected count, by adding the expected counts of the pieces. This is a localized illustration, not a full treatment of nonhomogeneous processes.

A(t) = .05t for 0 ≤ t ≤ 10A(t) = .5 + .15(t−10) for t > 10S(t) = P(N(t)=0) = e−A(t)

For a total wait of fifteen minutes, A(15)=.5+.75=1.25. Given that the first eight minutes were empty, five additional minutes cross the change point at ten. The next two minutes contribute .05×2=.10 expected arrivals, and the following three contribute .15×3=.45. Thus the residual tail is e−.55 ≈ .576950.

A fresh five-minute wait starting at the original origin has tail e−.25 ≈ .778801. The two tails differ, so the original waiting distribution is not Memoryless. Future increments may still be independent of past counts; the scheduled rate over the future interval is simply not identical to the initial rate schedule. Independence and a constant rate are separate conditions.

The density on each side is the current rate multiplied by S(t). S and F remain continuous at ten, while density can jump when the schedule changes. This example explains why one constant-rate exponential may fail even when the absence of past arrivals has no causal effect on the future mechanism. The calendar or clock position already contains relevant information.

22Hidden rates: waiting can change what we know

Consider a different mechanism. At the start of a run, choose a slow state with rate .05 or a fast state with rate .15, each with probability one half, and keep that state fixed throughout the run. Conditional on its state, the wait is Exponential and Memoryless. Before we know the state, the distribution is a mixture of the two laws.

Smix(t) = .5e−.05t + .5e−.15tP(slow | T > s) = e−.05s/(e−.05s + e−.15s)

At s=10, the slow-state weight rises from .5 to about .731059. Long unfinished runs are more likely to have come from the slow component because fast runs tend to finish earlier. This is conditioning, not a physical slowdown caused by the observer. The state was selected once and never changed during the run.

The fresh five-minute tail is .5e−.25+.5e−.75 ≈ .625584. After ten unfinished minutes, the additional five-minute tail is Smix(15)/Smix(10) ≈ .696388. Both components are memoryless, but the mixture is not, because the weights among unfinished runs change with elapsed time.

Its mean is .5(20)+.5(1/.15)=13.333333 minutes. Averaging the rates gives .10, whose reciprocal is ten; that is a different model and a different mean. Averaging reciprocals and taking the reciprocal of an average are not interchangeable operations. This example is a useful warning against pooling heterogeneous waiting populations and assuming one exponential parameter will preserve all their probabilities.

23The next event is different from several events

The Exponential waiting time ends at the next arrival. If the task requires two arrivals after the origin, let T₁ and T₂ be the independent interarrival gaps and write W=T₁+T₂. Linearity gives E[W]=20 minutes and independence gives Var(W)=200 minutes² in our case. An Exponential distribution with mean twenty would have variance four hundred, so W cannot have that exponential law.

This moment comparison is a quick disproof, not a method for identifying every possible distribution from two moments. Here the Poisson count provides a second route. The second arrival has not occurred by t exactly when N(t) is zero or one. Consequently:

P(W > t) = P(N(t) ≤ 1) = e−ρt(1+ρt)

That tail is not a single exponential. At ten minutes it is 2e−1 ≈ .735759, while an Exponential mean twenty would give e−.5 ≈ .606531. Waiting for several arrivals introduces progress information, just as waiting for several Bernoulli successes did in Guide 16.

For a contrasting operation, suppose two independent channels have rates ρ₁ and ρ₂, and we stop at the first arrival from either. The minimum exceeds t only if both waits exceed t, so its tail is e−ρ₁te−ρ₂t=e−(ρ₁+ρ₂)t. A minimum can therefore remain Exponential while a sum does not. State whether the task means “either source first” or “two arrivals accumulated.” Guide 19 develops sums through the Gamma/Erlang family; the distinction here prepares that next step.

24Boundaries, rounding and unfinished observations

The proper finite-valued Exponential family requires ρ>0. If an arrival mechanism has rate zero forever, no arrival occurs and the wait is infinite; it is not an Exponential distribution with ordinary finite mean. A very large positive rate produces a short but still continuous wait. Negative rates and negative durations are outside the model.

Suppose an observation ends at a fixed cutoff c=10 minutes and we record V=min(T,c). For v−1≈.367879. All longer unfinished waits are recorded at the cutoff. V is not an ordinary continuous Exponential variable even though the underlying T is.

A completion indicator distinguishes a true arrival observed before the cutoff from a run that remains unfinished. Knowing only the recorded value ten does not provide an exact ten-minute completed duration. Recording rules therefore belong in the variable definition, not as an afterthought in the analysis. Formal censoring methods are developed in Guide 72; here we only identify the information that was and was not observed.

Rounding is another observation mechanism. If a duration is rounded to the nearest minute, a recorded value five typically represents 4.5≤T<5.5, with a probability calculated from an interval. It does not contradict P(T=5)=0 for the underlying continuous clock. Extra measurement conventions are needed near zero and at ties, but they do not change the underlying density into point probabilities at every integer.

These distinctions are practical: a mathematically correct Exponential calculation can answer the wrong question if the actual recorded variable is capped, rounded or defined from a different origin. Audit the measurement before interpreting the parameter.

25Reproduce the central calculations with Python

The following optional program translates the same definitions into deterministic calculations. It does not generate observations or fit a rate. The inputs are the stipulated .10 per minute, the interval [5,15], elapsed seven minutes and completion probability .95. Python’s math module supplies exponentials and logarithms; no downloaded dataset or scientific package is required to run this example.

Before executing, predict the direction of each result. The interval probability should be positive and smaller than S(5). Conditional survival beyond original fifteen should be larger than unconditional survival beyond fifteen because we have restricted attention to runs unfinished at seven. The .95 quantile should exceed both median and mean. These expectations help detect a correct-looking answer to the wrong event.

from math import exp, expm1, log1p, ceil, isfinite

def check_rate(rate):
    if not isfinite(rate) or rate <= 0:
        raise ValueError("rate must be finite and positive")

def survival(time, rate):
    check_rate(rate)
    if not isfinite(time):
        raise ValueError("time must be finite")
    return 1.0 if time < 0 else exp(-rate * time)

def cdf(time, rate):
    check_rate(rate)
    if not isfinite(time):
        raise ValueError("time must be finite")
    return 0.0 if time < 0 else -expm1(-rate * time)

def quantile(probability, rate):
    check_rate(rate)
    if not 0 <= probability < 1:
        raise ValueError("probability must be in [0, 1)")
    return -log1p(-probability) / rate

arrival_rate = 0.10  # per minute
interval_probability = survival(5, arrival_rate) - survival(15, arrival_rate)
cdf_difference = cdf(15, arrival_rate) - cdf(5, arrival_rate)
assert abs(interval_probability - cdf_difference) < 1e-14
residual_ratio = survival(15, arrival_rate) / survival(7, arrival_rate)
fresh_eight = survival(8, arrival_rate)
assert abs(residual_ratio - fresh_eight) < 1e-14
deadline_95 = quantile(0.95, arrival_rate)
assert abs(cdf(deadline_95, arrival_rate) - 0.95) < 1e-14
integer_deadline = ceil(deadline_95)
assert cdf(integer_deadline - 1, arrival_rate) < 0.95
assert cdf(integer_deadline, arrival_rate) >= 0.95
print(f"P(5 <= T <= 15): {interval_probability:.10f}")
print(f"P(T > 15 | T > 7): {residual_ratio:.10f}")
print(f"95% quantile (minutes): {deadline_95:.10f}")
print("Minimum whole-minute deadline:", integer_deadline)
print(f"Mean, variance: {1/arrival_rate:.1f}, {1/arrival_rate**2:.1f}")

Expected output

P(5 <= T <= 15): 0.3834004996
P(T > 15 | T > 7): 0.4493289641
95% quantile (minutes): 29.9573227355
Minimum whole-minute deadline: 30
Mean, variance: 10.0, 100.0

After execution, compare the printed interval value with both the integral and CDF difference developed earlier. The two residual-tail calculations agree: a survival ratio and a fresh eight-minute tail. The quantile is verified by substitution, and the integer deadline is checked on both sides. Those are independent expressions of the same events, not extra observations supporting the industrial model.

The implementation uses expm1 and log1p to avoid subtracting nearly equal floating-point values in small-probability calculations. In Python, expm1(x) evaluates eˣ−1 accurately near zero and log1p(x) evaluates ln(1+x) there. They preserve the mathematics while improving numerical behavior. Inputs are checked before use. The program is still a teaching example, not a certification of every extreme numerical regime or an estimation procedure.

26Practice with complete, reasoned solutions

Attempt each problem before opening its solution. State the event and the clock before choosing a formula. The problems change a unit, a conditioning event or a mechanism; this is deliberate. Transfer means identifying what still holds and what must be rebuilt, rather than replacing numbers in one expression. The solutions retain intermediate reasoning so that a disagreement can be traced to a model choice, an endpoint or arithmetic.

A. Change the time unit without changing the event

A homogeneous arrival process has rate 12 per hour. Define T in minutes. Find its rate, scale, mean, variance and P(T > 7.5). Check the tail again using hours.

Complete worked solution

The clock is measured in minutes, so divide the hourly rate by sixty: ρ=12/60=.20 per minute. Scale and mean are 1/.20=5 minutes. Variance is 1/.20²=25 minutes², and SD is five minutes. The rate is not twelve when the time argument is 7.5 minutes.

P(T > 7.5) = e−.20×7.5 = e−1.5 ≈ .2231301601

For the independent unit check, 7.5 minutes is .125 hour. Using twelve per hour gives e−12×.125, the same result. The hourly mean is 1/12 hour and the variance is 1/144 hour². Multiplying mean by sixty gives five minutes; multiplying variance by sixty squared gives twenty-five minutes². Applying only sixty to variance would fail dimensional consistency.

The event remains a wait exceeding a specified duration. Changing its numerical representation does not change the arrival process or probability. About 22.3% of waits exceed one and a half mean durations under this model. That fraction is not a forecast that one particular run must last either five or 7.5 minutes.

B. Separate an area from a height and a point

Let T have rate .05 per minute. Find P(2 ≤ T ≤ 8), f(5), P(T=5), and P(−2 ≤ T ≤ 3). Explain the different meanings.

Complete worked solution

The interval probability subtracts survival at its endpoints, because both are nonnegative. It is an area, whereas f(5) is an inverse-minute height.

P(2 ≤ T ≤ 8) = e−.1 − e−.4 ≈ .2345173720f(5) = .05e−.25 ≈ .0389400392 per minute

The exact point probability is zero. It is not .038940: multiplying a height by a zero width gives zero area. An observed rounded five could represent an interval and therefore have positive recording probability, but that is a measurement question different from T=5.

The interval from −2 to3 intersects the support only on [0,3]. Thus its probability is F(3)−F(−2)=1−e−.15≈.1392920236, since F(−2)=0. Extending the positive-time exponential formula to −2 would give a negative CDF and the wrong answer.

The results pass different checks: the two probabilities lie between zero and one, the density has inverse-time units, and the exact point has no mass. A checklist that merely checks whether every reported number is below one would miss the central difference between density and probability.

C. Keep elapsed and total time separate

At rate .10 per minute, no arrival has occurred in twelve minutes. Find the probability of finishing by original minute twenty, the expected additional wait, and the expected total wait. What if the original deadline was minute ten?

Complete worked solution

The condition is T>12 and the deadline leaves eight minutes, not twenty. Using conditional completion or a fresh residual wait gives:

P(T ≤ 20 | T > 12) = 1 − S(20)/S(12)= 1 − e−.8 ≈ .5506710359

The expected remaining duration stays ten minutes by Memoryless. Expected total duration conditional on being unfinished is twelve plus ten, or twenty-two minutes. An expected total later than the deadline does not make the probability of finishing by that deadline zero: an average and a probability answer different questions.

If the original deadline was ten, it is already past. Conditional on T>12, P(T≤10)=0. A blind substitution of d−s=−2 into the positive residual formula would give a negative “probability”; the domain check must come first.

D. A different completion target

For the main rate .10, find the median, the .90 quantile, and the smallest whole-minute deadline with completion probability at least .90. Compare with the mean.

Complete worked solution

Inverting the CDF gives qᵤ=−ln(1−u)/.10. The median is −ln(.5)/.10≈6.931472 minutes. The .90 quantile is −ln(.1)/.10≈23.025851 minutes, which is not twenty-three if the target is a strict requirement of at least .90.

F(23) ≈ .899741156 < .90F(24) ≈ .909282047 ≥ .90

Monotonicity makes twenty-four the smallest permitted integer deadline. Rounding the CDF at twenty-three to .90 before comparing would incorrectly accept a failing value. The reporting precision cannot weaken the target.

The mean remains ten minutes; changing a reporting probability did not alter the process. It lies above the median and below the .90 quantile. Substituting ten into F gives about .632121, so using the mean as a .90 deadline would be unjustified.

For a substitution check, S(q₉₀)=exp(ln(.1))=.1 and F(q₉₀)=.9. This check is independent of the integer reporting rule: the exact continuous quantile reaches the target, while an integer deadline must respect the rounding restriction as well.

E. A bounded positive wait

Let U be Uniform(0,20). Given U>16, calculate P(U>19), the conditional expected remaining wait, and the corresponding quantities for Exponential(.10). Compare with a fresh three-minute tail.

Complete worked solution

Conditioning the uniform wait on U>16 leaves a uniform duration between16 and20. There is one minute of support above19 and four minutes above16, hence conditional tail1/4=.25. The residual U−16 is Uniform(0,4), whose mean is two minutes.

P(U > 19 | U > 16) = [(20−19)/20]/[(20−16)/20] = .25P(U > 3) = 17/20 = .85

Those tails differ sharply, so the uniform wait fails Memoryless despite being positive and having unconditional mean ten. In contrast, the exponential conditional tail is e−.10×3≈.740818, equal to its fresh three-minute tail. Its expected remaining wait is ten minutes, not two.

The mechanism explains the contrast: sixteen unfinished minutes put the uniform wait near a known upper endpoint. Exponential has no such finite endpoint and its residual law stays invariant. Do not infer the exponential property from positivity, a mean or an observed long wait. These are deliberately stipulated models, so the comparison teaches a distinction; it does not identify which one describes an unobserved real system.

F. Cross a scheduled change point

Use rate .05 until minute10 and .15 afterward. Given no arrival by minute8, find the probability of finishing by minute13. Compare with finishing within five minutes from the original origin.

Complete worked solution

The residual interval is (8,13], which contains two minutes at the low rate and three at the high rate. The expected count there is .05×2+.15×3=.55. Zero arrivals in that interval has probability e−.55; completing at least once is its complement.

P(T ≤ 13 | T > 8) = 1 − e−.55 ≈ .4230501896P(T ≤ 5) = 1 − e−.25 ≈ .2211992169

For the ratio check, A(13)=.5+.15×3=.95 and A(8)=.4. The ratio S(13)/S(8)=exp(−.95+.4)=exp(−.55) gives the same residual survival. Subtracting from one recovers the answer.

The probabilities differ because the future five minutes cross into a higher scheduled rate, not because observing eight empty minutes altered the process. Independent increments permit conditioning away the past count; they do not imply stationarity. A single exponential rate would discard the schedule and answer a different model. The required distinction is between independent future counts and identical future behavior at all clock positions.

G. Update a hidden rate state

In the equally weighted .05/.15 rate mixture, a run is unfinished at20 minutes. Find the conditional probability of the slow state and the probability of remaining unfinished for ten more minutes. Compare with the fresh ten-minute tail.

Complete worked solution

The likelihood of remaining unfinished at20 is e−1 in the slow state and e−3 in the fast state. Multiply each by its prior weight one half and normalize. The common half cancels, giving slow-state weight e−1/(e−1+e−3)≈.880797.

Residual tail = Smix(30)/Smix(20) ≈ .5608281999Fresh tail = Smix(10) ≈ .4148304099

Equivalently, weight the next-ten-minute component tails by the updated state probabilities: .880797e−.5+.119203e−1.5. Keeping unrounded weights gives the same .560828 answer. Retaining the original half-and-half weights after observing the unfinished run would ignore informative selection.

Each component separately keeps its own residual law; the population mixture changes because long waits favor the slow component. The state is fixed within a run, so this is not a story of a machine physically slowing as time passes. State the conditioning information before interpreting the apparent persistence of a pooled waiting population.

H. Sum, minimum and scaling are different operations

Two independent waits each have rate .10 per minute. Compare their sum W, minimum M and doubled first wait V=2T₁. Give means, variances and identify which are Exponential.

Complete worked solution

For the sum, means add and independent variances add: E[W]=20 and Var(W)=200. An Exponential with mean20 would have variance400, so the sum is not Exponential. Its tail is e−.1t(1+.1t), obtained from fewer than two Poisson arrivals.

For the minimum, independence gives P(M>t)=P(T₁>t)P(T₂>t)=e−.2t. Hence M is Exponential with rate .20, mean5 and variance25. It ends at the first arrival of either channel, not when both have arrived.

For V=2T₁, P(V>v)=P(T₁>v/2)=e−.05v. Thus V is Exponential with rate .05, mean20 and variance400. It shares the sum’s mean but not its variability or distribution. Multiplying one random wait by two is not adding a new independent source of waiting.

The units remain minutes for all three variables, yet their experiment definitions differ. Use the event ending each clock to decide the transformation first. Choosing a formula because two reported means happen to match would conceal exactly the distinction this exercise is designed to test.

I. An observation cutoff changes the distribution

Let T have rate .10 and observe V=min(T,10). Find the mass at10, the chance of a completed event before the cutoff and E[V]. Explain why averaging capped records is not automatically estimating E[T].

Complete worked solution

The cutoff aggregates every underlying wait longer than ten at one recorded value. Its mass is S(10)=e−1≈.367879. Completion before the cutoff has probability F(10)=1−e−1≈.632121; exact completion at ten has probability zero in the continuous model.

The mean of the capped observation combines the density on [0,10) and the atom at ten:

E[V] = ∫010 t(.10)e−.10t dt + 10e−1= −10e−1 + 10(1−e−1) + 10e−1= 10(1−e−1) ≈ 6.3212055883 minutes

The boundary terms cancel, providing a direct check of the mixed observation law. This expectation is below the underlying mean10 because every longer duration has been shortened in the record. A raw average of such capped values targets the mean of V under repeated observation, not the mean of T.

Keep a completion indicator and the cutoff rule. Formal methods for incomplete durations are developed later, but recognizing the information loss already prevents interpreting an unfinished record as an exact completed event. The cap belongs to observation, not to the support of the original waiting mechanism.

27A reusable reasoning workflow and a working vocabulary

Begin with a sentence defining the event and clock: “T is the time in minutes from this origin until that arrival.” State the source of the rate and whether it is stipulated or estimated. Ask which assumptions justify a homogeneous Poisson connection; do not infer them from a convenient fit or positive durations alone.

Translate the question before calculating. Use an area or CDF difference for an interval, survival for an exceedance, and a ratio or new increment for a conditional wait. Distinguish total deadlines from additional durations. For a quantile, invert the CDF and substitute back; if reporting integers, verify neighbors without premature rounding.

For a new mechanism, change the relevant probability model instead of forcing the old formula. A known schedule changes accumulated expected arrivals. A hidden rate state changes composition among unfinished runs. A cap changes the observed variable. A sum and a minimum define different completion events. Each can be analyzed, but it must be named correctly first.

Rate ρ
Events per unit time; positive for the finite Exponential law.
Scale β
Reciprocal rate, measured in time units.
PDF f(t)
Probability density; integrate it over an interval.
CDF F(t)
Probability of completion by the clock reading t.
Survival S(t)
Probability of remaining unfinished beyond t.
Residual wait
Additional duration given that the event has not happened.
Memoryless
Invariance of the conditional residual distribution.
Quantile qᵤ
Deadline with completion probability u.
Mixture
Population law obtained by weighting component laws.
Observation cutoff
End of observation; it need not be a completed duration.
