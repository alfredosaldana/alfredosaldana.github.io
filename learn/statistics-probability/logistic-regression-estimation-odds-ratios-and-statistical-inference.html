01. Why a binary outcome needs a different regression model

Equation / derivation · Bernoulli conditional probability
\[p(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x),\qquad Y\mid x\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p(x))\]

A university advisor wants to understand whether a student's completed preparation workshops are associated with finishing an optional academic support program. A student either completes it (recorded as 1) or does not (recorded as 0). This is a binary outcome, not a continuous measurement. If we used ordinary least squares without changing the model, fitted values could fall below zero or exceed one. They would then be impossible to interpret as probabilities. The problem is not simply that the data contain zeros and ones: it is that the conditional mean of a Bernoulli variable is a probability, and its variance changes with that probability.

Our scientific question concerns the conditional probability of completing the program at a given level of preparation. The response is \(Y_i\), with values 0 and 1; the predictor \(x_i\) is the number of preparation workshops, from 0 to 3. Each observation is one student, workshops are counted in sessions, and the outcome has no physical unit. The study is illustrative, observational and entirely synthetic. It is not evidence about any real university. Observational association must not be represented as the causal effect of workshops. Prior motivation, access, mentoring, and schedule availability could be shared causes of preparation and completion.

Logistic regression provides a coherent response to the mathematical part of this problem: it maps a linear score to a number strictly between zero and one. We will construct that map, derive the likelihood, take a genuine numerical estimation step, evaluate uncertainty and identify when the method breaks down. At each stage the question is not merely which formula should I use?, but which probability statement does that formula justify?

02. Essential prerequisite: the Bernoulli distribution

For a student with predictors \(x\), define \(p(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x)\). The notation means a probability conditional on a workshop count; it does not mean that the same student can be observed repeatedly at every value of \(x\). The Bernoulli model says \(P(Y=1\mid x)=p(x)\) and \(P(Y=0\mid x)=1-p(x)\). A convenient single formula for an observed \(y\in\{0,1\}\) is \(p(x)^y[1-p(x)]^{1-y}\). When \(y=1\), the exponent zero removes the second factor. When \(y=0\), it removes the first. This elementary substitution is the foundation of the likelihood that follows.

The conditional expectation is \(E(Y\mid x)=0[1-p(x)]+1p(x)=p(x)\). Because \(Y^2=Y\) for zeros and ones, the variance is \(E(Y^2\mid x)-E(Y\mid x)^2=p(x)-p(x)^2=p(x)[1-p(x)]\). At a 50% probability the variance is 0.25; at a 10% probability it is 0.09. Therefore equal-variance assumptions borrowed mechanically from classical linear regression are unsuitable for the binary response itself.

The model treats students as independent conditional on the predictors used for inference. If observations come from repeated measurements, teams, classrooms or schools, independence may fail even when the mathematical curve looks satisfactory. Logistic coefficients can still be computed, but ordinary standard errors may be misleading. We will keep this assumption visible rather than burying it underneath a significance test.

03. Probability, odds and log-odds are not synonyms

Equation / derivation · Probability → odds → log-odds
\[\operatorname{odds}(p)=\frac{p}{1-p},\qquad \operatorname{logit}(p)=\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\]

Suppose a completion probability is \(p=0.80\). The probability is four completions per five comparable opportunities in the long-run model. The odds in favor are the success probability divided by the failure probability: \(p/(1-p)=0.8/0.2=4\). The odds are 4 to 1, not 80% and not 4%. They compare two competing probabilities. Their domain is from zero to positive infinity, whereas probabilities are restricted to the unit interval.

At \(p=0.20\), odds are \(0.20/0.80=0.25\); at \(p=0.50\), odds equal 1. An odds value of 1 therefore corresponds to equal chances of the two outcomes. Converting back is as important as converting forward. If odds are \(o\), solve \(o=p/(1-p)\): multiply by \(1-p\), collect the \(p\) terms, and obtain \(p=o/(1+o)\). For odds of 4, this returns 4/5=0.8; for odds of 0.25, it returns 0.25/1.25=0.2.

The log-odds, or logit, is the natural logarithm of the odds. A probability of 0.8 gives \(\ln(4)=1.3863\), a probability of 0.5 gives zero, and a probability of 0.2 gives \(\ln(0.25)=-1.3863\). The transformation turns the interval of probabilities into the entire real line. That is why it is suited to a linear predictor. When a coefficient is measured in log-odds, adding a constant to that coefficient means multiplying odds, not adding a constant number of percentage points.

04. Build the logistic link instead of memorizing a curve

Equation / derivation · Inverse-logit model
\[\boxed{p(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1x)}}}\]

Let \(\eta(x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x\) be a linear score. We impose \(\ln[p(x)/(1-p(x))]=\eta(x)\). Exponentiate both sides to get \(p/(1-p)=e^{\eta}\). Multiplying out gives \(p=e^{\eta}-pe^{\eta}\); collecting terms yields \(p(1+e^{\eta})=e^{\eta}\). The resulting inverse logit is \(p(x)=e^{\eta(x)}/[1+e^{\eta(x)}]=1/[1+e^{-\eta(x)}]\). Each version is algebraically identical; choosing the first or second is a numerical implementation detail, not a change of model.

At a score of zero, \(p=1/2\). At \(\eta=1\), \(p\approx0.7311\); at \(\eta=-1\), \(p\approx0.2689\). A score of 2 gives about 0.8808, not a probability of 2. The curve approaches zero or one but does not reach either at finite coefficients. The function is increasing because its derivative with respect to the score equals \(p(1-p)>0\) for finite scores.

A positive \(\beta_1\) shifts log-odds upward as workshop count grows; a negative one shifts them downward. A zero slope means the fitted conditional probability is constant across workshop counts. This sign interpretation is valid without claiming that the association is causal. The curve is nonlinear in probability even though the logit is linear in \(x\).

A 1 in the final column means completion, 0 means noncompletion. These rows were constructed to practice estimation; there was no random sample from a university and there are no missing values.

It does not prove that selection or omitted-variable bias is absent.

06. From Bernoulli probabilities to a likelihood for all students

Equation / derivation · Bernoulli log-likelihood
\[\ell(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[y_i\eta_i-\ln(1+e^{\eta_i})\right]\]

Conditional on the workshop counts and assuming independent outcomes, the joint probability of the observed sequence, viewed as a function of unknown coefficients, is its likelihood: \(L(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\prod_{i=1}^{16}p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}\), where \(p_i=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)\). Independence explains the product; Bernoulli sampling explains each factor. Maximum likelihood estimation selects coefficients that give the observed outcomes a larger likelihood than nearby coefficient choices within the model.

Products of many fractions can be extremely small. Taking a natural logarithm turns the product into the log-likelihood: \(\ell(\beta)=\sum_i[y_i\ln(p_i)+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)]\). Substituting the inverse-logit form simplifies this to \(\sum_i[y_i\eta_i-\ln(1+e^{\eta_i})]\). Both representations describe the same objective because logarithm is strictly increasing. The second form is particularly helpful for derivatives; robust software also uses numerically stable variants of the log-sum-exp expression.

At \(\beta_0=\beta_1=0\), all fitted probabilities are 0.5. Each student's likelihood factor is 0.5, whether the outcome is zero or one. Hence the full likelihood is \(0.5^{16}\) and \(\ell=16\ln(0.5)\approx-11.09035\). We have a reproducible baseline before applying optimization. In this study, a larger log-likelihood means a value closer to zero because all individual probability logarithms are nonpositive. Likelihood is not the probability that the model is true.

07. Derive the score and show why the observations have weights

Equation / derivation · Score vector
\[U(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-p_i)\begin{pmatrix}1\\x_i\end{pmatrix}\]

Differentiate the log-likelihood with respect to the intercept and slope. Because \(dp_i/d\eta_i=p_i(1-p_i)\), the derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to \(\eta_i\) simplifies to \(y_i-p_i\). The derivative of \(\eta_i\) with respect to the intercept is 1; with respect to the slope it is \(x_i\). The resulting score vector is \(U(\beta)=\sum_i(y_i-p_i)(1,x_i)^T\). Its components are the sum of residuals on the probability scale and the sum of residuals multiplied by workshop counts.

At the zero-coefficient starting point, the first component is \(8-16(0.5)=0\). The second component equals \(\sum_ix_iy_i-0.5\sum_ix_i\). The successful students' workshop counts sum to 16, and all workshop counts sum to 24. Thus the slope score equals \(16-12=4\). A positive score means that a small positive change in slope increases log-likelihood locally at the starting point, while the intercept score is already zero there. It does not mean the final intercept must remain zero: parameters move together because their score equations are linked.

To stop the optimization, numerical software seeks a score near zero. The score is not a classical residual in the original outcome units, and its zero does not guarantee that each observed outcome is perfectly predicted. It states that weighted residual discrepancies balance in the directions defined by the model's columns. This connects logistic estimation to normal equations while explaining why its mathematics and variance calculations differ from least squares.

08. A full Newton–Raphson / Fisher scoring step

Equation / derivation · Complete Newton update
\[\begin{aligned}I_0&=\begin{pmatrix}4&6\\6&14\end{pmatrix},\quad U_0=\begin{pmatrix}0\\4\end{pmatrix}\\I_0^{-1}U_0&=\frac1{20}\begin{pmatrix}14&-6\\-6&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1.2\\0.8\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}\]

Define the design row \(z_i=(1,x_i)^T\) and let \(W\) be a diagonal matrix with entries \(w_i=p_i(1-p_i)\). The observed negative Hessian for this canonical Bernoulli-logit model is \(I(\beta)=\sum_iw_iz_iz_i^T=X^TWX\). Because each \(w_i\) is nonnegative, this information matrix measures local curvature in the two coefficient directions. Newton–Raphson maximization updates \(\beta^{new}=\beta^{old}+I(\beta^{old})^{-1}U(\beta^{old})\), provided the matrix is invertible.

Starting at \((0,0)\), every weight equals 0.25. There are 16 observations, \(\sum x_i=24\) and \(\sum x_i^2=56\). Therefore \(I_0=0.25\left(\begin{smallmatrix}16&24\\24&56\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&6\\6&14\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Its determinant is \(4(14)-6^2=20\), so it is invertible. The score is \((0,4)^T\). The matrix inverse is \(\frac1{20}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}14&-6\\-6&4\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Multiplication gives the step \((-1.2,0.8)^T\).

The first updated coefficient pair is therefore \(\beta^{(1)}=(-1.2,0.8)\). Its predicted probabilities for \(x=0,1,2,3\) are approximately 0.2315, 0.4013, 0.5987 and 0.7685. Recalculating the complete log-likelihood gives approximately −9.41038, higher than −11.09035. That is a numerical verification that this particular step improved the objective. Another iteration updates the probabilities and information weights; repeating until convergence gives the maximum likelihood coefficients below. One iteration is not the MLE, and increased likelihood at one step does not prove global model validity.

09. The fitted model and five essential substitutions

Equation / derivation · Numerical substitution
\[\begin{aligned}\hat\eta(2)&=-1.362276+0.908184(2)=0.454092\\\hat p(2)&=\frac1{1+e^{-0.454092}}\approx0.611612\end{aligned}\]

Independent numerical optimization of our sixteen rows yields \(\hat\beta_0\approx-1.362276\) and \(\hat\beta_1\approx0.908184\). The fitted model is \(\widehat{\operatorname{logit}(p)}=-1.362276+0.908184x\). To predict, first calculate the linear score, then exponentiate through the inverse logit. At \(x=2\), the score is \(-1.362276+0.908184(2)=0.454092\), and \(\hat p(2)=1/(1+e^{-0.454092})\approx0.611612\).

The four fitted values are approximately 0.203871, 0.388388, 0.611612 and 0.796129 for x from 0 to 3. They need not equal 0.25, 0.25, 0.75 and 0.75, the corresponding sample proportions. Substituting x=4 gives about 0.906401, but x=4 is outside our observed range and that prediction is extrapolation. The model may still return a mathematically valid probability, yet its empirical credibility decreases without evidence beyond x=3.

For a transparent check of prediction and units, consider x=1: \(\eta=-0.454092\) and \(\hat p\approx0.388388\). For x=3, \(\eta=1.362276\) and \(\hat p\approx0.796129\). Increasing preparation by two workshops changes the predicted probability by about 0.407741 for these two values, but that is not a universal two-workshop effect on probability. The corresponding two-workshop odds multiplier is constant under the model, which is precisely why odds ratios need separate interpretation.

10. Interpret an odds ratio without calling it a risk ratio

Equation / derivation · Conditional OR and absolute probability
\[\boxed{OR_{x+1:x}=e^{\beta_1}}\qquad p_1=\frac{OR\cdot p_0}{1-p_0+OR\cdot p_0}\]

In the fitted logit, raising x by one changes the linear score by \(\hat\beta_1=0.908184\). The odds at x+1 divided by the odds at x are \(e^{\hat\beta_1}=e^{0.908184}\approx2.479816\). This is an Odds Ratio (OR): for one additional workshop, the fitted odds of program completion are about 2.48 times as large, conditional on the model. In a multivariable model, the same construction holds while the other included predictors are held fixed.

An odds ratio of 2.48 does not mean the probability is multiplied by 2.48, increased by 148 percentage points, or increased by 148%. For example, at x=0 the fitted probability is about 0.2039 and odds are about 0.2561. At x=1 odds become about 0.6350, or 2.48 times as large, yet probability becomes about 0.3884, a risk ratio of about 1.905. At x=2 and x=3 the probability ratio is only about 1.302. The odds multiplier stays constant in this model, while probability ratios and probability differences vary with the starting value.

To translate any odds ratio back into a probability, specify a baseline probability \(p_0\). If the OR is \(R\), multiply baseline odds by R and transform back: \(p_1=Rp_0/[1-p_0+Rp_0]\). Taking \(p_0=0.20\) and R=2.48 gives 0.496/(0.80+0.496)=0.3827, not 0.496. Without a baseline probability, no unique absolute probability is implied by an OR. This is a fundamental reporting safeguard in health, education and economics.

11. Information matrix, standard errors and covariance

Equation / derivation · Model-based covariance
\[\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\hat\beta)\approx(X^T\hat WX)^{-1}\]

A coefficient estimate by itself tells us nothing about sampling variability. Under correct mean specification, independent observations, adequate sample size, identifiable parameters and other regularity conditions, the inverse of the information matrix at the MLE approximates the coefficient covariance matrix. The observed information uses fitted weights \(\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)\); for our simple model the matrix inverse is approximately \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.975041&-0.441616\\-0.441616&0.294410\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). The diagonal entries are variances, not standard errors.

Take square roots: \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_0)\approx\sqrt{0.975041}=0.987441\) and \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\approx\sqrt{0.294410}=0.542596\). The negative off-diagonal entry says the two estimates co-vary under the fitted information approximation. This dependence matters for uncertainty in a predicted linear score, because \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_0+x\hat\beta_1)\) includes \(2x\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1)\). It would be incorrect to add just the two separate standard errors.

Sixteen observations is a pedagogical sample, not a strong justification for large-sample normal approximations. Standard errors here describe what the asymptotic theory would report. They are not empirical proof of reliable coverage, especially with sparse cells, covariate imbalance or separation. In a real study, sample design, dependence, missing data and model specification require dedicated inspection before presenting an interval as trustworthy.

12. Wald tests and intervals, one arithmetic step at a time

Equation / derivation · Wald test and interval
\[\begin{aligned}z&=\frac{0.908184}{0.542596}\approx1.673776\\CI_{95\%}(OR)&=\exp\!\bigl(0.908184\pm1.96(0.542596)\bigr)\\&\approx[0.8562,\;7.1827]\end{aligned}\]

To test \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) against a two-sided alternative, the Wald statistic is \(z=(\hat\beta_1-0)/\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=0.908184/0.542596\approx1.673776\). Under regular large-sample conditions the reference distribution is approximately standard normal. The two-sided p-value is about 0.094175. With a preselected \(\alpha=0.05\), this does not reject the null. It does not show that the true slope is zero, and it does not erase the estimated positive association.

A nominal 95% Wald interval for the log-odds slope is \(0.908184\pm1.96(0.542596)\), approximately [−0.155304, 1.971673]. Exponentiating each endpoint, which is allowed because the exponential function is strictly increasing, yields an approximate 95% confidence interval for the OR: [0.856155, 7.182680]. The interval includes 1, the OR value corresponding to zero log-odds slope. This agrees with the two-sided 5% Wald non-rejection. Its width communicates that the small illustrative dataset supports many association magnitudes.

The meaning of 95% coverage is about a hypothetical repeated-sampling procedure: across repeated studies meeting the assumptions, approximately 95% of similarly computed intervals cover the fixed population parameter. It is not a 95% probability that this already computed interval contains the fixed parameter, under the classical frequentist interpretation. Exponentiation preserves the set of plausible parameter values; it does not transform the interval into an absolute-risk interval.

13. Likelihood ratio tests and why answers can differ

Equation / derivation · Likelihood-ratio test
\[D=2(\ell_F-\ell_R)=2[-9.3899461+11.0903549]\approx3.4008175\]

The Likelihood Ratio (LR) test compares two nested models: a reduced model that fixes the slope at zero and a full model that estimates it. Under the reduced model, the intercept-only fitted completion probability is 8/16=0.5 and its maximized log-likelihood is \(\ell_R=-11.0903549\). Under the full model, the maximized log-likelihood is \(\ell_F=-9.3899461\). The full model cannot fit worse in maximum log-likelihood because it contains the reduced model as a special case.

The LR statistic is \(D=2(\ell_F-\ell_R)=2[-9.3899461-(-11.0903549)]\approx3.4008175\). The full model adds one free parameter, so under regular asymptotic conditions this is compared with a \(\chi^2\) distribution with one degree of freedom. Its p-value is approximately 0.065164. At 5% it also fails to reject, but it is not identical to the Wald p-value of about 0.094175.

Why the difference? Wald measures the fitted coefficient in units of its estimated local standard error; LR measures improvement in maximized log-likelihood. Their large-sample agreement is asymptotic, not an identity for finite samples. With a small sample, different approximations can produce noticeably different numbers. Neither approach automatically solves separation or confounding. Comparing their methods is educational; choosing whichever gives significance after seeing the results would be post hoc selection.

14. Predicted probability versus uncertainty about a prediction

Equation / derivation · Variance of the fitted linear score
\[\operatorname{Var}(\hat\eta(x))=V_{00}+2xV_{01}+x^2V_{11}\]

A fitted probability is a point estimate, not a certainty about a student's eventual outcome. Even if \(p(x)\) were known exactly, a Bernoulli student can finish or not finish. There are two separate uncertainties: uncertainty about the model's estimated parameters and the irreducible variation in individual binary outcomes. A 0.61 estimated completion probability does not guarantee that 61% of the next four students at that workshop count will complete.

For the fitted score at x=2, use the covariance matrix: \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\eta(2))\approx0.975041+4(0.294410)+4(-0.441616)\approx0.386218\). Thus \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat\eta(2))\approx0.621464\). A pointwise approximate 95% interval on the score scale is \(0.454092\pm1.96(0.621464)\), or roughly [−0.7640, 1.6722]. Apply inverse logit to the endpoints to obtain an approximate probability interval of about [0.318, 0.842]. This interval concerns the fitted conditional mean under the model, not an interval of possible zero/one outcomes for one individual.

A probability interval requires accounting for the covariance of intercept and slope, not just multiplying the slope's confidence interval by x. A confidence interval for \(p(x)\) can also be obtained through profile likelihood or other methods. With limited data, normal-score approximations may have poor coverage. The principle is to state the inferential target and the approximation before declaring a precise probability.

15. A geometric look at the likelihood and identifiability

For a fixed predictor matrix, the logistic log-likelihood is concave in its coefficient vector. Its Hessian is \(-X^TWX\), negative semidefinite because \(w_i=p_i(1-p_i)\geq0\). When design columns are independent and observations carry enough information, the fitted curvature has positive information in relevant directions. That supports stable local estimation. When columns are linearly dependent, the matrix cannot be inverted uniquely and separate coefficients may be unidentifiable.

No exact formula analogous to the ordinary least-squares closed form generally solves the logistic score equations. This explains why iteration was not an optional programming trick: probabilities change with the coefficients, which means the information weights change at every step. The first update computed earlier is an illustration of that dependence. Convergence checks should consider changes in coefficients, score, likelihood and numerical warning messages, not merely the number of iterations performed.

The likelihood is a property of the observed data under a specified sampling model. A narrow likelihood peak may signal statistical information conditional on that model; it does not verify whether students were selected fairly, whether unmeasured causes exist or whether outcomes were entered correctly. Model validity and coefficient identifiability are distinct questions.

16. Complete and quasi-complete separation

Equation / derivation · Separation creates divergent coefficients
\[\beta_0=-a,\quad\beta_1=2a,\quad a\longrightarrow+\infty\]

Imagine a different dataset where all students with x=0 fail and all students with x=1 complete. A logistic slope can become arbitrarily positive while pushing probabilities toward zero and one. The maximized likelihood is approached as some coefficients diverge, so a finite maximum likelihood estimate need not exist. Numerical software might return extremely large coefficients, huge standard errors or a convergence warning. Those values are not evidence of an enormous precisely measured effect.

Quasi-complete separation occurs when a linear combination of predictors separates observations apart from boundary cases. It can be harder to detect in multiple regression, especially with many sparse categorical combinations. A common but invalid response is to delete inconvenient observations until the software stops warning. Better responses include checking data coding and timing, reducing unnecessary predictors based on scientific design, increasing relevant data when possible, or using a suitably justified penalized or bias-reduced estimator. Those methods change the estimation procedure and must be disclosed.

Our 16-student example is not completely separated: among students with x=0, completion and noncompletion both occur, and the same is true at x=3. A finite two-parameter MLE is available. Nevertheless, small cell counts still make asymptotic inference tentative. Identifying a computational warning is part of statistical reasoning, not an optional final software detail.

17. Multivariable logistic regression and adjustment

With workshop count \(x_1\) and a previously recorded placement score \(x_2\), a two-predictor model would be \(\operatorname{logit}[p(x_1,x_2)]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2\). At a fixed placement score, one additional workshop multiplies the modeled odds by \(e^{\beta_1}\). The phrase holding other included variables fixed is essential. In the original one-predictor model, \(e^{\beta_1}\) is an unadjusted odds ratio with respect to this richer model; it cannot be retroactively described as adjusted.

Even after adjustment, the coefficient is not necessarily causal. Students who attend more workshops may differ in motivation, prior achievement or responsibilities not captured in the observed predictors. Unmeasured confounding remains possible. In addition, odds ratios are noncollapsible: a conditional OR can differ from a marginal OR even without classical confounding. Therefore a change in OR after adding a predictor does not, by itself, measure the amount of bias that was removed.

The interpretation of the intercept depends on the reference values of all predictors. If x=0 and placement score=0 are implausible, the intercept may be mathematically necessary but not substantively interesting. Centering a numerical predictor can create a more meaningful reference without changing fitted probabilities. Re-scaling workshops from single sessions to pairs would double its coefficient, while its OR would apply to two sessions rather than one.

18. Model diagnostics must match the intended use

A good classifier and a good probability model are not the same thing. Discrimination measures how well higher predicted risk ranks actual events above nonevents. Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed frequencies in comparable cases. An accuracy computed at a threshold is sensitive to the event prevalence and threshold choice. A model predicting every student will complete might get high accuracy in a population with very high completion prevalence while being practically useless for finding those who need support.

A coherent evaluation first identifies the purpose: understanding associations, estimating conditional probabilities, ranking cases, or making a decision. For probability estimation, investigate calibration plots, out-of-sample log loss or Brier score, and stability across relevant subgroups when adequate data exist. For decision rules, specify the costs of false positives and false negatives before picking a threshold. The log-likelihood used in model fitting is not an automatic proof of generalization.

With sixteen synthetic cases we cannot claim performance on new real students. A random train/test split would produce very small and unstable subsets; cross-validation may illustrate a procedure, but does not manufacture independent information. This guide focuses on estimation and statistical inference, with model deployment and classification metrics recognized as neighboring topics rather than used to substitute for uncertainty analysis.

19. Sensitivity to unit changes, thresholds and baseline risk

Equation / derivation · Change of predictor units
\[\operatorname{OR}_{\Delta x=2}=e^{2\beta_1}=\bigl(e^{\beta_1}\bigr)^2\]

The slope \(0.908184\) is attached to one workshop. If a new predictor is defined as \(z=x/2\), one unit of z corresponds to two original workshops. Substituting x=2z yields \(\eta=-1.362276+1.816368z\). The two-workshop OR is \(e^{1.816368}\approx6.1495\), the square of the one-workshop OR. Fitted probabilities for the same students are unchanged. Reporting coefficients without predictor units can make equivalent models sound contradictory.

A classification threshold such as 0.5 is not part of the likelihood. At x=2 the fitted probability is about 0.6116, which would predict completion under a 0.5 threshold; under a 0.7 threshold it would not. Neither decision changes the estimated coefficients, standard errors or scientific evidence. Thresholds encode a downstream decision rule; changing them to increase apparent accuracy on the same sample is not a valid inferential strategy.

Baseline risk also determines the absolute interpretation of an OR. The same OR=2.48 maps 0.10 to approximately 0.216, 0.50 to approximately 0.713, and 0.80 to approximately 0.908. The odds ratio itself is constant in a simple logit with fixed slope, but its probability-scale meaning is contextual. Presenting an OR next to a representative baseline or several predicted probabilities is often more helpful to readers than reporting an exponentiated coefficient alone.

20. Reporting and limits of the evidence

A careful summary of the illustrative dataset would say: a one-workshop increase was associated with fitted odds about 2.48 times as high (approximate Wald 95% CI 0.86–7.18); the slope Wald p-value was about 0.094 and the one-degree-of-freedom LR p-value about 0.065. Those results do not clear a 5% threshold under either test. The fitted probability was approximately 0.204 for zero workshops and 0.796 for three workshops. Data are synthetic, sample size is 16, and assumptions for large-sample inference are weakly supported.

The appropriate conclusion is not that workshops do nothing, that workshops guarantee completion, or that the fitted OR is a causal effect. Statistical uncertainty is wide, participation was not randomized, and the exercise is not a study of a real population. Future data collection would need a clearly identified target population, predeclared outcome and covariates, appropriate sampling and measurement, an analysis plan and sufficient overlap in the predictors.

When discussing statistical results, distinguish the estimate, inferential interval, test decision, modelling assumptions, external validity and causal question. These are six different objects. The most sophisticated formula cannot replace a trustworthy study design. We now test these distinctions with independent exercises whose answers include both operations and interpretations.

20A. A second check of the maximum likelihood solution

An optimization result should be checked against the equations it claims to solve. At the MLE, fitted probabilities at x=0,1,2,3 are 0.20387058, 0.38838827, 0.61161173 and 0.79612942. Each predictor level has four students. The sum of all predicted completions is \(4(0.20387058+0.38838827+0.61161173+0.79612942)=4(2)=8\), matching the observed eight. This shows that the intercept score \(\sum(y_i-\hat p_i)\) is approximately zero. It is not a coincidence: it is the intercept score equation for this model with an unrestricted intercept.

For the slope score, weight each level by x. Predicted workshop-weighted completions are \(4[0(0.20387058)+1(0.38838827)+2(0.61161173)+3(0.79612942)]\). The bracket is approximately 0.38838827+1.22322346+2.38838826=4.000000, so the weighted total is 16, matching the observed \(\sum x_iy_i=16\). Thus the slope score is also approximately zero. These checks verify the numerical optimum equations, not that each fitted probability is equal to the observed proportion. Notice how the outcome totals, rather than individual classifications, enter the optimization first-order conditions.

20B. Why the logistic probability change depends on the baseline

Differentiate \(p(x)=1/[1+e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1x)}]\) with respect to x. By the chain rule, \(dp/dx=\beta_1p(x)[1-p(x)]\). The slope on the probability scale therefore depends on x even though the coefficient \(\beta_1\) on the log-odds scale is constant. At the fitted x=0 probability of 0.203871, the local probability slope is approximately \(0.908184(0.203871)(0.796129)=0.1474\) per workshop. At x=2, where p=0.611612, it is approximately \(0.908184(0.611612)(0.388388)=0.2158\). The local slope is largest where p is near one-half, not at every value of x.

The derivative is a local rate for a continuous change in x. Workshops, however, are counted in whole sessions. To describe a real one-session comparison, calculate the finite difference \(p(x+1)-p(x)\), not the derivative multiplied by one and declared exact. From x=0 to x=1, the fitted increase is about 0.184518. From x=2 to x=3, it is about 0.184518 again in this symmetric illustrative fit; from x=1 to x=2, the increase is about 0.223223. This pattern confirms the S-shape and reinforces why a single percentage-point coefficient would misrepresent logistic regression.

20C. Grouped binomial data and likelihood bookkeeping

Our rows can also be described as four groups of four students, with observed successes \(s=(1,1,3,3)\) at workshop counts 0 through 3. Conditional on a group's common predicted probability \(p_g\), its binomial count likelihood is \({4\choose s_g}p_g^{s_g}(1-p_g)^{4-s_g}\). The combinatorial coefficient counts possible orders of successes among four students. If we instead use the original sixteen individually labeled Bernoulli outcomes, each observed order has likelihood \(\prod_i p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}\) without this coefficient.

The combinatorial coefficients do not depend on regression parameters. Consequently grouped binomial counts and individual Bernoulli rows produce the same maximizing coefficients when the grouping is valid, even though their numerical likelihood values include different constants. For a group's x=0 data, s=1 and n=4, the count probability is \({4\choose1}p_0(1-p_0)^3=4p_0(1-p_0)^3\), whereas our specific ordered labels contribute \(p_0(1-p_0)^3\). Mixing grouped likelihood with individual likelihood numbers in an LR statistic without consistent bookkeeping would create an invalid comparison. The lesson is to specify what was observed before writing a likelihood.

20D. A fair comparison with a linear probability model

A Linear Probability Model (LPM) applies ordinary least squares directly to zero/one outcomes: \(E(Y\mid x)\approx a+bx\). It can be useful descriptively, but its linear prediction is not constrained to [0,1], and Bernoulli heteroscedasticity still needs appropriate uncertainty handling. In our balanced four-level dataset, x has mean 1.5 and y has mean 0.5. The sample cross-product sum is \(\sum(x_i-1.5)(y_i-0.5)=\sum x_iy_i-16(1.5)(0.5)=16-12=4\). The x deviation sum of squares is \(56-16(1.5)^2=56-36=20\). The LPM slope is therefore 4/20=0.20 and its intercept is 0.5−0.20(1.5)=0.20.

This straight line gives probabilities 0.20, 0.40, 0.60 and 0.80 at x=0,1,2,3, close to the logistic estimates in our limited observed range. However, extrapolating to x=5 produces 1.20, an impossible probability. Logistic regression at x=5 gives a value below one. This comparison does not prove that logistic regression is always superior at every prediction task; it demonstrates the coherent probability range that the logit guarantees. The two models also answer different coefficient questions: an LPM slope is an additive probability change, while a logistic slope is an additive log-odds change.

20E. Confidence intervals for an Odds Ratio are not symmetric

A common presentation error is to report \(\widehat{OR}\pm1.96\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\). This combines an OR with an error standard expressed in log-odds units and is mathematically invalid. The correct Wald procedure constructs a symmetric interval on the coefficient's original scale, then transforms both bounds by the exponential function. Because exponentiation is nonlinear, the resulting OR interval is generally asymmetric around the point OR.

Our coefficient interval is approximately [−0.1553,1.9717]. Its OR endpoints are about [0.8562,7.1827]. The lower distance from the point estimate 2.4798 is about 1.6236, whereas the upper distance is about 4.7029. There is no contradiction: additive uncertainty on the log scale becomes multiplicative uncertainty on the odds scale. The symmetry that remains is multiplicative: \(\widehat{OR}/OR_{lower}\) and \(OR_{upper}/\widehat{OR}\) are both approximately \(e^{1.06349}\). An effective report specifies which scale was used for estimation and which for communication.

20F. What changes with a binary predictor?

A model can use a binary predictor such as whether a student had access to a preparatory advising session. Suppose z=0 means no session and z=1 means access, with \(\operatorname{logit}(p)=\beta_0+\beta_1z\). Then \(\beta_0\) is the log-odds for z=0 and \(\beta_0+\beta_1\) is the log-odds for z=1. Their log-odds difference is \(\beta_1\), and the OR comparing z=1 with z=0 is \(e^{\beta_1}\). Changing the coding so that 1 means no access reverses the coefficient sign and replaces the OR by its reciprocal.

For example, if an illustrative binary-exposure model gives OR=3, switching reference group produces OR=1/3, not zero or −3. Both descriptions carry the same association with opposite comparison directions. Confidence interval endpoints must likewise be reciprocated with their order reversed: if an OR interval is [1.2,7.5], the interval for the reverse comparison is [1/7.5,1/1.2]≈[0.1333,0.8333]. This reinforces the need to report variable coding and reference categories, not merely list coefficients. For more than two categories, separate indicator coefficients refer to a clearly declared baseline category.

20G. Why rare events and small samples call for caution

If only two of 300 students complete a very rare program milestone, a conventional logistic model may have numerical difficulties even though the total sample size sounds large. Information about the event depends on the number and distribution of successes, not just on total n. A predictor category with no successes may induce separation or extremely weak curvature. Multiple predictors can consume information faster than readers intuitively expect. The appropriate response is not a universal fixed rule about required observations, but a design-aware evaluation of event counts, sparsity, overlap, and estimator stability.

Confidence intervals and tests derived from a local quadratic approximation to log-likelihood can behave badly when the likelihood is strongly asymmetric. The Wald statistic divides an estimate by a local error standard, so it can become unstable near boundaries or separation. Profile-likelihood or bias-reduced analyses may offer more useful perspectives when properly justified, but they have their own assumptions and need transparent documentation. Our example with eight completions does not support a grand generalization about numerical performance; it is deliberately chosen to make the algebra inspectable.

20H. Additional independent transfer questions and worked solutions

Exercise 13 — Center a predictor. Let \(c=x-1.5\). Re-express our fitted model and verify a prediction. Solution. Since x=c+1.5, substitute into \(-1.362276+0.908184x\): \(\eta=(-1.362276+1.362276)+0.908184c\approx0+0.908184c\). The intercept near zero corresponds to x=1.5 and probability approximately 0.5. At x=2, c=0.5, so the score is about 0.454092 and probability 0.611612, precisely as before. Centering changes the intercept's reference; it does not change the slope, OR or fitted probabilities. The transformed intercept's uncertainty is not simply the uncertainty of the former intercept, because covariance with the slope contributes.

Exercise 14 — Compare two independent uncertainty questions. A fitted model gives \(\hat p(2)=0.6116\) and a pointwise approximate 95% confidence interval [0.318,0.842]. Can we say that the next student's outcome lies in that interval with 95% confidence? Solution. No: the individual Bernoulli outcome must equal either zero or one, neither of which belongs to that numerical interval. The interval is about the unknown conditional probability at x=2 under the assumed model and repeated-sampling approximation. The next observed student may complete with estimated probability 0.6116 or not complete with estimated probability 0.3884. Parameter uncertainty and event randomness are separate. To check understanding, explain why narrowing the coefficient interval through more observations does not eliminate the randomness of an individual zero/one result.

20I. Numerical stability: a formula can be right but a computation wrong

There are two algebraically equivalent formulas for inverse logit: \(e^\eta/(1+e^\eta)\) and \(1/(1+e^{-\eta})\). Both are correct for ordinary scores, but a computer's floating-point arithmetic has limited range. If \(\eta\) is very large and positive, evaluating \(e^\eta\) directly may overflow; if it is very negative, evaluating \(e^{-\eta}\) directly may overflow. A reliable numerical implementation uses the form appropriate to the sign of the score or a stable expit function. The goal is not to change statistical assumptions, but to avoid manufacturing an arithmetic failure while representing a valid mathematical probability.

Likewise, a naive log-likelihood may calculate \(\ln(1+e^\eta)\) by creating an enormous exponential. A stable alternative evaluates the softplus term using numerical transformations. In a study guide, writing the symbolic function is useful for deriving the Score; in a reproducible implementation, checking the stability of the actual calculation is equally necessary. Never interpret an overflow warning as scientific evidence about student behavior. It is a computing warning that requires a computational correction and, if estimates diverge, a separate investigation of statistical separation.

20J. Independence and selection: what regression cannot repair

Imagine that students sign up for workshops voluntarily and that those with more free time also find it easier to complete the support program. The workshop count is then associated with an omitted determinant of completion. The regression may estimate a positive conditional relationship perfectly accurately for the observed population, yet the coefficient does not identify what would happen if we required an otherwise comparable student to attend another session. Statistical precision, causal identification and usefulness for decisions remain separate.

A stronger causal investigation might define a feasible intervention, explain eligibility, measure important pre-exposure variables and compare groups with a design that supports exchangeability. Random assignment, when ethical and feasible, can help balance measured and unmeasured causes in expectation; observational designs require explicit assumptions and sensitivity analysis. Even a randomized intervention must consider noncompliance, missing outcomes, interference among students and whether the intervention matches what the predictor represents. Logistic regression is a flexible statistical analysis component, not a substitute for the design that makes a causal question answerable.

21. Worked exercises: probability and odds as separate quantities

Exercise 1 — Forward conversion. A hypothetical applicant has a completion probability of 0.70. Find the odds and log-odds, stating the domains and interpretation. Solution. First calculate the failure probability \(1-0.70=0.30\). Odds are \(0.70/0.30=7/3\approx2.3333\), or approximately 2.33 to 1 in favor of completion. The natural logarithm is \(\ln(7/3)\approx0.8473\). Probability 0.70 is unitless and lies between zero and one; odds 2.3333 are a positive ratio; log-odds 0.8473 may take any real value. None of these quantities means 2.3333 completed students. A probability is a model-based expectation over comparable opportunities, not a deterministic label for a single applicant. Verification: odds 2.3333 give \(2.3333/(1+2.3333)\approx0.70\).

Exercise 2 — Reverse conversion. A fitted logistic score for a student is \(\eta=-0.7\). Recover probability through two equivalent operations. Solution. Because the score equals log-odds, exponentiate: odds are \(e^{-0.7}\approx0.496585\). Convert to probability: \(0.496585/(1+0.496585)\approx0.331812\). Alternatively use the inverse logit directly: \(1/(1+e^{0.7})\approx0.331812\). The agreement checks the algebra and the implementation. A negative score predicts a probability below 0.5, but it is not a negative probability. If we changed the threshold to 0.3, this same probability would be classified as completion; the estimate itself has not changed. Explain to a colleague why a decision threshold is not part of the fitted mathematical curve.

Exercise 3 — Compare risk and odds. Two groups have completion probabilities 0.20 and 0.40. Compute their probability difference, risk ratio, and odds ratio. Solution. The probability difference is 0.40−0.20=0.20, or twenty percentage points. The risk ratio is 0.40/0.20=2. The first group's odds are 0.20/0.80=0.25; the second group's odds are 0.40/0.60=2/3. Their OR is \((2/3)/0.25=8/3\approx2.6667\). The risk ratio and OR differ because their denominators differ. On a multiplicative scale, 2 and 2.6667 are not interchangeable. For an event that is very uncommon, OR and risk ratio can be closer, but our probabilities are too large to treat them as equivalent without qualification. Transfer: repeat with 0.02 and 0.04; the risk ratio remains 2, while the OR is about 2.0417.

22. Worked exercises: reconstruct the estimation mechanics

Exercise 4 — Recover the data summaries. Using all sixteen student rows, compute \(n\), \(\sum y_i\), \(\sum x_i\), \(\sum x_i^2\), and \(\sum x_iy_i\). Solution. Each x=0,1,2,3 occurs four times; therefore n=4+4+4+4=16. Completion counts by group are 1,1,3,3, so \(\sum y=8\). Total workshop count is \(4(0+1+2+3)=24\). Squared workshop total is \(4(0^2+1^2+2^2+3^2)=4(14)=56\). The workshop sum among successes is \(0(1)+1(1)+2(3)+3(3)=16\). These five summaries are sufficient to reproduce the score and information matrix at zero coefficients. They do not suffice to reconstruct all aspects of more complicated logistic models with new predictors; one must retain the student-level data and define the design matrix explicitly.

Exercise 5 — Derive one score component. Starting at \(\beta=(0,0)\), evaluate the intercept and slope scores, explaining each term. Solution. At zero coefficients, \(p_i=1/2\) for all sixteen observations. The intercept score is \(U_0=\sum_i(y_i-0.5)=8-8=0\). The slope score is \(U_1=\sum_i x_i(y_i-0.5)=\sum x_iy_i-0.5\sum x_i=16-12=4\). The score is thus \((0,4)^T\). It is a direction of increasing log-likelihood, not a regression coefficient or an Odds Ratio. To check its meaning, increase the slope slightly while holding intercept fixed and inspect whether log-likelihood initially rises. A nonzero score implies the initial pair fails the first-order optimality equations. A zero intercept score alone does not make the entire coefficient pair optimal.

Exercise 6 — Reproduce Newton's first update. With \(I_0=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&6\\6&14\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(U_0=(0,4)^T\), show the inverse and new parameters. Solution. The determinant is \(4(14)-6(6)=20\). The inverse of a symmetric 2×2 matrix is \(\frac1{20}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}14&-6\\-6&4\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Multiplying the inverse by the score gives \(\frac1{20}(-24,16)^T=(-1.2,0.8)^T\). Add this increment to the initial zero vector: the new coefficients are (−1.2,0.8). For x=2, \(\eta=-1.2+0.8(2)=0.4\), giving \(p=1/(1+e^{-0.4})\approx0.598688\). The starting prediction was 0.5. Checking the full log-likelihood confirms improvement to −9.41038. The next step requires recomputing all four distinct information weights; reusing 0.25 would no longer be correct.

23. Worked exercises: inference and parameter scales

Exercise 7 — Wald confidence interval. Use the fitted slope 0.908184 and standard error 0.542596 to compute a two-sided 95% Wald interval for the slope and OR. Solution. First calculate the normal critical value 1.96, which is an asymptotic reference, not a small-sample t critical value. The half-width is \(1.96(0.542596)=1.063488\). Therefore the slope interval is \([0.908184-1.063488,\;0.908184+1.063488]\approx[-0.155304,1.971672]\). The OR interval follows by exponentiating both endpoints: approximately [0.8562, 7.1827]. The point OR is \(e^{0.908184}\approx2.4798\). It would be incorrect to exponentiate the standard error directly and then add or subtract that number from the OR. The endpoints include OR=1, corresponding to a zero slope. The method's nominal coverage depends on regularity and sufficiently large samples, which should not be assumed merely from software output.

Exercise 8 — Reproduce the LR test. The reduced log-likelihood is −11.0903549 and the full log-likelihood is −9.3899461. Compute the LR statistic and interpret the p-value of approximately 0.06516. Solution. Take the gain in log-likelihood: \(-9.3899461+11.0903549=1.7004088\). Multiply by two to get \(D\approx3.4008176\). The full model adds one slope, so reference degrees of freedom equal 1 under the stated regular conditions. The upper-tail chi-square p-value is about 0.06516. At alpha 0.05, do not reject. At a prespecified alpha 0.10, the same statistic would exceed the threshold; changing alpha after observing 0.06516 to obtain rejection would be inappropriate. The Wald p-value 0.09417 is different because the two methods use distinct approximations. Check: the LR statistic is nonnegative because the larger nested model cannot have a worse optimized log-likelihood.

Exercise 9 — Predict an individual probability. Use \(\hat\beta_0=-1.362276\), \(\hat\beta_1=0.908184\) for a student with three workshops. Solution. The linear score is \(-1.362276+3(0.908184)=1.362276\). Exponentiating gives odds \(e^{1.362276}\approx3.905\). Dividing by one plus odds gives \(p\approx3.905/(4.905)\approx0.796129\). Alternatively use \(1/(1+e^{-1.362276})\) and obtain the same result. This is an estimated conditional probability, not a guarantee of completion or a causal benefit of attending three workshops. Compare x=0: predicted probability is 0.203871. The difference is about 0.592259, yet it applies specifically to those two predictor values; it is not a constant per-workshop effect.

24. Worked exercises: transport, separation and model criticism

Exercise 10 — Convert an OR to an absolute risk. Suppose a separate model reports OR=2.5 for exposure, with baseline event probability 0.10. Calculate the exposed probability. Solution. Baseline odds are \(0.10/(1-0.10)=1/9\). Multiply by the OR: exposed odds are \(2.5/9\approx0.277778\). Convert to probability: \(0.277778/(1.277778)=0.217391\). Using the equivalent formula, \(2.5(0.10)/[0.90+2.5(0.10)]=0.25/1.15=0.217391\). The event probability rises by about 0.1174, not by 150 percentage points. If the baseline were 0.50 instead, the exposed probability would be 2.5/3.5≈0.7143. Thus the same OR can imply different absolute changes. In applied reporting, always state the assumed baseline and whether the OR is conditional or marginal.

Exercise 11 — Diagnose complete separation. A new four-row dataset has (x,y)=(0,0),(0,0),(1,1),(1,1). What happens to an unpenalized logistic MLE? Solution. The predictor perfectly separates failure at x=0 from success at x=1. Let \(\beta_0=-a\) and \(\beta_1=2a\), with positive a growing. For x=0 the score is −a, so predicted probability tends toward zero; for x=1 the score is +a, so predicted probability tends toward one. As a grows, each observed Bernoulli factor tends toward one, and total log-likelihood tends toward zero, its supremum, without a finite optimum at any finite a. Finite maximum likelihood estimates of the coefficients therefore do not exist in this direction. A reported giant coefficient should trigger a diagnostic, not an OR interpretation. Consider data design, coding, or explicitly justified penalization rather than silently keeping a misleading Wald interval.

Exercise 12 — Transfer to a more realistic study. Imagine 300 students across ten classrooms, with workshop attendance, an entry assessment and program completion. Design an analysis without pretending to know the results. Solution. Define the outcome and observation unit first, including who is eligible and when completion is measured. Record whether workshops precede the outcome; specify attendance units and how entry assessment is measured. Decide whether the estimand is an adjusted association or a causal effect, because the latter requires substantially stronger identification assumptions. A conditional logistic model might include attendance and entry score, with theory-led nonlinearities if appropriate. Since students share classrooms, inspect within-classroom dependence and consider cluster-appropriate uncertainty or a justified hierarchical structure rather than uncritically using independent Bernoulli standard errors. Investigate missing values, overlap, class imbalance, separation and calibration. Predeclare the primary coefficient and comparison, examine predicted probabilities at meaningful baselines, report OR with an interval and avoid translating it into absolute risk without a baseline. Reserve truly new data for external validation. This exercise cannot yield an estimated numerical OR because the 300 records were not supplied; inventing one would replace analysis with fabrication.

25. Independent synthesis and final understanding test

Before leaving the guide, reconstruct the inferential chain in your own words: an individual Bernoulli outcome has conditional mean p and variance p(1−p); the logit maps p to a linear score; observed outcomes determine the likelihood; a numerical algorithm solves score equations; the inverse information approximates coefficient uncertainty; exponentiating a slope gives a conditional odds multiplier. Each arrow depends on specific definitions and assumptions. If you cannot explain why a step is valid, return to the corresponding section rather than memorizing the final coefficient.

Now answer three independent questions without looking up the solved answers. First, if a probability increases from 0.1 to 0.2, what changes more: risk ratio or OR, and why? Second, if the coefficient confidence interval includes zero, must the point estimate also be zero? Third, if a computer reports a slope of 45 with a separation warning, should we report \(e^{45}\) as a well-estimated association? A defensible solution must show the relevant formulas, articulate the reference distribution or mathematical pathology, and distinguish an observed association from a causal claim.

The central transfer is methodological: a logistic regression output is not a story about a program until the study population, measurement, modelling choice, uncertainty and target interpretation are understood. Especially in a small sample, plausible fitted probabilities and impressive-looking odds ratios can coexist with wide uncertainty. The correct endpoint is the ability to reproduce a coefficient-related calculation, challenge its assumptions, and tell a reader precisely what was and was not established.
