01What do we lose when we report only mean and variance?

Suppose two delivery processes both average four days and have the same variance. They can still differ in how frequently a very long delay occurs, how observations balance on the two sides of the mean, or whether times cluster around several locations. Mean and variance compress a distribution; they do not reconstruct it. A moment is an average of a specified power of values or deviations. Changing the reference and the power asks a different question about the same distribution.

This guide builds from signed distances to absolute distances, squared distances, cubed distances and fourth powers. We first work with complete observed records, then distinguish those empirical averages from properties of a probability model. Finally we remove units to compare shape. Higher powers can reveal features that low-order summaries miss, but they also amplify extreme values and may fail to have a finite expectation.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish raw, central, absolute and standardized moments and state their units and existence conditions.
  • Reproduce the first four empirical moments from all observations, explaining signed cancellation and the variance identity.
  • Calculate Skewness, Kurtosis and Excess Kurtosis using declared sample conventions.
  • Compare distributions with equal mean and variance, diagnose misleading shape claims, and explain sensitivity to extreme values.

Preparation: algebra, powers, averages and the idea of an expected value; Calculus I supports the continuous example. Guides 03 and 11 provide useful background. We restate the notation and expectation rules needed here. No programming or inference course is assumed. Read Sections 2–9 before interpreting the standardized summaries in Sections 10–15; the solved exercises then test both arithmetic and judgment.

03Signed deviations balance; they do not measure overall size

Choose a reference c and form xi−c. A negative difference means below the reference; a positive difference means above it. The mean signed deviation is x̄−c, so it answers where the data center lies relative to c. At c=x̄ it is always zero. This cancellation holds whether the observations are tightly grouped or widely separated.

(1/n)Σ(xi−c) = (Σxi)/n − c = x̄−cAt c=4:   di = −3, −2, −2, −1, 0, 0, 1, 7 daysΣdi = −3−2−2−1+0+0+1+7 = 0;   average = 0/8 = 0

The negative deviations total −8 days and the positive deviations total +8 days. The balance is exact. It does not mean each parcel took four days; only two did. Around a target of three days, the average signed deviation would be 4−3=1 day. That is an average target overrun, not an absolute-distance measure of variability.

Pause and explain: Would multiplying all deviations by 100 make the average signed deviation useful as spread?

No. The new signed sum is 100×0=0. Scaling preserves cancellation. To summarize magnitude, change the operation before averaging: absolute values or even powers prevent opposite signs from canceling.

04Absolute deviations preserve distance without cancellation

The absolute value |u| is the nonnegative distance of u from zero. Thus |x−c| measures distance from c without direction. The Mean Absolute Deviation about the mean averages |xi−x̄|. We spell out the center and averaging operation because the abbreviation MAD is also used for Median Absolute Deviation, a different statistic.

|di| = 3, 2, 2, 1, 0, 0, 1, 7 daysMean absolute deviation about x̄ = (3+2+2+1+0+0+1+7)/8= 16/8 = 2 days

The signed deviations average zero while their distances average two days. Squaring those distances and averaging will give 8.5 days², not 2²=4 days²: averaging and applying a nonlinear operation generally do not commute. A useful bound is mean absolute deviation ≤ root mean square deviation. Here 2 ≤ √8.5≈2.9155 days. The bound follows from the fact that the average squared difference between the distances and their own mean is nonnegative.

For comparison, the median is 3.5 days. Absolute distances from that median sort as 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 1.5, 1.5, 2.5, 7.5; their median is 1.5 days. That is the unscaled Median Absolute Deviation. Its value differs from 2 because both the reference and the final aggregation differ. Neither measure is the Standard Deviation.

05From observations to population moments

A random variable X is a numerical outcome with a probability distribution. E[g(X)] is the probability-weighted average of a function g of that outcome. In a discrete model with values vj and probabilities pj, multiply each function value by its probability and sum. Probabilities must be nonnegative and sum to one. In a continuous model with density f(x), use an integral; f(x)≥0 and its total area is one. A density height is not a point probability.

Discrete:   E[g(X)] = Σj g(vj)pjContinuous:   E[g(X)] = ∫ g(x)f(x) dx over the support

For a positive integer r, the r-th Raw Moment is ar=E[Xr], measured about zero. If μ=E[X] exists, the r-th Central Moment is μr=E[(X−μ)r], measured about the mean. We use ar for raw moments to keep them visually distinct from μr; some sources instead use μ′r. The prime there labels a raw moment, not a derivative.

The Absolute Raw Moment is E[|X|r]; the Absolute Central Moment is E[|X−μ|r]. Even powers already remove the sign, so absolute and signed central moments coincide for even r. For odd r they generally differ. Finite moments require absolute integrability: E[|X|r]<∞ is a sufficient and standard condition for a finite raw moment. A finite mean and the corresponding finite absolute central moment support the central definitions. We will examine failure of existence explicitly.

06First and second moments: location, balance and variance

The first raw moment is a1=μ, the mean. The first central moment is zero because the mean is the balance point. The second raw moment E[X²] averages squared locations relative to zero. The second central moment averages squared distances from the mean and is the variance σ². These second moments answer different questions. A constant X=5 has E[X²]=25 but zero variance, since it never differs from its mean.

μ1 = E[X−μ] = E[X]−μ = μ−μ = 0μ2 = E[(X−μ)²]= E[X²−2μX+μ²]= a2−2μE[X]+μ² = a2−μ² = σ²

The expansion treats μ as a fixed number and uses linearity of expectation. It requires the second moment to be finite. Conversely a2=σ²+μ² separates spread around the mean from squared location. The Standard Deviation σ=√σ² uses the original units, while both a2 and μ2 use squared units. A variance cannot be negative; a negative result after subtraction signals numerical rounding or an error.

A different reference adds a location penalty

For a fixed target c, E[(X−c)²]=σ²+(μ−c)². Write X−c=(X−μ)+(μ−c), expand the square, and note that the cross term has expectation 2(μ−c)E[X−μ]=0. Squared error around a target includes both variability and distance of the mean from that target. It equals variance only when c=μ.

07Empirical moments and sample conventions

To describe n equally weighted observed values, replace expectation by their arithmetic average. The empirical distribution assigns probability 1/n to each observation, retaining repetitions. A value occurring twice receives total probability 2/n. For this empirical distribution the raw and central moments are exact descriptive quantities. They are not automatically the unknown moments of a larger population.

Empirical raw:   âr = (1/n)ΣxirEmpirical central:   mr = (1/n)Σ(xi−x̄)rm1=0;   m2=â2−x̄²

The circumflex on â labels an empirical raw moment. It does not assert unbiasedness. Here m2 uses divisor n. The conventional sample variance s² instead divides the same squared-deviation sum by n−1. Under independent, identically distributed sampling with finite variance, that correction makes s² unbiased for population variance: its expectation across repeated samples equals σ². That is an inferential property under a sampling model, not a reason to change every empirical moment divisor to n−1.

s² = [n/(n−1)]m2;   m2 = [(n−1)/n]s²Here:   m2=68/8=8.5 days²;   s²=68/7≈9.7143 days²

We use mr with divisor n throughout the descriptive Skewness and Kurtosis calculations. We later give named adjusted alternatives. Mixing an n-based third moment with an n−1-based SD changes the ratio; it is a different convention, not the defined empirical standardized moment. Stating the entire formula is more reliable than reporting “sample kurtosis” without details.

09Derive central moments from raw moments

Raw moments summarize powers of locations; central moments summarize powers after subtracting the mean. The bridge is the binomial expansion. For order three, multiplying (X−μ) three times gives X³−3μX²+3μ²X−μ³. Average term by term and use E[X]=μ. For order four the expansion coefficients are 1, −4, 6, −4, 1. These identities require finite moments of the orders used.

μ3=a3−3μa2+2μ³μ4=a4−4μa3+6μ²a2−3μ⁴

The empirical versions replace μ by x̄, aᵣ by âᵣ and μᵣ by mᵣ. Substitute the raw moments of our complete register:

m3=203.5−3×4×24.5+2×4³=203.5−294+128=37.5 days³m4=1986.5−4×4×203.5+6×4²×24.5−3×4⁴=1986.5−3256+2352−768=314.5 days⁴

These agree with direct averages of centered powers, a genuinely different arithmetic route. For example the fourth-power expansion includes −4μ³E[X]+μ⁴=−3μ⁴, which explains the final coefficient. Omitting that simplification or treating E[X²] as μ² would break the identity.

For hand calculations, the identities are useful cross-checks. In floating-point software, subtracting nearly equal large raw moments can lose precision. If durations are all near a large baseline, directly averaging centered powers is often numerically safer. Mathematical equivalence does not guarantee identical rounding behavior. A negative computed variance should be investigated rather than interpreted as a physical property.

10Standardized moments remove units, not information limits

A third moment measured in days³ cannot be compared directly with one measured in hours³. To compare shape, divide each centered value by its own Standard Deviation. Assume 0<σ²<∞ and define Z=(X−μ)/σ. The numerator and denominator share units, so Z is dimensionless. A value Z=2 means two SD above the mean, regardless of the original measurement unit. It does not by itself imply a Normal Distribution or a particular tail probability.

E[Z]=E[X−μ]/σ=0E[Z²]=E[(X−μ)²]/σ²=σ²/σ²=1λr=E[Zr]=μr/σr

λᵣ denotes a standardized population moment, when the corresponding absolute moment is finite. The first two standardized moments are always 0 and 1 under these conditions, so shape differences first appear at higher orders. For the empirical distribution use zi=(xi−x̄)/√m₂, and λ̂ᵣ=mᵣ/m₂r/2. The empirical average z² is then exactly one. Using the conventional sample SD s instead would give average z²=(n−1)/n, not one.

In our register √m₂=√8.5≈2.9155 days. The 11-day observation has z=7/√8.5≈2.4010. A one-day duration has z=−3/√8.5≈−1.0290. Both are measured relative to the same register center and spread. Keep √8.5 unrounded when computing their powers.

Pause and explain: What if every recorded duration equals 4?

Then m₂=0 and every centered value is zero. Standardization would divide 0 by 0, so Skewness and Kurtosis are undefined. Zero spread is a valid descriptive result; a standardized shape ratio is unavailable.

11Skewness: balance of cubed standardized deviations

Moment Skewness is the third standardized central moment. Cubing preserves direction and increases the influence of large distances. A positive result means positive cubed contributions outweigh negative ones; a negative result means the reverse. In many simple single-peaked distributions this agrees with a longer right or left tail, but the definition is the weighted cube balance, not a universal statement about which side contains more observations.

Population:   γ1=μ3/σ³=E[Z³]Empirical:   g1=m3/m23/2g1=37.5/(8.5√8.5)≈1.5132

Our third moment is positive because the 7-day positive deviation contributes 343, outweighing the combined −44 from all negative cubes. The observed pattern supports describing a strong right-sided cubic imbalance in this register. It does not establish a universal threshold for “severe skewness,” a probability of delay, or a population parameter known without uncertainty. Four of the eight observations lie below the mean, two equal it, and two lie above; the signs alone do not determine the moment.

Symmetry about a finite mean forces every integrable odd central moment to be zero: opposite deviations have matching probability and their odd powers cancel. The converse fails. A zero third moment can arise from cancellation in an asymmetric distribution. That distinction matters when numerical summaries are used to justify a symmetric model. The next example demonstrates the failure directly rather than asking you to accept it as a warning.

13Kurtosis and Excess Kurtosis: fourth powers relative to variance

Kurtosis in the Pearson convention is the fourth standardized central moment, β₂=E[Z⁴]. It averages nonnegative fourth powers of standardized distances. Large |Z| values receive substantial weight: |Z|=2 contributes 16 per unit of probability, whereas |Z|=0.5 contributes only 0.0625. Their ratio is 256. Kurtosis therefore summarizes fourth-power extremity relative to variance; it is not simply the height of a peak.

β2=μ4/σ⁴;   γ2=β2−3Empirical Pearson:   b2=m4/m2²b2=314.5/(8.5²)=314.5/72.25≈4.3529Empirical excess:   g2=b2−3≈1.3529

Excess Kurtosis subtracts three because a nondegenerate Normal Distribution has Pearson Kurtosis three. This is a reference convention, not a statement that all distributions should have that value. The normal model is developed in Guide 21. Pearson and excess conventions differ only by three; neither should be compared with the other without converting. β₂ is nonnegative, but γ₂ can be negative.

For any finite fourth moment and positive variance, β₂≥1. Let W=Z². Since E[W]=1, its variance is E[W²]−E[W]²=E[Z⁴]−1≥0. Therefore β₂≥1 and γ₂≥−2. The same argument applies to empirical b₂ when m₂>0. These bounds are a useful arithmetic diagnostic, not a normality test. More detailed information about tail probabilities still requires the distribution, not just one fourth-moment number.

14Equal mean and variance can hide very different extremes

Compare two constructed, symmetric distributions of standardized deviations. Both have mean zero and variance one. Distribution A places equal probability at −1 and +1. Distribution B places 0.90 at zero and 0.05 each at −√10 and +√10. These are finite-support teaching models, not fitted delivery processes. Both have Skewness zero by symmetry.

A has no outcome at least two SD from its mean. B assigns 0.10 to such outcomes because √10≈3.1623>2. Thus equal mean and variance do not imply equal probability of operationally extreme deviations. The greater fourth moment correctly distinguishes these examples, but it does not encode every threshold probability: distributions with larger kurtosis need not have larger tails at every possible cutoff.

The same comparison can be expressed in days with T=5+Z: both mean five days and have variance one day², yet B allows approximately 1.8377 and 8.1623 days as well as five. A allows four and six. Using both sides of the tail makes the arithmetic transparent; a real service decision would define a one-sided delay threshold and examine its actual probability. Neither this example nor Kurtosis alone identifies the cause of delays.

15Four moments still do not identify a distribution

A compact summary is useful partly because it discards detail. Even adding Skewness and Kurtosis does not generally recover the whole distribution. Consider Z with probability 1/6 at each of −√3 and +√3 and probability 2/3 at zero. By symmetry its mean and third moment are zero. Its variance is one and its fourth moment is three:

E[Z²]=(1/6)×3+(2/3)×0+(1/6)×3=1E[Z⁴]=(1/6)×9+0+(1/6)×9=3γ1=0;   β2=3;   γ2=0

Those four moment summaries match a standard Normal Distribution, yet this variable takes only three distinct values and has point mass 2/3 at zero. A continuous normal variable has zero probability at each single point. The distributions are plainly different. Therefore zero Skewness plus zero Excess Kurtosis does not prove normality, and a positive or negative sample coefficient is not a significance test.

Do not translate a moment into a full distribution

Report what the quantity actually averages. A fourth standardized moment measures an average of fourth powers; it does not uniquely determine peak height, modality, extreme-event frequency at every threshold, or whether support is bounded. Pair moment summaries with a distribution plot and context. Statistical model checking requires additional evidence.

16A continuous example: integrate before standardizing

Let X be a constructed duration uniformly distributed from zero to two hours: f(x)=1/2 per hour on 0≤x≤2 and zero elsewhere. “Uniform” means equal density across the interval, not positive probability for a single exact duration. The density is valid because ∫₀²(1/2)dx=1. Symmetry puts the mean at one hour; integration confirms it.

ar = (1/2)∫02xrdx= (1/2)[xr+1/(r+1)]02 = 2r/(r+1)a1=1 hour;   a2=4/3 hours²μ2=4/3−1²=1/3 hours²

For central moments substitute u=x−1. The bounds x=0 and 2 become u=−1 and 1, while dx=du. This simply recenters the integration interval, introducing no new probability assumption. Odd powers integrate to zero on this symmetric interval; even powers add:

μ3=(1/2)∫−11u³du=(1/2)[u⁴/4]−11=0μ4=(1/2)∫−11u⁴du=(1/2)[u⁵/5]−11=(1/2)(1/5+1/5)=1/5 hours⁴γ1=0;   β2=(1/5)/(1/3)²=9/5=1.8γ2=1.8−3=−1.2

Negative Excess Kurtosis is valid; it does not mean a negative fourth moment. This bounded uniform model has μ₄=0.2 hours⁴ and β₂=1.8, both positive. All moments exist because X is bounded. This model provides an integral counterpart to the discrete sums, but the observed register has not been assumed uniform.

17A finite mean and variance do not guarantee higher moments

An expectation over an unbounded range is an improper integral: first integrate to a finite endpoint M, then examine its limit as M grows. The limit must be finite for a finite moment. Consider a nonnegative constructed model with density f(x)=3/x⁴ for x≥1, using dimensionless x. Its total probability is [−x⁻³]₁∞=1, so it is a valid density. Multiplying by xʳ gives:

E[Xr]=3∫1∞xr−4dxFor r<3:   [3xr−3/(r−3)]1∞ = 3/(3−r)E[X]=3/2;   E[X²]=3;   Var(X)=3−(3/2)²=3/4

For r=3 the integral is 3∫₁ᴹ(1/x)dx=3 log M, which grows without bound. For r=4 it is 3(M−1), also divergent. Thus a finite mean and variance coexist with no finite third or fourth raw moment. Centering does not cure this: for x≥3, x−1.5≥x/2, so the central third and fourth absolute powers have divergent positive tails as well. Skewness and Kurtosis are not finite defined coefficients for this model.

A finite sample from such a model still produces finite empirical sums. That arithmetic fact does not prove the corresponding population moment exists. Also, symmetry cannot repair an undefined odd moment by informal cancellation of positive and negative infinities. A finite expectation requires control of absolute magnitudes, not a symmetric-looking plot. Higher finite absolute moments imply lower finite orders because |x|ˢ≤1+|x|ʳ for 0<s<r; the reverse implication fails, as this example shows.

18Predict what shifting, scaling and reflecting will change

Let Y=a+bX for fixed constants a and b≠0. Its mean is μY=a+bμ. Subtract it from Y: Y−μY=b(X−μ). Raising both sides to the r-th power and averaging gives μᵣ(Y)=bʳμᵣ(X). Absolute central moments scale by |b|ʳ. In particular σY=|b|σ. This proof explains why central moments ignore a shift but raw moments generally change.

λr(Y) = [br/|b|r] λr(X)b>0:   standardized moments unchangedb<0:   odd standardized moments reverse sign; even ones unchanged

Converting days to hours uses Y=24X. The mean becomes 96 hours, variance becomes 24²×8.5=4896 hours², the third central moment becomes 24³×37.5=518400 hours³ and the fourth becomes 24⁴×314.5=104343552 hours⁴. Despite the large numerical changes, g₁≈1.5132 and b₂≈4.3529 stay the same: numerator and denominator acquire the same power of 24.

Adding two days changes the mean from four to six but leaves all central moments and standardized moments unchanged. Reflecting about ten days with Y=10−X flips g₁ to −1.5132 and leaves b₂ unchanged. This reflected variable is an algebraic comparison, not a proposed delivery-time model; it includes −1. Nonlinear transformations such as logs need a fresh calculation and valid domain. Their effect on shape does not follow the affine rule. If b=0, Y is constant and standardized moments become undefined.

20Software conventions: a different answer may be a different formula

Our descriptive definitions use divisor n in every central moment. Some software offers size-adjusted coefficients. For n>2 and m₂>0, the adjusted Fisher–Pearson Skewness coefficient G₁ multiplies g₁ by √[n(n−1)]/(n−2). For n>3, a common adjusted Excess Kurtosis G₂ uses the formula below. The adjustment is not obtained by replacing every divisor with n−1. Nor does an adjustment guarantee unbiased estimation of arbitrary population standardized ratios.

G1 = [√(n(n−1))/(n−2)]g1G2 = [(n−1)/((n−2)(n−3))]× [(n+1)g2+6]
n=8:   G1=(√56/6)×1.5132228101≈1.8873G2=(7/30)×[9×1.3529411765+6]≈4.2412

Thus the same register can legitimately produce g₁≈1.5132 or G₁≈1.8873, and excess g₂≈1.3529 or G₂≈4.2412, when the stated convention changes. With SciPy, skew(..., bias=True) matches g₁, while bias=False requests G₁. kurtosis(..., fisher=False, bias=True) matches b₂; fisher=True returns excess, and bias=False requests its adjusted form. The supplied reproducible calculation below implements the formulas directly without requiring SciPy.

Name the convention, denominator, sample size, missing-value handling and whether three was subtracted. Adjusted coefficients describe a convention for sample estimation; our finite-register moment identities and the empirical bound b₂≥1 apply to unadjusted empirical ratios, not automatically to every adjusted estimator. Constant data still have undefined standardized moments. Small-sample coefficients can vary substantially even when their formulas are correctly implemented.

21Report a distribution summary without overclaiming

For the complete original register, a defensible statement is: “Across eight illustrative completed deliveries, the mean is four days and the empirical variance with divisor n is 8.5 days². The unadjusted moment Skewness is 1.5132 and Pearson Kurtosis is 4.3529, or Excess Kurtosis 1.3529. One 11-day record contributes about 95.43% of the fourth-power total. These are descriptive summaries of this register.” This states the unit, scope, convention and source of influence.

If an operational question asks how many deliveries exceeded five days, count the relevant records: only 11 exceeds five, so the observed fraction is 1/8=12.5%. Kurtosis 4.3529 is not that fraction. Predicting a future delay rate requires a suitable sample, information on unfinished parcels, dependence and changes in the process. The moments alone do not supply a confidence interval, a service guarantee or a causal explanation.

Diagnose common mistakes

  • “Average deviation is zero, so there is no spread.” Signed cancellation hides distance; m₂=8.5 contradicts the conclusion.
  • “Third moment 37.5 is Skewness.” It still has days³ units; divide by (√8.5)³.
  • “Kurtosis 4.3529 means a peak 4.3529 high.” It averages standardized fourth powers; no peak height is specified.
  • “Zero excess proves normality.” The three-point counterexample has excess zero and is discrete.
  • “Finite sample moments prove finite population moments.” A finite sample cannot establish the tail integrability condition.

22An optional reproducible Python cross-check

The mathematical argument above does not require code. This optional script reconstructs the complete register in a fresh Python 3 session using only the standard library. Each list comprehension visits all observations; order ranges from one through four. The script keeps exact integer inputs, uses unrounded floating-point intermediates, and rounds only selected printed coefficients. No download or random seed is needed because no random sampling occurs.

from math import sqrt

delivery_days = [1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 11]
parcel_count = len(delivery_days)
mean_days = sum(delivery_days) / parcel_count
centered_days = [value - mean_days for value in delivery_days]
raw_moments = [sum(value ** order for value in delivery_days) / parcel_count
               for order in range(1, 5)]
central_moments = [sum(value ** order for value in centered_days) / parcel_count
                   for order in range(1, 5)]
mean_abs_deviation = sum(abs(value) for value in centered_days) / parcel_count
variance_n = central_moments[1]
if variance_n <= 0:
    raise ValueError("Standardized moments require positive variance")
skewness = central_moments[2] / variance_n ** 1.5
pearson_kurtosis = central_moments[3] / variance_n ** 2
excess_kurtosis = pearson_kurtosis - 3
adjusted_skewness = sqrt(parcel_count * (parcel_count - 1)) / (parcel_count - 2) * skewness
adjusted_excess = ((parcel_count - 1) / ((parcel_count - 2) * (parcel_count - 3))
                   * ((parcel_count + 1) * excess_kurtosis + 6))
print("mean:", mean_days)
print("raw:", raw_moments)
print("central:", central_moments)
print("mean absolute deviation:", mean_abs_deviation)
print("g1, b2, g2:", *(round(value, 6) for value in
                          [skewness, pearson_kurtosis, excess_kurtosis]))
print("G1, G2:", round(adjusted_skewness, 6), round(adjusted_excess, 6))

Expected output

mean: 4.0
raw: [4.0, 24.5, 203.5, 1986.5]
central: [0.0, 8.5, 37.5, 314.5]
mean absolute deviation: 2.0
g1, b2, g2: 1.513223 4.352941 1.352941
G1, G2: 1.88732 4.241176

The index central_moments[1] selects the second-order moment because Python indices start at zero. The positive-variance check prevents division by zero; the adjusted formulas here are valid because the fixed input has n=8. A reusable function for arbitrary input would also validate n>2 or n>3 as appropriate, finite numeric values and missing-data rules. Do not let a library silently drop observations and then compare its answer with a different register.

For a future lab, replace the illustrative data only after sourcing a suitable real service-time dataset. Define completed and unfinished cases, units, duplicates and missingness before computing anything. Reconcile a small manual subset with software, compare raw-data plots and coefficients under named conventions, and explain why an observed tail summary does not prove a future population guarantee.

23Practice with complete solutions

Try each task before opening its complete solution. The problems move from reconstructing powers to changing reference, scale, probability structure, software convention and scientific interpretation. Each answer states why the operation is appropriate.

A. Rebuild moments on new data

For 1, 1, 4 hours, calculate the mean, the first four central moments, mean absolute deviation, g₁ and b₂. Explain why zero first central moment does not imply zero variability.

Worked solution

The mean is (1+1+4)/3=2 hours. Deviations are −1, −1 and +2 hours, whose average is 0. Squares are 1, 1 and 4, so m₂=6/3=2 hours². Cubes are −1, −1 and 8, so m₃=6/3=2 hours³. Fourth powers are 1, 1 and 16, so m₄=18/3=6 hours⁴.

The mean absolute deviation is (1+1+2)/3=4/3 hours. Then g₁=2/(2√2)=1/√2≈0.7071, b₂=6/2²=1.5 and g₂=−1.5. The sign of the first moment disappears through balance; squared distances remain positive. As an independent check, the raw second moment is (1+1+16)/3=6, and 6−2²=2 recovers the variance.

B. Change the reference, not the observations

For the original delivery register, average the signed and squared deviations from the target c=3 days. Is the squared result a variance?

Worked solution

The signed average is x̄−3=4−3=1 day. Direct differences are −2, −1, −1, 0, 1, 1, 2, 8. They sum to 8, giving 8/8=1. Squared differences sum to 4+1+1+0+1+1+4+64=76, so their average is 76/8=9.5 days².

The variance identity gives the same result: 8.5+(4−3)²=9.5. This is mean squared deviation around the target three, not variance around the actual mean four. The excess one day² is the squared center-to-target difference. The observations did not change, so their variance remains 8.5.

C. Reflection reverses an odd standardized moment

For the data in Exercise A, use Y=10−2X. Find the transformed mean, m₂, m₃, m₄, g₁ and b₂, and verify with the transformed list.

Worked solution

With a=10, b=−2 and original mean 2, the new mean is 10−2×2=6. Central moments multiply by bʳ: m₂=4×2=8, m₃=−8×2=−16 and m₄=16×6=96 in the corresponding transformed powers. Thus g₁=−16/(8√8)=−1/√2≈−0.7071 and b₂=96/8²=1.5.

The transformed list is 8, 8, 2; its average is 18/3=6 and its deviations are 2, 2, −4. Squared sum 4+4+16=24 gives 8; cubed sum 8+8−64=−48 gives −16; fourth-power sum 16+16+256=288 gives 96. The reversal changes cubic direction while preserving standardized fourth-power extremity.

D. Generalize the equal-variance comparison

Let 0<q≤1. Put probability q/2 at −1/√q and +1/√q and probability 1−q at zero. Derive mean, variance and Kurtosis. Evaluate q=0.25.

Worked solution

Probabilities total q/2+(1−q)+q/2=1. Symmetric contributions give mean and third moment zero. The second moment is (q/2)(1/q)+(q/2)(1/q)=1. Since the mean is zero, variance is one. The fourth moment is (q/2)(1/q²)+(q/2)(1/q²)=1/q; dividing by variance squared leaves β₂=1/q.

At q=0.25 the support is −2, 0, +2 with probabilities 0.125, 0.75, 0.125. Kurtosis is four and excess is one. The probability |Z|≥2 is 0.25, including the endpoints. Compared with q=0.10, this model has smaller Kurtosis but larger probability at this particular threshold (0.25 rather than 0.10). That directly demonstrates why Kurtosis is not a ranking of tail probability at every cutoff.

E. Check a continuous absolute deviation

For Uniform[0,2] hours, compute E[|X−1|] and compare it with SD. Why does the signed integral give a different answer?

Worked solution

Split at the mean because absolute value changes form there: E|X−1|=(1/2)∫₀¹(1−x)dx+(1/2)∫₁²(x−1)dx. The first integral before multiplying by 1/2 is [x−x²/2]₀¹=1/2; the second is [x²/2−x]₁²=1/2. The result is (1/2)(1/2+1/2)=1/2 hour.

SD is √(1/3)≈0.5774 hour, larger than 0.5 as expected from the absolute-deviation bound. Without absolute value, the lower half contributes −1/4 hour and the upper half +1/4 hour, totaling zero. These are two distinct averages: distance versus signed balance. Neither requires a normal assumption.

F. Resolve a convention mismatch

An analyst reports Pearson Kurtosis 4.3529 and another reports adjusted Excess Kurtosis 4.2412 for the original register. Are they simply separated by three? Explain what to compare.

Worked solution

Subtracting three from the unadjusted Pearson coefficient gives unadjusted excess 1.3529, not adjusted excess 4.2412. The size adjustment also matters: G₂=(7/30)[9×1.3529411765+6]=4.2411764706. To reverse it, g₂={G₂×30/7−6}/9=1.3529411765, then add three to recover b₂=4.3529411765.

The two reported numbers can both be correct under their formulas. Compare coefficients only after matching excess versus Pearson, adjustment, divisor and input observations. A constant shift of three converts Pearson and excess only at the same adjustment convention; it does not replace the size adjustment.

G. Diagnose undefined shape and infinite moments

A dataset is 5,5,5. Separately, a model has density 3/x⁴ on x≥1. Can each have zero Skewness and zero Excess Kurtosis because no finite computed imbalance was shown?

Worked solution

For 5,5,5, the mean is five and every central power is zero. But the standardized ratios are 0/0 because m₂=0. Skewness and Kurtosis are undefined, not zero. The finite raw moments are 5,25,125,625; raw moment existence is not the issue.

For the unbounded model, μ=1.5 and σ²=0.75 exist, but E[X³]=limₘ→∞3 log M diverges. The third central tail also diverges, as does the fourth. Its standardized third and fourth moments are not finite defined coefficients. These are different failure mechanisms: zero denominator in the finite constant dataset, and divergent numerator moments in the model. Neither justifies reporting zeros.

H. Repair a decision based on four summaries

A manager says: “Our standardized mean is zero, variance one, Skewness zero and excess zero, so deliveries are normal and we can guarantee their tail rate.” Use the three-point example to repair the claim.

Worked solution

The first two standardized moments are zero and one by construction whenever positive finite variance permits standardization. They are not evidence favoring normality. The three-point distribution at −√3,0,+√3 with probabilities 1/6,2/3,1/6 also has Skewness zero and excess zero, as verified by E[Z²]=1 and E[Z⁴]=3, but it is not normal.

For that distribution P(|Z|≥2)=0, because √3<2. The four summaries do not determine the tail rate for an arbitrary alternative distribution, and observed sample summaries do not guarantee future behavior. Repair the report by stating the descriptive conventions and data scope, plotting observations, defining the actual delay threshold, and seeking an appropriate model or sampling analysis. No causal or service guarantee follows from these moment values.

24A working vocabulary and notation

Raw Moment
E[Xʳ], an average of powers about zero.
Central Moment
E[(X−μ)ʳ], an average of powers about the mean.
Absolute Moment
An average of |X|ʳ or |X−μ|ʳ; declare the reference.
Empirical Moment
A moment of the observed distribution, weighting each record by 1/n here.
Standardized Moment
A central moment divided by the matching power of positive SD.
Skewness
Third standardized central moment; a signed cubic balance.
Pearson Kurtosis
Fourth standardized central moment; normal reference is three.
Excess Kurtosis
Pearson Kurtosis minus three, under the same convention.
Absolute integrability
Finite expected absolute magnitude; prevents undefined cancellation.
Sample adjustment
A named correction based on sample size; distinct from a raw empirical ratio.
μ, σ², σ
Population mean, variance and Standard Deviation.
mᵣ, g₁, b₂, g₂
Empirical central moment, Skewness, Pearson and excess coefficients using divisor n.

25What this guide adds and where it leads

Guide 03 introduced descriptive spread, quartiles and outlier screening. Guide 11 developed E[g(X)], variance and affine transformations. This guide connects those ideas through a hierarchy of raw, central, absolute and standardized powers. Its distinctive skill is choosing the reference, power, weights and convention before interpreting a number, and checking whether a population version exists.

You should now be able to reproduce the complete moment table, explain cancellation, derive central moments from raw ones, and compare distributions sharing the same lower moments. Equally important is knowing what the summaries cannot tell you: zero Skewness need not mean symmetry; matching four moments need not imply normality; an empirical fourth moment does not prove finite population Kurtosis or identify a future tail probability.

Instead of powers of one centered variable, it will use products of deviations from two variables to describe relationships. Neither generating functions nor parameter estimation is required to understand the present descriptive definitions.
