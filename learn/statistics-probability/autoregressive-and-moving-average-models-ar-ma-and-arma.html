01. What a dependence model is supposed to explain

An industrial plant observes electrical demand every hour. A forecast produced at 18:00 must use readings and operational information available by 18:00; it cannot borrow the 19:00 observation. Ordinary independent-data reasoning loses an important feature of this sequence: a high-demand hour is often followed by another relatively high-demand hour. An autoregressive model (AR) represents that persistence through earlier values of the series. A moving-average model (MA), despite its confusing name, represents persistence through current and earlier unpredictable innovations. Their combination is ARMA. These are models of how observations arise, not simply recipes for drawing a smoother curve.

The central question is therefore more precise than “is the graph smooth?” We want an explicit stochastic rule, its assumptions, what mean and variance that rule implies, how correlations change with lag, and exactly which quantities can be used at a forecast origin. A model can reproduce a pattern on a short synthetic series and still fail on real plant demand when production schedules, holidays, equipment shutdowns or price changes intervene. We will separate identities proved from model assumptions from estimates that need evidence in observed data.

This guide first develops AR(1) and MA(1) entirely, because their derivations give meaning to the symbols that appear in AR(p), MA(q) and ARMA(p,q). The purpose of the later polynomial notation is to compactly express the same dynamics, not to hide the reasoning. We will also distinguish a theoretical innovation, which is generally unobserved, from a fitted residual calculated using an estimated model.

02. Prerequisite bridge: probability, conditional expectation and time

Let \(Y_t\) be demand at time \(t\), measured in megawatts (MW). A lag is an earlier observation: \(Y_{t-1}\) is one hour earlier and \(Y_{t-2}\) is two hours earlier. The time index identifies order and frequency, not independent replicate customers. The unconditional mean \(E(Y_t)\) averages over hypothetical repetitions of the entire stochastic process. The conditional mean \(E(Y_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t)\) instead uses an information set \(\mathcal F_t\) containing information available by time \(t\). This distinction becomes decisive for forecasting.

A covariance \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-k})\) compares deviations from their respective means across repetitions of the process. Under weak stationarity the mean is constant, variance finite and constant, and the covariance depends on lag \(k\), not calendar time. The autocorrelation function (ACF) is \(\rho_k=\gamma_k/\gamma_0\), where \(\gamma_k\) is lag-\(k\) autocovariance and \(\gamma_0\) is the variance. Partial autocorrelation (PACF) removes intermediate linear-lag effects. The current guide assumes the introductory definitions from Guides 64 and 65 but reconstructs them wherever a computation needs them.

A conditional expectation has a mathematical justification: among all forecasts measurable with available information, it minimizes expected squared prediction error when the necessary moments exist. That optimality is about a specified model and squared-error loss. It does not guarantee that its parameters have been estimated correctly or that real-world relationships will persist.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\rho_k=\frac{\gamma_k}{\gamma_0},\qquad \gamma_k=\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-k})\]

03. What white noise does—and does not—assume

An innovation \(\varepsilon_t\) represents the new shock arriving at time \(t\) that cannot be predicted from the permitted earlier information. In our baseline derivations, innovations have mean zero, common finite variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), and zero covariance at distinct times. White noise commonly means precisely these second-order properties; it does not automatically mean a normal distribution or statistical independence. For conditional forecast calculations we additionally assume the innovation has zero conditional mean given the past. When we use independent innovations for a constructive example, we will label that stronger assumption.

The units matter: \(\varepsilon_t\) is measured in MW, its variance in MW squared, and \(\phi\) and \(\theta\) below are dimensionless coefficients. A high value of an innovation is a surprise relative to the chosen stochastic model, not necessarily a measurement error, a causal production intervention or a unique physical event. If residuals from a fitted model appear uncorrelated, that is evidence about leftover linear dependence, not a proof that hidden nonlinear dependence, drift or interventions have disappeared.

For example, if \(Z_t\) are independent standard normal variables and \(\varepsilon_t=Z_tZ_{t-1}\), neighboring innovations can be uncorrelated while still dependent because they share a factor. Consequently a sample ACF near zero is not proof of independence. We use the word shock descriptively, while avoiding the causal claim that the model discovered a physical cause.

Core relation / Relación central
\[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\quad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\quad\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_s)=0\ (t\neq s)\]

04. A reproducible twelve-hour teaching dataset

The main teaching example is synthetic, not an observed industrial dataset. The hypothetical plant has baseline demand \(\mu=50\) MW and an illustrative innovation sequence, in MW, \((1,-1,2,0,-2,1,0,2,-1,1,-2,0)\). We deliberately fix the innovations so every row can be checked without random seeds or software. The sequence is a teaching realization, not evidence that the innovations are a representative independent sample. Two alternative generating mechanisms use exactly these shocks: an AR(1) process with \(\phi=0.6\) and a MA(1) process with \(\theta=0.5\). They do not represent two independent measured experiments.

For the finite AR illustration, set the initial centered state \(Z_0=Y_0-50=0\), then calculate \(Z_t=0.6 Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\). For MA, set the presample shock \(\varepsilon_0=0\) and calculate \(Y_t=50+\varepsilon_t+0.5\varepsilon_{t-1}\). The deterministic starting values make the first twelve entries exactly reproducible, but a finite series initialized at its mean is not itself proof that the theoretical stationary distribution was used at time zero. Stationary moments derived later concern a process extending into the indefinite past.

For the first three hours the AR values are 51.000, 49.600 and 51.760 MW. The matching MA values are 51.000, 49.500 and 51.500 MW. Students should verify all rows of the table, then ask why two models driven by the same shocks can display noticeably different paths. What differs is the memory structure: AR propagates old effects geometrically; MA(1) carries each shock through at most one subsequent hour.

05. AR(1): explain every term before calculating

A first-order autoregression can be written \(Y_t=c+\phi Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\). Here \(c\), measured in MW, is an intercept; \(\phi\) is the coefficient of the preceding hour; and \(\varepsilon_t\) is the new shock. Under stationarity, the mean \(\mu\) satisfies \(\mu=c+\phi\mu\), giving \(c=(1-\phi)\mu\). It is generally incorrect to call \(c\) the long-run mean: unless \(\phi=0\), the intercept and mean differ. With \(\mu=50\) and \(\phi=0.6\), the intercept is \(20\) MW. Thus \(Y_t=20+0.6Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\).

Centering makes interpretation more transparent: define \(Z_t=Y_t-\mu\). Substitute \(Y_t=Z_t+\mu\) and \(Y_{t-1}=Z_{t-1}+\mu\) in the original equation, cancel \(\mu\), and obtain \(Z_t=\phi Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\). At hour 2, \(Z_1=1\) and \(\varepsilon_2=-1\); therefore \(Z_2=0.6(1)-1=-0.4\) MW and \(Y_2=49.6\) MW. At hour 3, \(Z_3=0.6(-0.4)+2=1.76\), hence \(Y_3=51.76\) MW.

An AR coefficient of 0.6 is not a claim that one MW of demand causes 0.6 MW next hour. It describes a conditional linear pattern under a particular model. The unmeasured innovation may include operational factors correlated with the past if assumptions fail. The coefficient can aid forecasting while lacking causal interpretation.

\[Y_t=c+\phi Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad c=(1-\phi)\mu\]

06. AR(1) mean and variance, derived rather than memorized

For the centered stationary process \(Z_t=\phi Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), taking expectations gives \(E(Z_t)=\phi E(Z_{t-1})\). If the mean is finite and time invariant, then \(m=\phi m\); for \(\phi\neq1\), \(m=0\). The original series therefore has mean \(\mu\). To derive variance, square the centered relation and take expectations. Assuming the current innovation is uncorrelated with the earlier state, \(\operatorname{Var}(Z_t)=\phi^2\operatorname{Var}(Z_{t-1})+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Under stationarity, the same variance \(\gamma_0\) appears on both sides. Solve \(\gamma_0(1-\phi^2)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), yielding \(\gamma_0=\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)\).

With \(\phi=0.6\) and innovation variance \(1\) MW², \(\gamma_0=1/(1-0.36)=1/0.64=1.5625\) MW², so standard deviation is \(1.25\) MW. The finite twelve-hour example does not have to exhibit exactly this variance; its starting value was fixed and its innovation sequence is unusually small and deliberately structured. The formula describes an ensemble property, not a summary statistic forced to match twelve selected observations.

The denominator reveals a stability condition: if \(|\phi|<1\), the geometric propagation of old shocks fades and a finite stationary variance exists. At \(\phi=1\), the stationary variance equation has no finite solution for positive innovation variance. If \(|\phi|>1\), forward propagation amplifies rather than dissipates earlier shocks; it is not the usual causal stationary AR(1) driven only by present and past innovations.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\gamma_0=\phi^2\gamma_0+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\gamma_0=\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\phi^2}}\]

07. AR(1) autocovariance, ACF and PACF

Multiply \(Z_t=\phi Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) by \(Z_{t-k}\), take expectations, and use the assumption that a current innovation is uncorrelated with states from earlier times. For \(k\ge1\), \(\gamma_k=\phi\gamma_{k-1}\). Starting from \(\gamma_0\), this gives \(\gamma_k=\phi^k\gamma_0\), hence \(\rho_k=\phi^k\). For our model the first three theoretical autocorrelations are 0.6, 0.36 and 0.216. They decay instead of becoming precisely zero after a finite lag.

Why is the PACF different? The lag-2 ACF includes both the direct association and the path through lag 1. Once the intervening lag 1 is conditioned on linearly, AR(1) contains no additional direct linear lag-2 coefficient. Therefore the population PACF cuts off after lag 1 under the model. Sample PACF bars will not be precisely zero because of estimation noise and finite data. Treat the familiar “ACF tails off, PACF cuts off” pattern as a model-identification clue, not a deterministic classifier.

If \(\phi<0\), the ACF alternates sign while shrinking in magnitude: for \(\phi=-0.5\), \(\rho_1=-0.5\), \(\rho_2=0.25\), \(\rho_3=-0.125\). This behavior illustrates that persistence does not always mean adjacent positive association. Negative AR coefficients describe an alternating response around the mean, and their magnitude still controls how rapidly the effect fades.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\gamma_k=\phi^k\gamma_0,\qquad\rho_k=\phi^k\quad(k\ge0)\]

08. A shock viewed through an infinite AR memory

Repeatedly substitute the AR(1) recursion: \(Z_t=\varepsilon_t+\phi\varepsilon_{t-1}+\phi^2\varepsilon_{t-2}+\cdots+\phi^j Z_{t-j-1}\). If \(|\phi|<1\) and the remote state has a suitable bounded second moment, the last term converges to zero as \(j\to\infty\). The causal stationary representation is \(Z_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^j\varepsilon_{t-j}\). This explains why a single shock may influence arbitrarily many future hours, although with geometrically diminishing weight.

Suppose a shock of 2 MW occurs at hour \(t\), with all other innovations held at zero in a controlled model impulse calculation. Its incremental effects are 2 MW immediately, \(0.6(2)=1.2\) MW after one hour, \(0.6^2(2)=0.72\) MW after two hours and \(0.6^3(2)=0.432\) MW after three hours. The total infinite sum of impulse coefficients is \(2/(1-0.6)=5\) MW-hours when summed across hourly observations. This is an accumulation of modeled hourly responses, not an estimate of a real plant intervention.

The infinite expansion additionally checks the variance: independent or pairwise uncorrelated innovations contribute \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\phi^2+\phi^4+\cdots)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)\). Two derivations arriving at the same result increase our confidence in the algebra. Neither derivation establishes that industrial demand actually follows this idealized AR process.

Core relation / Relación central
\[Z_t=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^{j}\varepsilon_{t-j}\quad\text{si }|\phi|<1\]

09. AR(1) forecast and its horizon-dependent error

At forecast origin \(t\) the latest observation \(Y_t\) is available. The innovation \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) is not. Taking the conditional expectation of \(Z_{t+1}=\phi Z_t+\varepsilon_{t+1}\) therefore gives \(\widehat{Y}_{t+1\mid t}=\mu+\phi(Y_t-\mu)\). Iterating and setting the conditional expectations of unknown future shocks to zero gives \(\widehat{Y}_{t+h\mid t}=\mu+\phi^h(Y_t-\mu)\). Under \(|\phi|<1\), forecasts revert toward \(\mu\) as the horizon increases.

The final AR value in our table is \(Y_{12}=49.211606\) MW (rounded). Its centered deviation is \(-0.788394\) MW. Therefore \(\widehat Y_{13\mid12}=50+0.6(-0.788394)=49.526964\) MW; the second-step forecast is \(50+0.36(-0.788394)=49.716178\) MW. Both calculations use hour 12 information only. Substituting the as-yet-unobserved \(Y_{13}\) in the second calculation would constitute leakage.

With known parameters and independent future innovations of variance \(1\) MW², the one-step forecast error is \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\), with variance \(1\) MW². For horizon 2, the error is \(\phi\varepsilon_{t+1}+\varepsilon_{t+2}\), with variance \(0.36+1=1.36\) MW². In general \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\phi^{2j}\). If innovations are Gaussian, a conditional normal prediction interval can be built; if not, mean and variance alone do not license normal-tail coverage claims.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\widehat{Y}_{t+h\mid t}=\mu+\phi^h(Y_t-\mu),\quad \operatorname{MSE}_h=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\phi^{2j}\]

10. MA(1): a shock with finite memory

A moving-average process of order one is \(Y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\). The coefficient \(\theta\) determines how much of the preceding shock is carried into the present observation. Unlike a moving average used for smoothing, this is not the average of earlier observations. An MA(1) uses unobserved shocks; a four-hour rolling average might instead calculate \((Y_t+Y_{t-1}+Y_{t-2}+Y_{t-3})/4\). These operations have different definitions, units, information requirements and statistical interpretations.

With \(\mu=50\) MW, \(\theta=0.5\), and the illustrative presample value \(\varepsilon_0=0\), hour 1 is \(Y_1=50+1+0.5(0)=51\) MW. Hour 2 is \(Y_2=50-1+0.5(1)=49.5\) MW, and hour 3 is \(Y_3=50+2+0.5(-1)=51.5\) MW. The shock at hour 1 adds 1 MW to hour 1 and 0.5 MW to hour 2, then has no direct contribution from hour 3 onward. This contrasts with the AR geometric response.

A finite-order MA with white-noise innovations and finite variance is stationary in the weak sense because its mean and autocovariance do not depend on calendar time. That statement holds even for \(|\theta|\ge1\); the separate restriction \(|\theta|<1\) is about invertibility, meaning whether innovations can be recovered uniquely by a stable series of observed past values. Treating stationarity and invertibility as the same condition produces incorrect model conclusions.

Core relation / Relación central
\[Y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\]

11. MA(1) mean, variance and its unique nonzero covariance lag

Take expectations of \(Y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Since both shocks have mean zero, \(E(Y_t)=\mu\). Center the series, then calculate \(\gamma_0=\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1})\). Uncorrelated innovations remove the covariance cross-term, so \(\gamma_0=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\theta^2)\). For \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2=1\) MW² and \(\theta=0.5\), this is \(1+0.25=1.25\) MW². The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{1.25}\approx1.1180\) MW.

For lag one, write \(Y_t-\mu=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\) and \(Y_{t-1}-\mu=\varepsilon_{t-1}+\theta\varepsilon_{t-2}\). Their only common innovation is \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Multiplying the sums and taking expectations leaves \(\gamma_1=\theta\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), hence \(\rho_1=\theta/(1+\theta^2)=0.5/1.25=0.4\). At lag two or higher, the two combinations involve disjoint innovation times, so \(\gamma_k=0\) under the white-noise assumption.

The theoretical ACF thus cuts off after lag one. The PACF generally decays rather than cutting off there for invertible MA(1). A sample ACF from twelve deliberately chosen values may not look exactly like the population function. A cut-off is an ideal population property, while empirical sampling variability and imperfect parameter estimates create additional bars. This distinction prevents us from declaring a model identified solely by inspecting two short plotted sequences.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\gamma_0=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\theta^2),\qquad\rho_1=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2},\qquad\rho_k=0\ (k\ge2)\]

12. The invertibility problem and why parameters need a convention

For \(Z_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\), rearrange to \(\varepsilon_t=Z_t-\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Repeated substitution gives \(\varepsilon_t=Z_t-\theta Z_{t-1}+\theta^2Z_{t-2}-\theta^3Z_{t-3}+\cdots\). If \(|\theta|<1\) and suitable moment conditions hold, the weights diminish and the infinite representation is stable. Thus past observed values can reconstruct innovations in a well-behaved way. If \(|\theta|>1\), the recursive weights increase in magnitude, making that reconstruction unstable.

Surprisingly, the autocorrelation of MA(1) is identical for \(\theta=0.5\) and \(\theta=2\): \(0.5/1.25=2/5=0.4\). If innovation variance is 1 with \(\theta=2\), the marginal variance is 5. The same second-order structure arises with \(\theta=0.5\) and innovation variance 4, because \(4(1+0.25)=5\) and \(4(0.5)=2\) for lag-one covariance. This is why covariance information alone does not always choose an unrestricted MA parameter uniquely.

The invertible restriction conventionally selects the representative with coefficient magnitude below one. It is not a statement that data with \(\theta=2\) would fail weak stationarity. Nor does invertibility mean that the analyst knows the true shocks after seeing a finite sample: numerical filtering still needs initial states, parameters and a statistical method, creating differences between filtered residuals and ideal innovations.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\varepsilon_t=Z_t-\theta Z_{t-1}+\theta^2Z_{t-2}-\cdots\quad(|\theta|<1)\]

13. MA(1) forecasting: what is and is not observable

Under invertibility and an information history rich enough to recover shocks, \(\varepsilon_t\) is measurable from the infinite observation past. The one-step theoretical conditional forecast is \(\widehat Y_{t+1\mid t}=\mu+\theta\varepsilon_t\), since future \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) has conditional mean zero. For horizons two and higher, no retained shock from time \(t\) enters the MA(1) equation: \(\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}=\mu\) when \(h\ge2\). This sharp horizon change is a defining finite-memory feature.

In our constructed example we know all innovations because they were supplied to generate the data. The last one is \(\varepsilon_{12}=0\), so both the one-step and two-step forecasts happen to be 50 MW. That coincidence should not be mistaken for the universal MA forecast: if the last known innovation had been \(2\) MW, the first forecast would instead be \(50+0.5(2)=51\) MW while the second remained 50 MW. A genuine analyst sees demand, not the supplied innovation column, and must estimate the final shock from a fitted invertible model.

One-step forecast error is \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\), with variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). At any horizon \(h\ge2\), the outcome includes two future innovations, \(\varepsilon_{t+h}+\theta\varepsilon_{t+h-1}\), giving variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\theta^2)\) when future innovations are uncorrelated. With variance 1 and \(\theta=0.5\), this is 1.25 MW². The interval precision does not continue deteriorating past horizon two in the known-parameter stationary MA(1) model.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\widehat Y_{t+1\mid t}=\mu+\theta\varepsilon_t,\qquad\widehat Y_{t+h\mid t}=\mu\quad(h\ge2)\]

14. Comparing AR and MA without overreading ACF plots

The AR and MA mechanisms can both make adjacent hours positively correlated. Our AR(1) has \(\rho_1=0.6\), \(\rho_2=0.36\); our MA(1) has \(\rho_1=0.4\), \(\rho_2=0\). A long, stable dataset might help distinguish an exponential tail from a cutoff, but the twelve-row case is far too short to reliably select a model by visual pattern. A single outlier, drift, seasonal structure or a measurement gap can distort the sample correlogram, and the choice of ACF estimator also affects short-sample numerical results.

An AR(1) is defined by a regression on the preceding observed value plus a new innovation. An MA(1) is defined by a combination of new and previous innovations, not a lagged observed value. The AR shock survives through all future hours with ever-shrinking weight; the MA shock directly affects only two hours. The theoretical PACF shows the opposite qualitative distinction from the ACF: AR(1) partial correlation cuts off after lag one, while invertible MA(1) usually has a fading PACF.

There are important exceptions to simplistic diagrams. A small AR coefficient may make later correlations nearly invisible at realistic sample sizes. An MA coefficient near zero resembles white noise. Mixed ARMA dynamics can make both ACF and PACF tail off. In practical model identification, domain explanation, stationary transformation choices, estimated parameters, residual diagnostics and performance on chronological holdouts are all relevant; no single bar chart settles the question.

15. ARMA(1,1): persistence and temporary innovations together

An ARMA(1,1) combines both mechanisms: \(Z_t=\phi Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\), where \(Z_t=Y_t-\mu\). The AR part carries the preceding centered observation forward; the MA part adds a temporary contribution from the preceding innovation. The model is not equivalent to blending an AR forecast and a rolling-average smoother. Both effects operate in a single stochastic recursion, and the innovation history must be handled consistently.

For a demonstration with \(\phi=0.6\), \(\theta=0.5\), \(Z_{12}=-0.788394\) MW and \(\varepsilon_{12}=0\) assumed known by construction, the next forecast is \(50+0.6(-0.788394)+0.5(0)=49.526964\) MW. The second-step forecast is \(50+0.6(-0.473036)=49.716178\) MW because future innovations have mean zero. This numerical coincidence with the AR-only case arises solely because the last supplied shock was zero. If \(\varepsilon_{12}=2\), the first forecast would be one MW higher; the second would reflect its effect propagated through the AR coefficient.

For a stationary causal ARMA(1,1), \(|\phi|<1\). For the usual invertible representation, \(|\theta|<1\). The model's ACF generally has a decaying tail and its PACF generally has another tail. Parameter estimation must use the entire model rather than first fitting AR and then independently fitting MA to the original data. Forecast evaluation must keep both parameters and hidden-state estimates fixed at each forecast origin.

Core relation / Relación central
\[Z_t=\phi Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\]

16. ARMA covariance is a check on the combined mechanism

Using the infinite expansion of a stationary ARMA(1,1), the coefficients on innovations are \(\psi_0=1\) and \(\psi_j=(\phi+\theta)\phi^{j-1}\) for \(j\ge1\). The first coefficient is the immediate shock response; the following coefficients combine the carryover from the AR and MA components. Squaring and summing gives \(\gamma_0=\sigma_\varepsilon^2[1+(\phi+\theta)^2/(1-\phi^2)]\), equivalent to \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+\theta^2+2\phi\theta)/(1-\phi^2)\).

For \(\phi=0.6\), \(\theta=0.5\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2=1\), the numerator is \(1+0.25+0.6=1.85\) and denominator 0.64, giving \(\gamma_0=2.890625\) MW². The lag-one covariance is \(\gamma_1=\phi\gamma_0+\theta\sigma_\varepsilon^2=0.6(2.890625)+0.5=2.234375\) MW². Therefore \(\rho_1=2.234375/2.890625\approx0.77297\), and for higher lags \(\gamma_k=\phi^{k-1}\gamma_1\) for \(k\ge1\).

This is a population property derived under the model, not the sample covariance of the short constructed trajectory. AR and MA interactions affect the variance and first-lag correlation; one cannot obtain these by adding separate AR and MA correlation coefficients. The derivation also provides a diagnostic consistency check for the sign convention: we have written the MA term with a plus sign, \(+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Other textbooks or software interfaces may use a minus convention, which changes parameter signs but not the underlying physical relationship.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\gamma_0=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\frac{1+\theta^2+2\phi\theta}{1-\phi^2},\quad \gamma_1=\phi\gamma_0+\theta\sigma_\varepsilon^2\]

17. Lag operator: notation that simplifies, not replaces, thought

Define the lag operator \(L\) by \(LY_t=Y_{t-1}\), \(L^2Y_t=Y_{t-2}\), and more generally \(L^kY_t=Y_{t-k}\). It is an operator acting on a time series, not a number to insert casually into an ordinary calculator. For centered AR(1), \(Z_t=\phi LZ_t+\varepsilon_t\) becomes \((1-\phi L)Z_t=\varepsilon_t\). For MA(1), \(Z_t=(1+\theta L)\varepsilon_t\). Combining them yields \((1-\phi L)Z_t=(1+\theta L)\varepsilon_t\).

This compact notation is valuable because repeated lags become polynomial expressions. It also makes the difference between the two models visible: AR coefficients multiply a polynomial in the observed centered process, while MA coefficients multiply a polynomial in the unobserved innovation process. The formal series \((1-\phi L)^{-1}=1+\phi L+\phi^2L^2+\cdots\) corresponds to the infinite AR shock expansion when it converges appropriately. Its algebra is not a license to ignore initial values or convergence requirements.

A useful verification is to apply \((1-0.6L)\) to a sequence and confirm the result at time 3: \(Z_3-0.6Z_2=1.76-0.6(-0.4)=2\), matching \(\varepsilon_3=2\). The operator equation is meaningful precisely because it reproduces a concrete hour-by-hour calculation. Students who can only quote the polynomial but not perform this substitution have not yet understood the notation.

Core relation / Relación central
\[(1-\phi L)Z_t=(1+\theta L)\varepsilon_t\]

18. An independent residual calculation: conditional AR filtering

A filtering algorithm is easier to understand once one can reproduce a single prediction error without software. In the fixed-parameter AR demonstration, after hour 1 we know \(Y_1=51\) MW. The one-step prediction made for hour 2 is \(20+0.6(51)=50.6\) MW. Once hour 2 is observed at 49.6 MW, the one-step prediction error is \(49.6-50.6=-1\) MW. At hour 3, the forecast from hour 2 is \(20+0.6(49.6)=49.76\) MW. Observed hour 3 is 51.76 MW, so its prediction error is \(51.76-49.76=2\) MW. These exactly equal the supplied innovations because the teaching parameters and starting state are the ones used to construct the series.

Now imagine estimating \(\widehat\phi=0.5\) rather than using the known 0.6. If we kept the process mean at 50, the corresponding fitted intercept would be \((1-0.5)50=25\) MW. The fitted hour-2 prediction becomes \(25+0.5(51)=50.5\), and the residual is \(49.6-50.5=-0.9\) MW, not the generating innovation −1 MW. This difference is not a spreadsheet mistake. It arises because fitted parameters are different from the synthetic parameters.

For a fitted MA(1), the residual sequence is even more sensitive to the presample innovation used to initialize the recursion. If the analyst starts with \(\widehat\varepsilon_0=0\), the first filtered residual is \(Y_1-\mu\), followed by \(\widehat\varepsilon_2=Y_2-\mu-\theta\widehat\varepsilon_1\). A different initialization changes early residuals, although under invertibility those effects diminish. This is the beginning of conditional filtering, not an assertion that the estimated innovations have been independently observed.r

19. Parameter sensitivity and forecast robustness

The AR(1) forecast is a function of both the latest centered observation and the persistence coefficient. Suppose the last reading is 54 MW and the long-run mean is 50 MW. At horizon four, a model with \(\phi=0.6\) predicts \(50+0.6^4(4)=50+0.5184=50.5184\) MW. A model with \(\phi=0.8\), using the same latest observation and mean, predicts \(50+0.8^4(4)=50+1.6384=51.6384\) MW. A modest-looking difference of 0.2 in the coefficient becomes a 1.12 MW difference at horizon four.

The contrast is not evidence that either coefficient is the true one. It shows how model uncertainty may alter an operational decision. The theoretical forecast error variance also changes: for \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2=1\) MW², at horizon four it is \(1+\phi^2+\phi^4+\phi^6\). With \(\phi=0.6\), this equals \(1+0.36+0.1296+0.046656=1.536256\) MW². With \(\phi=0.8\), it is \(1+0.64+0.4096+0.262144=2.311744\) MW². Greater persistence slows the decay of past shocks and increases known-parameter medium-horizon error variance.

Such sensitivity motivates comparing plausible parameter estimates, checking the size of the training period and evaluating forecasts across multiple chronological origins. The formula may be exact under its assumptions while the parameter estimates remain uncertain. A responsible recommendation distinguishes these two uncertainties and avoids turning a point forecast into a claim of certain future demand.r

20. General AR(p), MA(q) and ARMA(p,q)

An AR(p) uses \(p\) observed lags: \(Z_t=\phi_1Z_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pZ_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t\). A MA(q) uses \(q\) innovation lags: \(Z_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}\). An ARMA(p,q) contains both sets of terms. The lag polynomials are \(\Phi(L)=1-\phi_1L-\cdots-\phi_pL^p\) and \(\Theta(L)=1+\theta_1L+\cdots+\theta_qL^q\), so \(\Phi(L)Z_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t\). Orders \(p\) and \(q\) are nonnegative integers, not multiplication factors.

The general condition for a causal stationary AR representation is that the complex-number roots of \(\Phi(z)=0\) lie outside the unit circle, meaning their modulus (distance from zero in the complex plane) exceeds one. For invertible MA representation the roots of \(\Theta(z)=0\) must similarly lie outside that circle. A complex root need not be purely real: a number \(a+bi\) has modulus \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). Students do not need to solve high-degree complex polynomials here, but must understand what a root condition asserts and how it reduces to \(|\phi|<1\) or \(|\theta|<1\) for order one.

For AR(1), \(1-0.6z=0\) has root \(z=1/0.6\approx1.667\), outside the unit circle. For MA(1), \(1+0.5z=0\) has root \(-2\), also outside. By contrast, \(\theta=2\) yields MA root \(-0.5\), inside, and therefore noninvertibility even though its finite MA covariance remains stationary. These short checks connect the general theorem with earlier algebra.

Core relation / Relación central
\[\Phi(L)Z_t=\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\quad\Phi(z)\neq0,\ \Theta(z)\neq0\text{ para }|z|\le1\]

21. Estimation: known parameters versus fitted parameters

Up to this point the illustrative coefficients \(\mu=50\), \(\phi=0.6\), \(\theta=0.5\) and the innovation sequence were supplied by construction. In real data none of those shocks is written in a separate column and the coefficients must be estimated. For an AR(1) one can estimate an intercept and lag coefficient by regression of \(Y_t\) on \(Y_{t-1}\) under suitable assumptions. One may also use conditional or exact likelihood approaches. A full stationary Gaussian likelihood accounts for the distribution of the initial observation rather than silently treating its first value as fixed. Differences in initialization and whether means are estimated explain why legitimate software outputs may differ.

For MA and mixed ARMA, current innovations are latent. Likelihood-based estimation filters them conditional on a model and handles initial uncertainty. Other methods provide starting estimates, but minimizing an ad hoc sum of squared filtered errors is not automatically identical to exact maximum likelihood. A sample of twelve observations is much too small to infer stable industrial ARMA coefficients reliably. Fitting an elaborate ARMA(p,q) only because its in-sample error decreases invites overfitting.

Statistical claims also need conditions: stationarity, invertibility where relevant, model specification, innovation moments, and assumptions for standard errors or intervals. An estimated coefficient's significance alone cannot establish forecast superiority. Compare simple benchmarks using rolling-origin holdouts, and report both estimation uncertainty and forecast error where possible. This is why the next curricular guide can move naturally from the model's mechanisms to formal ARIMA identification, estimation and diagnostics.

22. Residual diagnosis: a model is not validated by one coefficient

Suppose a model is fitted on hours 1 through \(T\). A one-step fitted innovation estimate can be written \(\widehat\varepsilon_t=Y_t-\widehat Y_{t\mid t-1}\), but it depends on the fitted coefficients, past observations and how the filtering algorithm initialized latent states. This is a residual, not automatically the exact unobserved innovation from the true generating mechanism. If our synthetic coefficients and supplied shocks were known, we could reconstruct illustrative residuals under the specified initialization; an applied researcher cannot make that identification by declaration.

Look at residuals over time, their mean and changing variance, possible seasonal patterns and their ACF. Ljung–Box can summarize serial correlation over a chosen set of lags, but its reference distribution requires attention to fitted parameters and sample size. Failure to reject a residual test is not proof of independence or correct model specification. Large residuals can indicate missing predictors, an equipment event, data error or an inadequately modeled distribution; model diagnostics alone do not tell us which explanation is true.

A responsible workflow documents the timestamp, missing-data handling and training period; examines stationarity and transformed scales; fits a small set of defensible models; checks residual dependence; and then performs strictly chronological forecasting on held-out observations. The model order and parameters must be chosen without using future test outcomes. The analyst should report both point accuracy and interval calibration rather than announcing that a visually acceptable residual plot proves forecast reliability.

23. Identification traps and the honest limits of the model

A model can be mathematically stationary while observations show a temporary run of high values; finite samples can be deceptive. Conversely, a visibly increasing mean may indicate nonstationarity or an omitted schedule, not merely an AR coefficient close to one. MA(1) invertibility does not confer stationarity on a badly transformed real series, and an apparently white residual does not validate assumptions about the innovation distribution. In short, theoretical conditions describe a model class; diagnostic evidence determines whether that class is a defensible approximation for a particular application.

Several tempting shortcuts fail. Do not conflate MA innovations with rolling averages of observed readings. Do not equate a model's known synthetic innovation column with the information available to an operational analyst. Do not call the intercept a process mean without conversion. Do not infer causality from autoregressive persistence. Do not identify order solely from two large ACF bars, nor add together AR and MA autocorrelations when forming ARMA. Do not claim a Gaussian prediction interval has exactly 95% coverage without the distributional and calibration assumptions that justify it.

The final transfer goal is to explain the mechanism in everyday words: “AR remembers past deviations; MA remembers recent surprises; ARMA remembers both.” Then reproduce the mean, variance and autocorrelations with algebra; issue a forecast with only known information; check the model restrictions; and identify which conclusions are theoretical, sample-specific or conditional on estimated parameters. That sequence demonstrates understanding more strongly than memorizing a list of model acronyms.

24. Solved exercise 1 — interpret the time index and innovation units

Problem. Hour 7 demand is 50.108096 MW in the synthetic AR table. Identify the unit, the preceding hour, the innovation at hour 7, and explain whether this represents seven independent plants.

Solution, step 1. The unit of \(Y_7\) is MW, a power measurement; the observation indexed \(t=6\) comes one hour before hour 7. Step 2. The supplied innovation for hour 7 is \(\varepsilon_7=0\) MW, not an observed prediction residual from a fitted dataset. Step 3. From the table, \(Y_6=50.18016\) MW, and the AR recursion gives \(20+0.6(50.18016)+0=50.108096\) MW. Step 4. This is the seventh observation of one hypothetical plant, not seven independently randomized plants. The short time separation and shared process can induce dependence.

Verification and interpretation. Subtracting the baseline gives \(0.108096\) MW. Multiplying the preceding deviation \(0.18016\) by 0.6 also gives \(0.108096\). This independent centered-form check demonstrates that an hour may remain above its mean even with no new shock. The result depends on the model's initial state and parameters, so it is not an empirically measured causal response.

25. Solved exercise 2 — derive the AR intercept from the mean

Problem. A stable hourly process has average demand 60 MW and AR coefficient \(\phi=0.75\). Find the intercept; then evaluate its one-hour conditional mean from an observed 68 MW if the future innovation has conditional expectation zero.

Solution. Starting from \(E(Y_t)=c+0.75E(Y_{t-1})\), stationarity implies \(60=c+0.75(60)\). Multiply \(0.75(60)=45\) MW, subtract from 60 and obtain \(c=15\) MW. The one-hour conditional forecast is \(15+0.75(68)=15+51=66\) MW. In centered form, the same calculation is \(60+0.75(68-60)=60+6=66\) MW. The two approaches agree exactly.

Interpretation. Although the previous demand is eight MW above the process mean, the forecast is only six MW above it because the excess decays by a factor of 0.75. Calling the intercept 15 MW the expected long-run demand would be wrong. This formula is a conditional prediction under a specified stochastic process; missing temperature or production schedules may invalidate it for real demand.

\[c=(1-\phi)\mu=(1-0.75)(60)=15\ \mathrm{MW}\]

26. Solved exercise 3 — derive and check AR variance

Problem. For stationary AR(1) with \(\phi=0.6\) and innovation variance \(4\) MW², determine the process variance, standard deviation and the corresponding autocovariance at lag two.

Solution. The innovation is orthogonal to the prior state, so \(\gamma_0=\phi^2\gamma_0+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Substitute \(\gamma_0=0.36\gamma_0+4\); subtract to get \(0.64\gamma_0=4\). Hence \(\gamma_0=4/0.64=6.25\) MW² and the process standard deviation is \(\sqrt{6.25}=2.5\) MW. Next, \(\gamma_2=\phi^2\gamma_0=0.36(6.25)=2.25\) MW². The normalized lag-two correlation is \(2.25/6.25=0.36\).

Verification. Substitute 6.25 into the variance equation: \(0.36(6.25)+4=2.25+4=6.25\). It balances. Do not interpret 6.25 as an estimate from the twelve-hour teaching table; it is the theoretical variance for a different assumed innovation variance. The units of variance are MW², not MW.

27. Solved exercise 4 — compare positive and negative AR dependence

Problem. Compare theoretical correlations at lags one, two and three for AR(1) coefficients 0.5 and −0.5. Both have innovation variance 1 MW². What is the same, and what changes?

Solution. For \(\phi=0.5\), \(\rho_1=0.5\), \(\rho_2=0.5^2=0.25\), \(\rho_3=0.5^3=0.125\). For \(\phi=-0.5\), compute \(\rho_1=-0.5\), \(\rho_2=(-0.5)^2=0.25\), \(\rho_3=(-0.5)^3=-0.125\). Both satisfy \(|\phi|<1\) and have variance \(1/(1-0.25)=4/3\) MW², because the variance depends on the square of \(\phi\).

Interpretation. Positive persistence keeps same-signed deviations for a while; negative dependence alternates predicted deviations above and below the mean. The same unconditional variance does not imply the same sequence dynamics. This matters operationally: a model with the wrong sign can predict the opposite direction of the next deviation even if its estimated overall variance looks plausible.

28. Solved exercise 5 — forecast AR at two horizons and quantify uncertainty

Problem. Starting from \(Y_t=54\) MW, take \(\mu=50\) MW, \(\phi=0.6\) and innovation variance 1 MW². Forecast one and two hours ahead and give the errors' variances.

Solution. The present deviation is \(54-50=4\) MW. One-step forecast: \(50+0.6(4)=52.4\) MW. Two-step forecast: \(50+0.6^2(4)=50+1.44=51.44\) MW. Neither uses a future observed value. At horizon one, forecast error is the unknown next innovation with variance 1 MW². At horizon two, the error is \(0.6\varepsilon_{t+1}+\varepsilon_{t+2}\), whose variance is \(0.36+1=1.36\) MW² given uncorrelated innovations.

Check and limit. The forecasts lie progressively closer to 50 MW and uncertainty rises. A Gaussian conditional interval requires Gaussian innovations as an additional assumption; simply multiplying the standard deviation by 1.96 does not establish real 95% coverage under a misspecified or nonnormal process. Parameter-estimation uncertainty is also omitted from these known-parameter calculations.

29. Solved exercise 6 — calculate MA moments and the cutoff

Problem. For MA(1) with \(\theta=-0.4\), innovation variance \(9\) MW² and mean 20 MW, find the process mean, variance, lag-one autocovariance, first two autocorrelations and stationarity status.

Solution. Zero-mean innovations imply process mean 20 MW. Variance: \(9(1+(-0.4)^2)=9(1.16)=10.44\) MW². Lag-one covariance is \((-0.4)(9)=-3.6\) MW². Therefore \(\rho_1=-3.6/10.44\approx-0.344828\); \(\rho_2=0\), as pairs two lags apart share no innovation. Weak stationarity holds for finite MA order under the assumed white noise; also \(|\theta|<1\) means the representation is invertible.

Check. The absolute correlation is less than 0.5, consistent with the MA(1) bound \(|\theta|/(1+\theta^2)\le0.5\). It is wrong to state that a negative MA coefficient necessarily produces a downward trend: the model has constant mean 20 MW and short negative lag-one association, not an expected time trend.

30. Solved exercise 7 — explain the MA forecast discontinuity

Problem. Suppose \(Y_t=30+\varepsilon_t+0.7\varepsilon_{t-1}\), with current innovation known to equal −2 MW for this synthetic demonstration. Forecast the next two observations using only available innovation information.

Solution. For time \(t+1\), write \(Y_{t+1}=30+\varepsilon_{t+1}+0.7\varepsilon_t\). Conditional expectation of \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) is zero, while \(\varepsilon_t=-2\) is known by stipulation. Thus \(\widehat Y_{t+1\mid t}=30+0.7(-2)=28.6\) MW. At time \(t+2\), both \(\varepsilon_{t+2}\) and \(\varepsilon_{t+1}\) are future shocks with zero conditional means, so \(\widehat Y_{t+2\mid t}=30\) MW.

Interpretation and verification. The negative current shock affects one forecast step, then disappears from the MA(1) structure. If someone forecasts 28.6 MW at every horizon, they have confused temporary shock memory with a persistent AR state. In a fitted real model \(\varepsilon_t\) must be filtered from observations, not supplied as an oracle.

31. Solved exercise 8 — test MA invertibility without confusing stationarity

Problem. Consider two MA(1) coefficients, \(\theta=0.5\) and \(\theta=2\). Determine their lag polynomial roots, stationarity and invertibility; explain why observing lag-one ACF alone cannot distinguish them.

Solution. For \(\theta=0.5\), solve \(1+0.5z=0\) to obtain \(z=-2\) with modulus 2, so it is invertible. For \(\theta=2\), the root of \(1+2z=0\) is \(z=-0.5\) with modulus 0.5, so it is noninvertible. Both are finite moving averages of white noise and consequently weakly stationary. Their lag-one autocorrelations are \(0.5/(1+0.25)=0.4\) and \(2/(1+4)=0.4\), respectively.

Check and boundary. By scaling innovation variance to match the marginal variance, their full second-order covariance structures can coincide. ACF alone therefore cannot identify the unrestricted representation. Invertibility selects a stable parameterization for reconstructing shocks; it is not a test that observations had to come from one particular physical mechanism.

32. Solved exercise 9 — apply a lag polynomial to concrete data

Problem. Given \(Z_2=-0.4\), \(Z_3=1.76\) and \(\phi=0.6\), evaluate \((1-0.6L)Z_3\) and interpret the result. Then give the MA(1) polynomial for \(\theta=0.5\).

Solution. The operator \(L\) shifts the time index backward one observation, so \(LZ_3=Z_2=-0.4\). Expanding the operator expression gives \(Z_3-0.6Z_2=1.76-0.6(-0.4)=1.76+0.24=2\) MW, matching the supplied hour-three innovation. The MA operator is \(1+0.5L\); applying it to innovations gives \(\varepsilon_t+0.5\varepsilon_{t-1}\).

Interpretation. The polynomial is a compact time-shifting instruction, not a symbolic shortcut disconnected from observed chronology. To check it, identify the appropriate preceding row first, then perform the arithmetic. Replacing \(L\) with a scalar number 0.6 would have no temporal meaning. The distinct AR and MA polynomials act on different sequences.

33. Solved exercise 10 — derive ARMA variance and covariance

Problem. For stationary ARMA(1,1) with \(\phi=0.6\), \(\theta=0.5\) and innovation variance \(1\) MW², derive the variance and first autocorrelation. State the sign convention.

Solution. Under \(Z_t=0.6Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t+0.5\varepsilon_{t-1}\), compute \(1+\theta^2+2\phi\theta=1+0.25+2(0.6)(0.5)=1.85\). Divide by \(1-\phi^2=0.64\) to obtain \(\gamma_0=1.85/0.64=2.890625\) MW². Then \(\gamma_1=\phi\gamma_0+\theta\sigma_\varepsilon^2=0.6(2.890625)+0.5=2.234375\) MW². Normalize: \(\rho_1=2.234375/2.890625\approx0.772973\).

Verification and meaning. These are population quantities. They are not sums of the AR-only and MA-only autocorrelations. The signs use a plus MA term; a software package with an alternative sign convention would require a corresponding parameter conversion. Do not compare coefficients by symbol alone without reading its model equation.

34. Solved exercise 11 — distinguish a theoretical innovation from a residual

Problem. A fitted ARMA model produces residual \(\widehat\varepsilon_{10}=1.4\) MW. A synthetic generating table has true innovation \(\varepsilon_{10}=1\) MW. Is the fitting procedure necessarily wrong, and may the analyst treat both values interchangeably?

Solution. No. A residual is calculated as observed value minus a one-step prediction based on estimated parameters and initialization. A true innovation belongs to an assumed data-generating process, which is not directly observed in practice. If the estimate of \(\mu\) or \(\phi\) differs from the generating values, or hidden states are initialized differently, a residual need not equal a true innovation even when the estimation procedure is appropriate. The difference is \(1.4-1=0.4\) MW, but this one difference does not identify the source of error.

Interpretation. The analyst should inspect temporal residual patterns, fitted-versus-forecast origins, estimation method and stability; not simply “correct” residuals toward a synthetic oracle. When we teach from a constructed dataset, we know true shocks because we designed it. This knowledge cannot be transported into a real forecasting workflow without explicitly modeling how shocks are inferred.

35. Solved exercise 12 — assess AR(2) roots and a complex-number bridge

Problem. Consider \(Z_t=0.5Z_{t-1}+0.2Z_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t\). State its lag polynomial and explain the root criterion, without assuming all roots are real.

Solution. Moving the lag terms to the left gives \((1-0.5L-0.2L^2)Z_t=\varepsilon_t\). The polynomial equation is \(1-0.5z-0.2z^2=0\), equivalent to \(0.2z^2+0.5z-1=0\). By the quadratic formula, \(z=[-0.5\pm\sqrt{0.25+0.8}]/0.4=[-0.5\pm\sqrt{1.05}]/0.4\). The roots are approximately \(1.3117\) and \(-3.8117\); both moduli exceed one, satisfying the usual causal stationarity condition.

Check and transfer. Substituting either root into the polynomial gives approximately zero, within rounding. For a higher-order polynomial, roots may be complex; use modulus \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), not just the real part. For example \(1.2+0.9i\) has modulus \(\sqrt{1.44+0.81}=1.5\), outside the unit circle. The criterion concerns the complete polynomial rather than each individual coefficient independently.

36. Solved exercise 13 — recognize the hazard of future information in model choice

Problem. A forecasting team tests AR(1), MA(1) and ARMA(1,1) on the last 100 hours, chooses whichever had the smallest error, then reports that same minimum as an unbiased assessment of the selected model. What is the flaw and what is a defensible alternative?

Solution. The 100-hour block has served both model selection and final evaluation, creating optimistic selection bias. The problem is not that ARMA is mathematically invalid; it is that future outcomes have influenced which model is presented as the winner. A defensible design uses a chronological training period for parameter estimation, an internal validation period or rolling-origin procedure for model/order selection, and a separate later holdout for final assessment. Each origin issues a forecast before the corresponding observation is available; residual updates are performed only when the next observation has genuinely arrived.

Interpretation. Compare the selected model against simple baselines such as Naive or Seasonal Naive, report errors by horizon and, where justified, prediction-interval coverage. Short-term ACF fit alone does not establish business usefulness. A well-performing sophisticated model can still be unsuitable when operating conditions change or exogenous events dominate demand.

37. Solved exercise 14 — final transfer: choose a justified dynamic explanation

Problem. A factory reports that (i) high demand tends to persist for several hours, (ii) a sudden unexpected outage has effects lasting only the current and following hour in one department, and (iii) another department combines both effects. Recommend candidate model structures and state the checks needed before deployment.

Solution. Step 1: For the first department, AR(1) is a parsimonious starting hypothesis because past centered demand persists geometrically. Verify stationarity using an appropriate transformation and check that the fitted AR root lies outside the unit circle. Step 2: For the second, MA(1) is a plausible shock-memory description, but do not identify actual outage causes from the model alone. Derive its first-lag ACF and verify the invertibility restriction. Step 3: For the third, ARMA(1,1) is a reasonable candidate because it combines persistence and short innovation memory. Estimate the model jointly rather than adding independent AR and MA forecasts.

Step 4: Inspect the observation frequency, timestamps, data gaps, cycles, variance changes, ACF/PACF and residual structure; compare with chronological baseline forecasts. Step 5: Communicate uncertainty, operational costs, signs of regime change and the distinction between predicted demand and an intervention's causal effect. Independent conceptual check: explain each model without an acronym, write its equation, calculate one forecast using available information and say what would disprove its adequacy. This final exercise tests transfer rather than rote substitution.

38. Final synthesis and academic reference trail

A first-order AR model passes old deviations forward through lagged observations. A first-order MA model carries recent innovations forward for a finite number of periods. An ARMA model combines the two, with explicit conditions for stable causal dynamics and for reconstructing latent innovations. In each case we have gone from a concrete temporal table to equations, derived theoretical moments, traced the fate of a shock and issued forecasts without borrowing future observations. The derivations are mathematical results conditional on the white-noise and model assumptions. The constructed hourly table is an illustration, not empirical support for the assumptions.

What should remain after studying? You should be able to reproduce hour 2 and hour 3 under both generating mechanisms, compute \(\gamma_0\) and \(\rho_1\) without a formula sheet, explain why AR(1) and MA(1) have opposite ACF/PACF identification patterns, distinguish weak stationarity from invertibility, translate a lag polynomial into a concrete time-index calculation, and compute one- and two-hour forecasts with an information cutoff. If any step remains a black box, return to the corresponding worked example and perform the arithmetic independently.

Sources for formal verification and further study. The main external technical references are Forecasting: Principles and Practice, third edition, sections on ARIMA/ARMA dynamics, and the official `statsmodels` time-series ARIMA documentation. The cited general root conditions and estimation conventions are standard theoretical results; the simplified algebra here is developed directly. External documentation does not validate the hypothetical industrial case or substitute for an applied data audit. See the links in the resource section after the exercises.
