01A number between zero and one needs a meaning

A value such as 0.40 might describe the fraction of study time spent practicing, the observed success fraction on a short quiz, or a belief about an unknown success probability. These numbers share a range, but they are not the same mathematical object. Before choosing a Beta distribution, we must decide what varies, what is observed, and what the probability statement refers to.

The Beta family is useful because its continuous density lives on the unit interval and can express many shapes: approximately flat, concentrated around a middle value, tilted toward an endpoint, or high near both endpoints. It therefore supplies a flexible language for positive fractions below one. Flexibility does not make it a universal model for every percentage, and a distribution fitted across people is not automatically a Bayesian Prior for one common parameter.

This guide develops the mathematics and those interpretation choices together. You will normalize an elementary density by integrating a polynomial, generalize it through the Beta function, derive moments, interpret both Shape parameters, calculate interval and conditional probabilities, and invert a CDF to find quantiles. You will then contrast population variation with parameter uncertainty and see why finite observed proportions require an additional sampling model.

The essential preparation is continuous random variables, expectation, variance, and moments from Guides 10–12. We recall these ideas where needed. Calculus I is sufficient for the central example; singular endpoints, integration by parts, and numerical bisection receive local explanations. Gamma-function identities are identified as cited extensions rather than hidden prerequisites. Programming is optional: a complete standard-library script later checks the central calculations.

02Define a continuous fraction before assigning a density

Our running education example is constructed, not a dataset from a school. Select one student from a specified population for one specified week. Let Q be the fraction of that student's positive total self-study time spent solving practice problems. If a student studies five hours and practices for two, the realized fraction is 2/5=0.40. The denominator must be positive; a week with no self-study needs a separate definition or exclusion rule.

We idealize time as continuously measured and suppose 0<Q<1. That assumption excludes structural cases with no practice at all or with all study time spent practicing; we later discuss how to handle such endpoints. The candidate population model is Q∼Beta(α=2, β=3). It describes variation across student-weeks under the stated selection scheme. It does not say that every student has the same fraction or that practice causes a particular educational outcome.

A probability such as P(Q>0.60) asks about the fraction of the modeled population whose practice share exceeds 60%. A density at q=0.60 instead describes local concentration near that fraction. A realized fraction 0.40 is one observation, and cannot by itself determine a population distribution with two unknown Shape parameters. Here the parameters are stipulated to teach the consequences of a model, not estimated from that one student.

The variable has no physical units: hours divided by hours is dimensionless. Its percentage version 100 Q is measured on a 0–100 scale. We will transform the density carefully when switching to percentages, because density heights depend on the coordinate system even when event probabilities remain unchanged. First we need to keep the different roles of similar-looking fractions separate.

03Separate observation, population variation, and uncertainty

A continuous population quantity Q, a fixed probability p, and an observed count fraction R=Y/n need separate symbols. Suppose a different experiment administers n binary questions under an ideal common success probability p. The number correct Y is an integer; R can only take values 0,1/n,2/n, …,1. A Beta density cannot be its exact finite-sample probability mass function, even though all those fractions lie between zero and one.

An unknown p is a parameter of that experiment. In a frequentist model it is fixed but unknown; the observations vary across repeated samples. In a Bayesian formulation we may assign a random quantity P a distribution representing declared uncertainty about p. Such an assignment must state what information it represents. Neither the word “unknown” nor the fact that p lies in[0,1] selects a particular Beta distribution automatically.

A useful diagnostic is to complete this sentence before calculating: “The probability refers to randomly selecting ___, while ___ is held fixed.” In our main model, we select a student-week and the stipulated population parameters are held fixed. In the later uncertainty illustration, the distribution instead represents uncertainty about one common parameter. The mathematics can look identical while the conclusion concerns a different object.

04Build a density from an elementary kernel

Start with the nonnegative curve q(1−q)² for 0<q<1. Call it a kernel: it supplies the shape but has not yet been scaled to total area one. Near zero, the q factor suppresses the height; near one, the squared factor(1−q)² suppresses it more strongly. This suggests a curve concentrated away from the endpoints and tilted toward smaller fractions. We must integrate before treating it as a probability density.

q(1−q)² = q−2 q²+q³.∫01 q(1−q)² dq = [q²/2−2 q³/3+q⁴/4]01.= 1/2−2/3+1/4 = (6−8+3)/12 = 1/12.

Multiplying the curve by 12 makes the area exactly one. Thus f(q)=12 q(1−q)² on the open interval and zero outside it is a legitimate density. This simple calculation is the motivation for a normalizing function, not an exercise in naming a formula before explaining it. The constant compensates for the entire area of the chosen kernel.

At q=0.40, f(q)=12·0.40·0.60²=1.728. A height exceeding one is allowed: only an integrated probability must lie between zero and one. A narrow interval of width 0.01 near 0.40 has probability approximately 1.728·0.01=0.01728 when density variation across it is small. The exact probability comes from integration, which we develop next.

Check the logic in order: the curve is nonnegative on the support; its integral is 1 after scaling; a single point still has probability zero. Dividing by 12 instead of multiplying would leave area 1/144, so the direction of normalization matters. The denominator form is f(q)=q(1−q)²/(1/12), which explains why the small integral produces a multiplier larger than one.

05From density height to accumulated probability

The cumulative distribution function F(c)=P(Q≤c) accumulates density from the left endpoint to c. For the main density, expand the polynomial and integrate term by term. The lower endpoint contributes zero. Within the support we obtain a simple polynomial that can be evaluated without special-function software.

F(c) = ∫0c 12 q(1−q)² dq.F(c) = 6 c²−8 c³+3 c⁴, 0≤c≤1.F(c)=0 for c<0; F(c)=1 for c>1.

At c=0.60, the three polynomial terms are 2.16, −1.728, and 0.3888. Adding gives F(0.60)=0.8208. Therefore P(Q>0.60)=1−0.8208=0.1792. Under the stipulated population law,17.92% of student-weeks have a practice fraction above 60%. This is not the claim that a particular student's fraction has probability 17.92% of changing, nor a causal effect of practice.

For this case the survival probability has a factored expression S(c)=(1−c)³(1+3 c). Expanding verifies that it equals 1−F(c). At c=.60, that expression gives .40³·2.80=.1792, an independent algebraic check that also avoids subtracting two nearly equal numbers near the right endpoint.

The figure shows complementary probabilities at each threshold, not two different populations. At c=0, F=0 and S=1; at c=1, F=1 and S=0. Inside, F grows and S decreases. Differentiating F gives 12 c−24 c²+12 c³=12 c(1−c)², recovering the original density and checking that accumulation and local height agree.

06Work an interval probability from beginning to end

Suppose the question is whether the practice fraction lies between 25% and 75%. The event is 0.25<Q<0.75. A CDF includes everything to the left of a threshold, so subtracting F(.25) from F(.75) removes the unwanted lower portion. Because Q is continuous, including either endpoint would not change this probability.

F(.25) = 6(.25)²−8(.25)³+3(.25)⁴.= .375−.125+.01171875 = .26171875.F(.75) = 3.375−3.375+.94921875 = .94921875.P(.25<Q<.75) = .94921875−.26171875 = .6875.

The shaded area is 68.75%, while the density heights along the interval are not percentages. The peak occurs near one third, but the event includes a broad range of fractions. It is neither the density at the midpoint nor the length of the interval alone. A length of .50 would equal probability .50 only for the Uniform density, not for this shaped curve.

Check the result by integrating 12 q(1−q)² directly from .25 to .75. A second check partitions the entire support: probability below .25 is .26171875, the middle probability is .6875, and probability above .75 is .05078125. Their sum is exactly 1. The asymmetry of the two outer pieces is consistent with the density favoring smaller fractions.

07Give the normalizing integral a general name

The polynomial example suggests a family of kernels qα−1(1−q)β−1, where α>0 and β>0 are Shape parameters. The exponents are one less than the parameter names. In our example, the powers 1 and 2 therefore correspond to α=2 and β=3. Reading the powers as the parameters would give the wrong density and normalizing constant.

B(α, β) = ∫01 qα−1(1−q)β−1 dq.f(q) = qα−1(1−q)β−1/B(α, β), 0<q<1.

The capital B names the Beta function, a deterministic function of two positive numbers. It returns the area of the unnormalized kernel. Dividing by that area creates the Beta distribution's density. The function B and the distribution Beta are related objects, but they are not interchangeable: B(2,3)=1/12 is a number, while Q∼Beta(2,3) specifies a probability law for a random variable.

Normalization now follows from the definition: ∫f(q) dq=B(α, β)/B(α, β)=1. The function is positive and finite for the allowed parameters, with endpoint convergence justified next. Neither parameter is a Rate or a Scale in the Gamma sense. Both are dimensionless Shapes; software may call them a and b. We use Greek letters to distinguish these Shape parameters from a later physical interval's endpoints L and H.

As a quick elementary comparison, B(1,1)=∫011 dq=1, so Beta(1,1) is Uniform on the unit interval. Also B(2,1)=∫01 qdq=1/2, giving density 2 q. A single family therefore contains flat and increasing curves as well as the main interior-peaked example, with normalization supplied by the same construction.

08Understand singular heights without inventing endpoint mass

If a Shape is below one, its corresponding exponent is negative and the density may diverge at an endpoint. This is not a reason to discard the distribution. What matters for normalization is whether the area is finite. Near q=0, the factor(1−q)β−1 is bounded above and below by positive finite constants on a sufficiently small interval. The kernel therefore behaves like qα−1 there.

∫0ε qα−1 dq = εα/α, α>0.

This area is finite and tends to zero as ε decreases to zero. Near q=1, substitute v=1−q; the same argument requires β>0. Splitting the integral at 1/2 handles both endpoints separately. These comparisons justify why every positive α and β works, even when the graph shoots upward near a boundary.

For Beta(1/2,1/2), B=π and the density is 1/[π√(q(1−q))]. It diverges at both ends but integrates to one. Neither P(Q=0) nor P(Q=1) is positive under this continuous law. A spike in a density plot is not a probability atom. The probability in a shrinking interval next to an endpoint still tends to zero, even though it can be relatively large compared with an equally short interval in the middle.

The condition is strict. Taking α=0 with β>0 would create a 1/q-type nonintegrable singularity at zero, so the ordinary Beta density is not defined there. Likewise β=0 is invalid. Some Bayesian discussions use formal improper expressions with zero Shapes; those are not proper probability distributions of the kind defined here and require separate analysis. We keep both parameters strictly positive.

09Connect B to useful identities without hiding prerequisites

The Beta integral has a classical connection to the Gamma function: B(α, β)=Γ(α)Γ(β)/Γ(α+β). Here Γ(a)=∫0∞xa−1 e−x dx for a>0, with Γ(a+1)=aΓ(a) and Γ(1)=1. Guide 19 develops that integral; it is not required to understand our polynomial example. We cite the general Beta–Gamma identity from NIST rather than claim its usual double-integral proof follows from the prerequisites here.

For positive integers, Γ(k)=(k−1)!, so B(2,3)=1!2!/4!=2/24=1/12. This agrees with the direct polynomial integration. The independent agreement checks both the parameter shift and the normalization. For noninteger Shapes, numerical special-function routines evaluate the same function, not a different probability model.

The moment calculations need two shift identities, which can also be obtained using only one-dimensional integration. First q+(1−q)=1 implies B(α+1, β)+B(α, β+1)=B(α, β). Second, integrate the derivative of qα(1−q)β from 0 to 1. Its boundary terms vanish for positive α and β, leaving α B(α, β+1)=β B(α+1, β). Solving these two equations gives the following identities.

B(α+1, β) = [α/(α+β)] B(α, β).B(α, β+1) = [β/(α+β)] B(α, β).

The derivative step can be performed first from ε to 1−ε and then passed to limits using the endpoint integrability established above. Thus the recurrences used for mean and variance have an accessible justification even if you do not use the general Gamma identity. Distinguishing what was proved locally from what was cited keeps the reasoning honest and the guide self-contained for its central tasks.

10General probabilities and an integrable singular example

For arbitrary positive Shapes, accumulated probability remains an area even when no convenient polynomial appears. Define the incomplete Beta integral B_c(α, β) by stopping the normalizing integral at c instead of one. Then F(c)=B_c(α, β)/B(α, β) for 0≤c≤1. The normalized ratio is often written I_c(α, β). A software routine computes this ratio numerically; the notation does not replace its probability meaning.

An instructive noninteger case is Beta(.5, .5). Its CDF is ∫0cdq/[π√(q(1−q))]. Substitute q=sin²θ with 0≤θ≤π/2. Then dq=2 sinθ cosθ dθ and√(q(1−q))=sinθ cosθ, so the difficult-looking integrand simplifies to 2 dθ/π. Evaluating at the transformed upper limit gives the following expression.

F(c) = (2/π) arcsin(√c), Q∼Beta(.5, .5).

At c=.10, this gives approximately .2048327647. Symmetry puts the same probability above .90, so P(Q<.10 or Q>.90)≈.4096655294. The high endpoint densities therefore correspond to substantial nearby area, but still zero probability at the endpoints themselves. This calculation supplies a quantitative reading of the U-shaped plot rather than relying on appearance alone.

For general Shapes, use a validated Beta CDF, survival function, and inverse CDF. A direct survival routine is preferable when 1−Fsubtracts nearly equal floating-point values. Always distinguish a normalized incomplete integral from an unnormalized one; forgetting the denominator changes total probability. The elementary central case and the trigonometric singular case provide independent reference examples against which software behavior can be checked.

11Read the roles of both Shape parameters

The two factors act near opposite ends. The exponent α−1 governs behavior near zero, while β−1 governs behavior near one. If both parameters exceed one, the density vanishes at both endpoints and has an interior peak. If both equal one, it is flat. If both are below one, it has a U-shaped pattern with high density near both ends. If one is below one and the other above one, it tilts strongly toward the endpoint associated with the smaller parameter.

The figure separates finite and singular examples into two panels. Curves with infinite endpoint limits are drawn only on[.01, .99], and their omitted behavior is explicitly described. No artificial finite value is inserted at a singular boundary. The height axis differs across panels so that the shape comparisons remain readable; compare the labels rather than assuming identical vertical scales.

When α>β, the mean will lie above one half; when α<β, below one half. That does not mean α is itself a probability. Shapes 2 and 3 are valid despite exceeding one, and α/(α+β), not α alone, determines the mean. Increasing both Shapes proportionally holds the mean fixed while changing concentration. Increasing just one changes both the mean and the concentration, so its effect cannot be summarized as “more certainty” without qualification.

Observe the contrast between Beta(.5, .5) and Beta(5,5). Both are symmetric around .5 and share mean .5, but one favors regions near the ends and the other favors the center. Mean and symmetry alone therefore do not locate where most density is concentrated. We derive the variance and the mode to make these differences quantitative.

12Reflect the fraction and swap the Shapes

Let V=1−Q be the fraction of self-study time spent on activities other than practice. The transformation reverses the horizontal axis. For 0<v<1, the density transformation gives fᵥ(v)=f_Q(1−v), because the absolute derivative of 1−v is one. Substitute into the Beta density: the powers become(1−v)α−1 vβ−1. Also B(α, β)=B(β, α), as can be shown by substituting v=1−q in its defining integral.

Q∼Beta(α, β) implies 1−Q∼Beta(β, α).

For Q∼Beta(2,3), V∼Beta(3,2). Its mean is .60 and its variance is the same as Q's. The event V>.60 means 1−Q>.60, or Q<.40. Thus P(V>.60)=F_Q(.40)=.5248. It is not the same event as Q>.60, whose probability is .1792. Interchanging the Shapes changes the direction of the asymmetry.

If α=β, reflection leaves the density unchanged and the distribution is symmetric about .5. If α≠β, the reflected distribution is generally different. This gives an exact way to check a plot or computation: the Beta(3,2) height at v=.70 must equal the Beta(2,3) height at q=.30. The relation holds for every interior coordinate, not just for matching means.

The interpretation changes with the variable definition. A high practice share becomes a low nonpractice share for the same student-week. It does not create a second independent observation; V and Q are deterministically linked. Treating them as two independent pieces of evidence would count the same information twice.

13Derive the interior mode and handle boundary cases

For positive interior q, maximize the logarithm of the density instead of the full product. Since the logarithm is increasing, the maximizer is unchanged. The variable part is(α−1) log q+(β−1) log(1−q). The negative sign in the second derivative term below comes from differentiating 1−q, not from an arbitrary convention.

d(log f)/dq = (α−1)/q − (β−1)/(1−q).(α−1)(1−q) = (β−1) q.q_mode = (α−1)/(α+β−2), α>1, β>1.

With both Shapes above one, the second derivative of log f is−(α−1)/q²−(β−1)/(1−q)²<0. The stationary point is therefore the unique interior maximum. For Beta(2,3), the mode is 1/3≈.333333. The mean will be .40 and the median≈.385728; each summary answers a different question.

Do not use the interior-mode formula without its conditions. If α=β=1, the density is constant and every interior point has the same height; the formula becomes 0/0. If both Shapes are below one, the interior stationary point is a minimum, with density increasing without bound toward both endpoints. For the U-shaped Beta(.5, .5), the formula returns .5 but that is the bottom of the U, not its peak.

For α=1 and β>1, the density decreases toward the right and has its greatest limiting height at zero; the reflected case increases toward one. A boundary supremum does not imply positive probability exactly at the boundary. Naming the condition is part of using a formula, not a footnote that can be omitted after obtaining a numerical answer.

14Calculate the mean directly before generalizing

Expectation weights each possible fraction by its density. The mean of a continuous Q is not the height of the density at a typical point; it is the integral of qf(q). For our polynomial density, multiply by q before expanding and integrating. The extra q changes the power, which is why normalization alone does not already supply the mean.

E[Q] = ∫01 q·12 q(1−q)² dq.= 12∫01(q²−2 q³+q⁴) dq.= 12(1/3−1/2+1/5) = 12(10−15+6)/30 = .40.

Under the population interpretation, .40 is the model's mean practice share across the defined student-weeks. It is not a requirement that each student practice for 40% of their time. Nor is it necessarily the ratio of total practice hours to total study hours across all students. Averaging fractions gives each student-week equal weight; a ratio of totals weights students by their total study time.

For a simple distinction, two students study for one and nine hours, with practice fractions .20 and .60. The equally weighted average fraction is(.20+.60)/2=.40. Their total practice time is .20·1+.60·9=5.60 hours out of ten, giving pooled fraction .56. These constructed observations illustrate two different summaries; they are not data used to fit our Beta parameters.

This matters when reporting an expected fraction: specify the sampling unit and weighting scheme. The main Q model selects a student-week uniformly from the target population, rather than selecting a random study minute from the pooled hours. A mathematical mean can be correct while answering the wrong applied question if that distinction is missing.

15Derive the general moment using shifted Beta integrals

For the general family, multiplying the density by qr changes only its first exponent. The resulting numerator is another Beta integral with its first Shape shifted by r. For real r, the moment exists when α+r>0; positive integer moments always satisfy that condition. Near one, β>0 continues to ensure integrability.

E[Qr] = B(α+r, β)/B(α, β), α+r>0.E[Q] = α/(α+β).E[Q²] = α(α+1)/[(α+β)(α+β+1)].

The mean follows by applying the first shift identity once. The second moment follows twice: first multiply by α/(α+β), then by(α+1)/(α+β+1). These are ratios of normalizing integrals, not probabilities of two actual trials unless a separate experiment supplies that interpretation. The integral defines a moment of the variable being modeled.

For α=2 and β=3, the general mean is 2/5=.40 and the second moment is 2·3/(5·6)=.20. Direct polynomial integration gives an independent check: E[Q²]=12∫01(q³−2 q⁴+q⁵) dq=12(1/4−2/5+1/6)=12/60=.20. The mean squared is .40²=.16, which differs from the mean of squares. That difference will be the variance.

As a connection to the moments vocabulary, a third raw moment is 2·3·4/(5·6·7)=4/35≈.114286 for the main case. The pattern continues through positive integer powers. You need not memorize a separate formula for each order: identify how the exponent shifts and apply the recurrence. The condition α+r>0 also explains why negative-power moments require care even though Q itself is bounded above.

16Develop variance and interpret its scale

Variance measures average squared deviation from the mean μ. Expanding(Q−μ)² and taking expectations gives E[Q²]−μ². Write κ=α+β temporarily to keep the algebra readable. Subtract the two moment expressions using a common denominator, rather than combining fractions mentally and skipping the cancellation.

Var(Q) = α(α+1)/[κ(κ+1)] − α²/κ².= [α(α+1)κ−α²(κ+1)]/[κ²(κ+1)].= α(κ−α)/[κ²(κ+1)] = αβ/[(α+β)²(α+β+1)].

For Beta(2,3), Var(Q)=.20−.16=.04 and SD(Q)=√.04=.20. A standard deviation of .20 in fractional units corresponds to 20 percentage points. It does not mean a 20% relative change from the mean; a relative change depends on the reference value. Variance on the percentage scale will be 400 percentage-point squared, because multiplying a variable by 100 multiplies its variance by 10,000.

The standard deviation describes spread of student-week fractions under the population model. It is not the standard error of an estimated population mean. We have not specified a sample size or an estimator here, and the parameters were assumed. Nor does mean±SD automatically contain a prescribed percentage of a non-Normal distribution. For this example, the interval .20 to .60 has probability F(.60)−F(.20)=.6400, not a universally promised 68%.

A check through an independently centered integral is ∫01(q−.40)²12 q(1−q)² dq=.04. It measures exactly the quantity variance defines and agrees with subtracting raw moments. Using two routes helps detect the common error of subtracting .40 instead of .40², which would even yield a negative value and violate variance's nonnegativity.

17Separate the mean from concentration

The two Shapes jointly control location and spread. A useful reparameterization names the mean μ and concentration κ=α+β. Since μ=α/κ, solve for α=μκ and β=(1−μ)κ. For a proper Beta,0<μ<1 and κ>0. This is a change of coordinates for the same family, not an additional assumption or a third independent parameter.

α=μκ, β=(1−μ)κ.Var(Q) = μ(1−μ)/(κ+1).

At a fixed mean, increasing κ reduces variance. For μ=.40, choices κ=5,20,80 give Shapes(2,3), (8,12), (32,48). The means match, while variances are .04, .0114285714, .0029629630. Standard deviations are .20, .106904497, .054433105. Thus the same average practice share is compatible with markedly different population heterogeneity.

The interpretation of concentration depends on the random variable's role. In a population distribution, larger κ means less variation across units. In an explicitly chosen uncertainty distribution, larger κ means less spread over plausible parameter values at the same mean. It does not automatically equal an actual number of students, observations, or successful trials. Any effective-sample-size interpretation requires a separately declared convention and model; we do not infer real evidence from κ alone.

18Check whether a proposed mean and variance are feasible

For any variable strictly between zero and one, Q²<Q, so E[Q²]<E[Q]=μ. Subtract μ² to obtain Var(Q)<μ(1−μ). Variance is positive for a nondegenerate continuous Beta. This simple bound is useful before solving for parameters: a requested variance can be impossible for any proper Beta, regardless of numerical fitting software.

From the concentration formula, a proposed mean 0<μ<1 and variance v determine κ=μ(1−μ)/v−1. Positive κ requires 0<v<μ(1−μ). Then α=μκ and β=(1−μ)κ. This is a population-level moment-matching specification, not yet an estimator or a guarantee that real data follow Beta.

For μ=.40 and v=.04, κ=.24/.04−1=5, giving α=2 and β=3. If instead a proposal used v=.30, the result would be κ=.24/.30−1=−.20, invalid. The issue is not failure of an optimization routine; the requested spread exceeds the support-implied bound. A variance equal to .24 would require an endpoint-supported Bernoulli-type variable with that mean, not a proper continuous Beta.

If v were exactly zero, Q would be fixed at μ and would not have an ordinary Beta density. Very large concentration can approximate a tightly concentrated variable, but an exact point mass is a different probability law. Likewise μ=0 or μ=1 cannot be obtained with strictly positive finite Shapes. This separates limiting intuition from the actual parameter domain and prevents using illegal Shapes to mimic endpoint atoms.

19Condition by restricting and renormalizing the population

Suppose we now select only student-weeks with practice fraction above .25. Among that restricted group, what fraction exceed .60? The event Q>.60 is contained in Q>.25, so conditional probability divides its original probability by the probability of entering the restricted group. It does not ask for the unconditioned fraction of all student-weeks.

P(Q>.60 | Q>.25) = S(.60)/S(.25).= .1792/.73828125 ≈ .2427259259.

The numerator is 17.92% of the full modeled population; the denominator is 73.828125% of that population. Their ratio is about 24.27% of the selected group. The larger conditional number does not mean practice shares changed when we applied the filter. The reference population changed.

A density view makes the normalization explicit. Within .25<q<1, the conditional density is f(q)/S(.25); it is zero below .25. Its integral over the restricted support is 1. Integrating that conditional density from .60 to 1 gives the same ratio. The original density's mass below .25 is removed, and the remaining area is rescaled to one.

This conditional distribution is generally not an ordinary Beta on the original unit interval: it is a truncated Beta with support(.25,1). Even after rescaling that smaller interval to[0,1], its density need not become a standard Beta kernel with simply adjusted Shapes. Conditioning is an operation on events, not a rule to add arbitrary numbers to parameters. Formal Bayesian updating later also requires a sampling model; it is not the same operation as this population filter.

20Invert the CDF to obtain a quantile

A quantile answers the reverse of a cumulative-probability question. Given a threshold c, F(c) returns the fraction below it. Given a target u between zero and one, qᵤ is the threshold satisfying F(qᵤ)=u. The Beta density is positive throughout the open support, so the CDF is continuous and strictly increasing there. Each interior target therefore has a unique solution.

For the main case, solve 6 q²−8 q³+3 q⁴=u. Although this is a polynomial, a numerical bracket is more transparent than a lengthy general quartic formula. At u=.50, F(.30)=.3483 and F(.40)=.5248, so the median lies between .30 and .40. It is approximately .3857275681, distinct from the mean .40 and mode 1/3.

22Change the reporting scale without changing probabilities

Let W=100 Q be the practice share expressed as a percentage. The event W≤w is equivalent to Q≤w/100. Thus F_W(w)=F_Q(w/100), and differentiation supplies f_W(w)=f_Q(w/100)/100. The divisor 100 compensates for stretching the horizontal axis; omitting it multiplies the total density area by 100.

For the main case, mean 40 and SD 20 are now on the 0–100 percentage scale, and variance is 400. The density height at 40 is 1.728/100=.01728 per percentage point, while the probability of lying between 25 and 75 remains .6875. A different vertical height does not mean the population changed. The area and event were simply expressed in another coordinate system.

More generally, if Z=L+(H−L) Q with fixed H>L, then F_Z(z)=F_Q((z−L)/(H−L)) inside the interval and f_Z(z)=f_Q((z−L)/(H−L))/(H−L). Its mean is L+(H−L)μ, and variance is(H−L)² Var(Q). This allows a Beta-shaped model on a physical interval while retaining both dimensionless Shapes.

For example, a constructed bounded duration Z=20+40 Q spans 20 to 60 minutes, with mean 36 minutes and variance 64 minutes². P(30<Z<50) equals P(.25<Q<.75)=.6875. An interval chosen from the smallest and largest observations is not automatically a genuine physical support, however. The endpoints here are declared model bounds. Fitting or estimating unknown bounds is a separate statistical problem, not a consequence of this change-of-variable identity.

23An observed finite proportion is still discrete

Return to the separate quiz experiment with five independent binary questions and fixed success probability p=.40. Let Y count correct answers and R=Y/5. Its support is exactly{0, .2, .4, .6, .8,1}. For an integer y from 0 to 5, P(R=y/5)=P(Y=y)=C(5, y) py(1−p)5−y. The coefficient C(5, y) counts the response sequences containing y successes.

The point at R=0 has positive mass .60⁵=.07776, and the point at R=1 has mass .40⁵=.01024. A continuous Beta puts zero mass at each exact point, even if its density diverges nearby. It therefore cannot be the exact law of this five-question observed fraction. Drawing a smooth curve through the six masses does not remove their discrete nature.

Conditional on the fixed p, E[R]=p=.40 and Var(R)=p(1−p)/5=.048. The mean is the same as the main Beta model, but both the support and the variance differ. With more questions the grid becomes finer and the sampling variance changes. A continuous approximation can sometimes help, but its accuracy and purpose must be checked rather than assumed from the 0–1 range.

The graph's vertical axis is probability mass, unlike the density axes used earlier. Each stem represents the probability of one specific observed fraction, and the six masses sum to one. In a real assessment, questions may have different difficulties or dependent outcomes, violating the simple Binomial assumptions. Then even this discrete formula needs revision; replacing it with a Beta curve does not solve those design problems.

24Model genuine zeros and ones explicitly

Exact zeros and ones can arise for different reasons. A student might deliberately spend no study time practicing, creating a structural zero in the continuous-share application. A quiz can yield no correct answers by ordinary finite sampling, producing a zero through the discrete mechanism just discussed. A measuring device can round a very small positive value to zero. These mechanisms should not be combined without explanation.

For a population containing genuine endpoint groups, one possible model assigns mass π₀ at zero, mass π₁ at one, and a continuous Beta component with remaining weight 1−π₀−π₁. Require nonnegative weights with π₀+π₁<1. The continuous density inside the interval is multiplied by that remaining weight; it no longer integrates to one by itself, because some probability resides in the endpoint atoms.

Take a constructed example with π₀=.10, π₁=.05, and continuous weight .85 on Beta(2,3). The mean is .10·0+.05·1+.85·.40=.39. The probability of exceeding .60 is .05+.85·.1792=.20232: the atom at one is included, while the atom at zero is not. The model answers a different population question from the ordinary Beta, even though its interior shape is related.

Adding tiny constants to observed zeros and ones might make a software function accept the data, but it does not justify a model or preserve endpoint probabilities. First identify why those values occur and what the scientific question requires. The mixture here is an explanatory construction, not a fitted recommendation or an empirical claim about students. Formal estimation of mixtures is beyond this guide.

25Assign a distribution to uncertainty only when that is intended

Now change the experiment explicitly. Suppose p denotes one common probability of a correct response for a specified student and a defined pool of comparable questions. We do not know p. An analyst may stipulate P∼Beta(2,3) to represent current uncertainty about that common probability before new responses are observed. This is a declared Prior model, not a conclusion extracted from the study-time distribution Q.

Under this interpretation, P(P>.60)=.1792 means the assigned uncertainty distribution places 17.92% probability on parameter values above .60. It does not mean 17.92% of students exceed a practice share, nor 17.92% of future questions will be correct. Those statements concern different random quantities. The same integral can be numerically correct and conceptually misreported.

The mean .40 is now the mean of the parameter-uncertainty distribution. A central 90% interval from .097611 to .751395 is a credible interval under that specified information model, rather than a frequentist confidence interval calculated from repeated samples. We have not supplied observations or a likelihood for updating; no Posterior has been computed in this guide.

A prior must be discussed in relation to its information, target, and sensitivity. Calling Beta(1,1)“uniform” describes a density in the pcoordinate; it does not prove universal absence of information under every transformation. Calling α and β“successes and failures” without a convention can also mislead. Here Shapes are parameters of a distribution. Their update under a Binomial likelihood, and any associated count interpretation, is developed systematically in Guide 57.

26Prepare a predictive bridge without doing the later update

Keep the explicitly assigned uncertainty P∼Beta(2,3). Conditional on a particular p, suppose future quiz responses A and B are independent Bernoulli outcomes with success probability p. For one response, average its conditional success probability over the uncertainty distribution: ∫01 p f_P(p) dp=E[P]=.40. This is a predictive probability under the stated model, distinct from the probability that the parameter exceeds a threshold.

For two responses, conditional independence gives probability p² of two successes at a fixed p. Averaging now yields E[P²]=.20, not(E[P])²=.16. The multiplication and the averaging occur in a different order. Both responses share the same uncertain parameter, so averaging over that shared uncertainty generally induces dependence between their predictions.

The probability that A succeeds is .40 and that B succeeds is .40. If their marginal predictions were independent, the joint would be .16; the computed joint .20 shows they are not. This does not establish a causal effect of one response on the other. It is a consequence of the shared uncertainty in the specified model and the conditional-independence assumption.

If instead each response drew its own independent parameter P₁ and P₂ from Beta(2,3), the joint would factor as E[P₁] E[P₂]=.16. That is a different generative model, not an alternative arithmetic shortcut for the same experiment. The distinction prepares the Beta–Binomial setting: a count over repeated trials sharing one uncertain p. Its full count distribution and Bayesian update belong to the later guide, after likelihood and inference are developed.

The bridge uses only the moments already derived and a clearly stated averaging rule. It does not turn the main study-time Q into a quiz-success probability, and it does not claim the assumed uncertainty was learned from observed responses. These boundaries keep a useful advanced connection from silently changing the scope of the present guide.

27Reproduce the elementary model in Python

The following optional script implements the specific Beta(2,3) law developed by hand. It uses only Python's standard library and includes its own parameter-free PDF, CDF, survival, and quantile functions. It does not claim to evaluate arbitrary α and β. The factored survival expression is used for upper tails, while the polynomial CDF supplies an independent complement check at moderate arguments.

Before running, predict that the interval probability is larger than .50, the conditional tail is larger than the unconditioned tail .1792, and the median lies below the mean .40. These predictions follow from the reasoning already shown, not from reading the output first. The assertions check normalization endpoints, complements, event calculations, and inverse substitution.

from math import isfinite, sqrt

def check_x(x):
    if not isfinite(x):
        raise ValueError("x must be finite")

def beta23_pdf(x):
    check_x(x)
    return 12.0 * x * (1.0 - x)**2 if 0.0 < x < 1.0 else 0.0

def beta23_cdf(x):
    check_x(x)
    if x <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    if x >= 1.0:
        return 1.0
    return 6.0*x*x - 8.0*x**3 + 3.0*x**4

def beta23_survival(x):
    check_x(x)
    if x <= 0.0:
        return 1.0
    if x >= 1.0:
        return 0.0
    return (1.0 - x)**3 * (1.0 + 3.0*x)

def beta23_quantile(probability):
    if not isfinite(probability) or not 0.0 < probability < 1.0:
        raise ValueError("probability must be strictly between zero and one")
    lower, upper = 0.0, 1.0
    for _ in range(80):
        middle = (lower + upper) / 2.0
        if beta23_cdf(middle) < probability:
            lower = middle
        else:
            upper = middle
    return (lower + upper) / 2.0

interval = beta23_cdf(0.75) - beta23_cdf(0.25)
tail = beta23_survival(0.60)
conditional = tail / beta23_survival(0.25)
median = beta23_quantile(0.50)
low90, high90 = beta23_quantile(0.05), beta23_quantile(0.95)
mean = 2.0 / 5.0
second_moment = 2.0 * 3.0 / (5.0 * 6.0)
variance = second_moment - mean**2
assert beta23_cdf(0.0) == 0.0 and beta23_cdf(1.0) == 1.0
assert abs(beta23_cdf(0.60) + tail - 1.0) < 1e-14
assert abs(interval - 0.6875) < 1e-14
assert conditional > tail
assert median < mean
assert abs(beta23_cdf(median) - 0.50) < 1e-13
assert abs(beta23_cdf(high90) - beta23_cdf(low90) - 0.90) < 1e-13
print(f"Interval probability: {interval:.10f}")
print(f"Upper tail above 0.60: {tail:.10f}")
print(f"Conditional tail given Q > 0.25: {conditional:.10f}")
print(f"Mean, variance, SD: {mean:.4f}, {variance:.4f}, {sqrt(variance):.4f}")
print(f"Median: {median:.10f}")
print(f"Central 90% interval: [{low90:.10f}, {high90:.10f}]")

28Exercises with complete solutions and transfer

Try each problem before opening its solution. State the random variable and the interpretation of probability, then choose the mathematical operation. The first problems reinforce normalization and area; the later problems change support, concentration, sampling mechanism, or information. Their purpose is to test whether you can adapt the reasoning rather than copy the central polynomial mechanically.

A. Normalize before interpreting

Find C so that f(q)=Cq(1−q) for 0<q<1 is a density. Identify its Shapes and find P(Q<.50).

Complete worked solution

Integrate the kernel: ∫01(q−q²) dq=1/2−1/3=1/6. Therefore C=6, and the exponents identify α−1=1 and β−1=1, so Q∼Beta(2,2). Dividing by six instead would not normalize; the required operation divides by the kernel area 1/6.

The CDF inside the support is F(c)=3 c²−2 c³. At c=.50, it gives .75−.25=.50. Symmetry around .50 provides an independent check of the half-probability result. The density there is 6·.5·.5=1.5, which is allowed and is not the point probability. The point event Q=.50 still has probability zero under the continuous law.

B. Compute a tail and conditional interval

For the central Beta(2,3), calculate P(Q>.75) and P(.25<Q<.75 given Q>.25). Interpret the denominator.

Complete worked solution

The factored tail gives S(.75)=(.25)³(1+2.25)=.015625·3.25=.05078125. This also equals 1−F(.75). The interval probability already obtained by integration is .6875.

Conditioning on Q>.25 retains probability S(.25)=.73828125 of the original population. Thus the conditional interval probability is .6875/.73828125≈.9312169312. The denominator is the probability of being selected, not the interval length. As a check, the conditional upper tail is .05078125/.73828125≈.0687830688; the two conditional probabilities sum to one. A larger interval probability after filtering reflects removal of the lower group, not a change in individual fractions.

C. Verify moments without a formula sheet

For density 2 qon 0<q<1, derive the mean, second moment, and variance directly. Compare with the general Beta formulas.

Complete worked solution

First verify ∫012 qdq=1. The mean is ∫01 q·2 qdq=2/3. The second moment is ∫01 q²·2 qdq=1/2. Therefore variance is 1/2−(2/3)²=1/2−4/9=1/18≈.0555555556. It is positive and below μ(1−μ)=2/9.

The density corresponds to Beta(2,1). Its general mean is 2/(2+1)=2/3, second moment 2·3/(3·4)=1/2, and variance 2·1/(3²·4)=1/18. The agreement checks both the arithmetic and the Shape reading. The mean is greater than .50, consistent with the increasing density. A concentration of density toward one does not assign positive probability to the exact value one.

D. Keep the mean and change dispersion

A proposed Beta has mean .40 and SD .10. Recover its Shapes. Then explain why a proposed variance .30 at the same mean is invalid.

Complete worked solution

SD .10 means variance v=.01, not .10. Compute κ=.40·.60/.01−1=24−1=23. Hence α=.40·23=9.2 and β=.60·23=13.8. Both are positive; they need not be integers. Substitution checks mean 9.2/23=.40 and variance .24/(23+1)=.01.

For v=.30, κ=.24/.30−1=−.20, which would produce negative Shapes. More fundamentally, any variable in[0,1] with mean .40 has variance at most .24; a proper continuous Beta has strictly less. The proposal cannot be fixed by changing numerical software. These calculations specify a candidate family member from population summaries; without observations, they are not an empirical parameter estimate.

E. Diagnose the misleading mode formula

Someone applies(α−1)/(α+β−2) to Beta(.5, .5) and reports a unique mode .50. Explain the error and calculate the probability within .10 of either endpoint.

Complete worked solution

The interior maximum formula requires both Shapes above one. Here both are below one, and the density 1/[π√(q(1−q))] is U-shaped. At .50 the logarithmic derivative vanishes, but the stationary point is a minimum. The density diverges toward zero and one without creating atoms there.

The two endpoint neighborhoods are disjoint, so their probabilities add. Using F(c)=(2/π) arcsin√c and symmetry, P(Q<.10 or Q>.90)=2 F(.10)=(4/π) arcsin√.10≈.4096655294. This is about 40.97% in the nearby intervals, not probability 40.97% of being exactly zero or one. The latter probability is zero for the continuous law. The calculation checks the meaning of an endpoint singularity as well as the conditions of a mode formula.

F. Transform a bounded measurement

Let Z=20+40 Q minutes with Q∼Beta(2,3). Find its mean, variance, density at 36 minutes, and P(30<Z<50).

Complete worked solution

The support becomes 20<Z<60. The mean is 20+40·.40=36 minutes, and variance is 40²·.04=64 minutes². At z=36, the corresponding fraction is(36−20)/40=.40. The density is f_Z(36)=f_Q(.40)/40=1.728/40=.0432 per minute. Omitting the divisor would violate normalization.

Transform both event bounds:30<20+40 Q<50 is equivalent to .25<Q<.75. Its probability is therefore .6875. An independent integral of f_Q((z−20)/40)/40 from 30 to 50 produces the same area by substitution. The affine transformation changes units and density heights, but not the probability of an equivalent event. Bounds 20 and 60 are declared model endpoints, not automatically inferred from observed minima and maxima.

G. Choose the probability statement that matches the object

Compare Q∼Beta(2,3) as a population study-time share with P∼Beta(2,3) as declared uncertainty about one quiz-success probability. Interpret the same upper-tail .1792 and central 90% interval in each case.

Complete worked solution

For Q, the tail is the model's fraction of student-weeks whose practice share exceeds .60. Its central 90% interval [.097611, .751395] describes individual fraction values covering 90% of that modeled population. It does not quantify uncertainty about a fixed population mean or about one student's quiz ability.

For P, the tail is the assigned probability that the common unknown success parameter exceeds .60. The same numerical interval carries 90% of the specified parameter-uncertainty distribution and is an equal-tailed credible interval under that model. No observations or likelihood have been supplied, so it is not a computed Posterior and not a frequentist confidence interval. Equal density formulas preserve numerical integrals, but they do not make the experiments or conclusions interchangeable.

H. Shared uncertainty changes a joint prediction

With P∼Beta(2,3), assume two future binary responses are conditionally independent given the same P. Find the probability of two successes. Compare with fixed p=.40 and with two independently drawn uncertain parameters.

Complete worked solution

At a fixed p, conditional independence gives joint success probability p². Average over the shared uncertainty: E[P²]=2·3/(5·6)=.20. Each marginal success probability is E[P]=.40, but their product .16 does not equal the joint .20, so the marginal predictions are dependent.

With a truly fixed p=.40 and independent responses, the joint is .40²=.16. With independent parameters P₁ and P₂ each having Beta(2,3), averaging the product gives E[P₁] E[P₂]=.16. These are different experiments. The contrast shows why knowing only the mean of an uncertain shared parameter is insufficient for joint prediction. No outcome has been observed here; this is a predictive averaging calculation, not an update or a causal claim that one answer changes the other.

I. Respect structural endpoint groups

A constructed population has 10% at zero practice,5% at all-practice, and 85% following Beta(2,3) inside. Find its mean and P(Q>.60). Explain why a singular ordinary Beta is not the same model.

Complete worked solution

Weight the conditional means by group probability: E[Q]=.10·0+.05·1+.85·.40=.39. For the event Q>.60, the zero group contributes nothing, the one group contributes its full .05 mass, and the continuous group contributes .85·.1792=.15232. The total is .20232.

An ordinary Beta with either Shape below one can have an infinite density near an endpoint, but its exact endpoint probabilities remain zero. It cannot reproduce the stipulated 10% and 5% atoms. The mixed law combines discrete masses with a continuous density of total area .85; trying to normalize that interior part to one while retaining both atoms would make the total probability 1.15. The distinction is therefore a probability-accounting requirement, not merely a preference for a particular plot style.

J. Audit an entire interpretation rather than one number

A report sees two correct answers out of five and states: “The student’s probability is Beta(2,3), its mean is .40, its variance is the quiz sampling variance, and the central 90% interval is a confidence interval. A high endpoint density allows exact 0 and 1.” Diagnose each step and state what can actually be calculated from the given information.

Complete worked solution

The observation establishes Y=2 and observed fraction R=2/5=.40. It does not, by itself, assign a distribution to the unknown p. A Bayesian uncertainty statement requires a declared Prior and a sampling model; a frequentist analysis requires its own inference procedure and assumptions. The numbers 2 and 3 cannot simply be relabeled as Beta Shapes because they count successes and failures. No posterior calculation is justified by that relabeling alone.

If p were known to be .40 and five responses were independent with that common probability, the sampling variance of R would be p(1−p)/5=.048. A separately stipulated Beta(2,3) has variance .04. The matching mean does not identify the laws, and the supplied observation does not make p known. One could report .048 as a plug-in calculation using .40, provided it is labeled an estimate under the Binomial assumptions rather than the known true sampling variance.

A central interval from a population Beta describes individual values; from an explicitly assigned parameter Beta it is a credible interval under that information model. Neither becomes a frequentist confidence interval merely because it contains 90% probability. Finally, a continuous density has zero point mass at exact 0 and 1 even if its height is unbounded nearby. In fact Beta(2,3) itself has zero limiting height at both ends.

A defensible report therefore starts with the observed 2/5, states the experiment and assumptions, and separates any proposed population or uncertainty model from the observation. It can calculate descriptive fractions immediately, but needs additional modeling choices to make inferential or predictive claims. This diagnosis is the transferable skill: identify the object and information before accepting an attractive density curve or a numerically familiar mean.

29A reusable workflow for bounded-quantity models

Begin with the object and experiment. Decide whether you have a continuous fraction across units, a discrete observed ratio, a fixed parameter, or an uncertainty distribution. Define the denominator, sampling unit, and treatment of exact endpoints. This step determines whether a Beta density is even a candidate and what its probabilities would mean.

Next specify positive Shapes and verify the density. For an elementary kernel, integrate it before normalizing. For the general family, identify B(α, β) and the endpoint conditions. Use mean and concentration together; a common mean does not imply a common tail, and a high density does not imply positive point mass. If using an interior-mode formula, check both Shapes exceed one.

Choose the function that answers the question. Use a CDF for a lower cumulative event, a survival function for an upper event, a difference for an interval, a ratio for a conditional event, and an inverse for a quantile. For the main Beta(2,3) example, mean .40, mode 1/3, median≈.385728, interval probability .6875, and upper tail .1792 are different summaries of the same law. None can be substituted for another simply because each is a number between zero and one.

Then check by another route: polynomial area versus incomplete Beta, mean by direct integration versus a shifted-normalizer ratio, variance by a centered integral, or quantile by CDF substitution. State the assumptions and the scope of the interpretation after the number. If the model changes to include endpoint atoms, a discrete count, shared uncertainty, or another reporting scale, update the probability calculation instead of reusing the old formula unchanged.

A useful future classroom or lab task is to compare clearly defined observed fractions with a candidate Beta while preserving exact zeros, ones, denominators, and repeated-unit identifiers. The learning target is to diagnose a mismatch, not force a good fit. Formal estimation, uncertainty assessment, and Bayesian updating are later curriculum topics. This guide supplies the distributional reasoning needed to approach them without confusing population variability with knowledge about a parameter.
